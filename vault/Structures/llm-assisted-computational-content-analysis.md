# LLM-Assisted Computational Content Analysis

## The arc of inquiry

This body of work charts the rapid diffusion of large language models into computational content analysis of political and social media text—moving from basic validation questions ("can an LLM do what a human coder or a specialized classifier did?") toward more ambitious applications (longitudinal and cross-national causal analysis, multimodal and narrative-level measurement) and, increasingly, toward critical reflection on what these pipelines get wrong, whose interests they encode, and how they should be governed methodologically. Read together, the notes trace a methodological maturation: from proof-of-concept classification tasks, to validated applied studies, to meta-level critique and agentic automation of research itself.

## Validating LLMs as classifiers and annotators

A substantial cluster establishes the basic empirical case for LLM-based coding against human and supervised baselines. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] show that small, fine-tuned open-source models (LLaMA family) can match or beat proprietary giants on domain-specific classification while improving reproducibility and cost—an argument extended practically by [[Farjam2026-zg]], whose agentic Instructor–Classifier–Judge–Human workflow replicates and scales a classic climate-news coding study across languages. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and its companion piece [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] perform a parallel validation exercise for embeddings rather than labels, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large outperforms Italian BERT variants for clustering political news, while candidly documenting the novel validation challenges that fully LLM-integrated pipelines pose (variable narrative granularity, the need for expert rather than crowd validators). [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this whole annotation-and-synthesis literature, giving the cluster a bird's-eye taxonomy. Several papers push into harder or more specialized classification problems: [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to detect collective-action frames in extremist Telegram discourse and links them causally to offline violence; [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text, raising the stakes of annotation work into surveillance and micro-targeting; and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] demonstrates that simply asking-and-averaging LLM judgments can scale political texts onto ideological dimensions competitively with established scaling methods. Together these establish that LLMs are not just adequate substitutes for earlier NLP pipelines but often superior ones—while leaving open exactly why, and at what hidden cost.

## Interrogating reliability, bias, and the limits of LLM judgment

A second thread complicates this optimism by probing where LLM judgment breaks down or diverges systematically from human meaning-making. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that LLM–human annotation disagreement on contentious tasks is driven far more by item difficulty (annotator entropy) than by demographic mismatch, a reassuring but double-edged result. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows LLMs falter badly on pragmatic phenomena—implicature and presupposition in Italian political speech—suggesting a ceiling on how much "understanding" current models bring to manipulative discourse. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] demonstrates that even reasoning- and search-augmented LLMs are poor fact-checkers absent curated retrieval context, and that their citation behavior carries an ideological skew. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] finds a more fundamental gap: self-reported dark personality traits do not reliably surface in LLM-scored textual toxicity, cautioning against assuming psychological constructs are legible in surface language. These critiques converge in [[Balluff2026-if]], an explicitly reflexive essay arguing that the field's LLM enthusiasm has outpaced attention to reproducibility, environmental cost, corporate dependency, and bias—effectively a thesis statement for the whole topic's cautionary counter-current, echoed methodologically by [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]], which reframes LLMs not as classifiers to be benchmarked but as "analytic collaborators" whose errors and justifications productively destabilize and refine human codebooks.

## Scaling measurement: narrative, framing, and multimodal content

Beyond classification, several papers use LLMs to construct genuinely new measurement constructs that were previously intractable at scale. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Elfes2026-jb]] both operationalize *narrative*—respectively, claim-level diffusion across languages and actantial role-assignment in polarized discourse—arguing that narrative similarity/polarization is a distinct estimand from lexical or topical overlap, and that LLMs finally make it measurable. [[Altoe2026-wu]] extends this narrative-as-unit-of-analysis logic into a streaming, pre-peak virality-prediction pipeline for misinformation triage, explicitly building check-worthiness detection around journalistic decision criteria. Framing analysis undergoes a similar expansion: [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] move framing detection beyond fixed categories and single modalities, while [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] (again) shows how LLM dialogue can surface latent or historically shifting frame boundaries that static codebooks miss. The visual and multimodal turn is represented by [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] (multimodal LLMs outperforming specialized computer-vision tools for political image analysis), [[Arminio2025-tw]] (VLLM-generated captions enabling connotative rather than merely denotative image clustering), and [[Gardam2025-er]] (a visual-first methodology for Instagram conspiracy discourse)—together arguing that political communication is irreducibly multimodal and that LLMs are the first tools equal to that complexity. Underpinning much of this embedding-based work, [[Fan2025-ut]] offers a general-purpose fix—linear concept erasure—for a confound that silently biases nearly all embedding-based clustering and retrieval pipelines used elsewhere in this topic (e.g., [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Arminio2025-tw]]), showing the topic's methods literature also produces portable infrastructural contributions.

## Applied studies: elections, extremism, and persuasion

A cluster of applied studies puts validated pipelines to substantive use. [[Larsson2026-ro]] combines GPT-4 zero-shot classification with human validation to trace a decade of negative-campaigning sentiment and engagement on Facebook; [[Iris2026-pg]] uses LLM-based entity extraction to document cross-national radical-right media overrepresentation ahead of the 2024 European elections; and [[Bailard2024-pj]], already noted, links online framing to offline violence. [[Triedman2025-uy]] turns the lens on AI-generated content itself, offering the first large-scale comparison of Grokipedia against Wikipedia and finding derivative but ideologically slanted, unreliably sourced rewriting—an unsettling coda showing LLM-generated text is now also an *object* of the same computational content-analysis toolkit used to study human discourse. On the persuasion side, [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] show that personalized LLM dialogue durably shifts conspiracy beliefs and policy attitudes across contested domains, particularly among skeptics—extending the topic from passive content classification to interactive, agentic intervention—while [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalizes a related idea defensively, using chatbot-delivered "conversational inoculation" to build resistance to misinformation before exposure. [[Minici2024-tf]] rounds out the applied set by fusing LLM and graph-neural-network signals to detect coordinated information operations across campaigns, showing the generalization benefits of foundation-model approaches in adversarial settings.

## Methods for methods: survey tools, agentic review, and meta-reproduction

Finally, several papers reflect on LLMs as infrastructure for the research process itself rather than only for primary text analysis. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended survey coding into continuous latent measures, and [[Ober2026-vd]] builds a human-in-the-loop topic-modeling-plus-LLM pipeline for qualitative interview analysis, both emphasizing that interpretive depth need not be sacrificed for scale. At the far end of automation, [[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] turn LLM agents onto the scientific process itself—automating manuscript review and reproducing published computational social science findings, respectively—raising the topic's concerns about validation, bias, and sycophantic conformity to a reflexive, second-order level: can the same agentic pipelines used to analyze political text also be trusted to check, and reproduce, the scholarship that studies it? Taken as a whole, the topic's trajectory runs from "LLMs as better classifiers" through "LLMs as new measurement instruments" to "LLMs as collaborators, persuaders, and now evaluators of research itself," with [[Balluff2026-if]] standing as the field's conscience, insistently asking whether each new capability is worth its trade-offs.
