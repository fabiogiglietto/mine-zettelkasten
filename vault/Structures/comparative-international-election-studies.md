# Comparative International Election Studies

## The arc of inquiry

This body of work extends a coordinated-behavior methodology first honed on Italian politics into a genuinely comparative register, testing whether the signatures of orchestrated online activity—synchronized posting, network modularity, astroturfed authenticity—travel across electoral systems, media regimes, and levels of democratic consolidation. The throughline is methodological transportability: can tools built for one national context (bot/coordination detection, URL-sharing audits, multimodal content analysis) reveal comparable or divergent dynamics in Germany, Brazil, Serbia, Russia, Korea, the UK, and the EU as a transnational arena? The answer emerging across these papers is that coordination and its effects are not uniform but are shaped by regime type, platform architecture, and party system—even as the underlying detection logic remains portable.

## Coordination as a traveling method

The foundational move is [[Nizzoli2020-cf]]'s reframing of coordination as a continuous, community-specific property rather than a binary threshold applied to the 2019 UK election—a methodological pivot that several papers filed here implicitly adopt. [[Keller2019-nk]] supplies the canonical ground-truth case (South Korea's NIS Twitter campaign), showing that coordination traces, not bot-like individual behavior, are the more durable signal of astroturfing, and that even well-resourced state campaigns may have limited discursive impact—a sobering counterpoint to assumptions of automatic effectiveness. This logic resurfaces in authoritarian and hybrid settings: [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework to "connective counteraction" in Russia's pro-Navalny/pro-government Twitter standoff, finding centralized activist networks pitted against modular, team-based pro-regime astroturfing; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] detects analogous forwarding-based coordination among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] applies coordination detection to multilingual Twitter activity around China's 2022 COVID protests, documenting dual-use infrastructure for both mobilization and repression; and [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues that Serbia's SNS has evolved a more mimetic, harder-to-detect form of coordinated inauthenticity than classic bot farms, calling for new analytical frameworks. [[Kim2026-br]] pushes coordination detection into a two-decade, 112-million-comment corpus of Korean news comments, showing that suspected state-linked trolls rely on morally condemning rhetoric rather than overt boosterism—broadening what "coordinated" content looks like. Together these papers suggest coordination analysis is maturing from a Twitter-centric, binary classification exercise into a flexible toolkit applied across platforms (Telegram, WhatsApp, news comments) and regime types.

Brazil is where this toolkit meets its most intensive stress test. [[Kansaon2025-id]] directly adapts "Rapid Spread Network" coordination detection (explicitly drawing on prior Twitter work) to 13 million WhatsApp messages, tracing coordinated accounts from misinformation dissemination to real-world mobilization culminating in the January 8 attacks—demonstrating that coordination analysis can be pushed into encrypted, closed messaging ecosystems where platform cooperation is minimal. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a deactivation experiment isolating WhatsApp's causal role in shaping beliefs, while [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] shows that even crowdsourced, volunteer-driven auditing (a browser plugin) can surface irregular advertising on Facebook absent platform transparency—an alternative, bottom-up route to the same evidentiary goal.

## Brazil as a post-Bolsonaro battleground

Beyond coordination per se, Brazil anchors a dense cluster of studies on the downstream consequences of polarized digital ecosystems after the 2022 election and January 2023 coup attempt. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a paired panel-survey investigation: the former shows electoral misinformation belief erodes political tolerance (with messaging-app use working indirectly through misinformation beliefs), while the latter shows the same misinformation beliefs erode trust in election results—with mainstream news consumption acting as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters, and WhatsApp network composition (strong-tie vs. stranger discussion) cutting in opposite directions. [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates the echo-chamber assumption by tracking a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network's affective engagement longitudinally, finding instability rather than fixed polarization around the inauguration and coup attempt, including evidence of deliberate cross-cutting "brigading." [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] turn to elite behavior: the former documents how mediatization creates perverse incentives pushing parliamentarians away from substantive legislative communication toward antagonistic virality-chasing ("lacração"), while the latter shows how, during the Manaus COVID crisis, government and opposition parliamentarians pursued distinct epistemic-authority strategies (direct claims vs. derivative reporting vs. local brokerage) to contest journalistic gatekeeping. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] studies a different kind of platform shock—Brazil's 2024 X ban—showing that politicized bans produce durable partisan "sorting ratchets" rather than symmetric demobilization. Across this cluster, Brazil functions less as a single case than as a laboratory for nearly every mechanism (misinformation, affect, elite strategy, platform governance) found piecemeal elsewhere.

## Germany and the mechanics of campaign visibility

The German federal election context—2021 and 2025—provides the most direct extension of the Italian coordinated-behavior and ad-monitoring agenda into a comparable Western European system. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] explicitly investigates political advertising and coordinated behavior ahead of the 2021 election, while [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] turns to visual personalization on Instagram, validating GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision tools and finding distinct concentrated-visibility strategies between candidate and party accounts. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Kakavand2026-kt]] both argue for multi-platform infrastructure thinking: the former treats the 2025 election as a case study in the infrastructural bottlenecks of cross-platform (X/TikTok) observability, finding weak cross-modal correlation in TikTok's "politainment" style; the latter shows that far-right network structure in Germany varies dramatically by platform affordance (Twitter as scalable broadcast, Facebook as hierarchically coordinated AfD apparatus, Instagram as fragmented echo chambers), arguing affordances are relational rather than fixed. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] closes the loop with a sock-puppet audit showing non-partisan TikTok users are incidentally overexposed to pro-AfD content, driven by supply-side volume and virality rather than overt algorithmic favoritism. Read together, these German studies trace a shift in the field's center of gravity from text-based coordination detection toward multimodal, platform-comparative, and audience-exposure designs.

## The 2024 European Parliament elections as a transnational testbed

A cluster of papers converges on the 2024 EP election as a natural comparative experiment across 27 member states. [[Darius2026-xl]] finds populist radical-right (and, more weakly, far-left) parties enjoy disproportionate audience support rather than superior activity, with TikTok the most asymmetric arena—an "equalizer" effect tied to emotional, anti-elitist, Eurosceptic communicative style. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a parallel asymmetry in news coverage itself: Radical Right actors receive structurally disproportionate media visibility relative to polling and electoral performance across five countries, intensifying before election day. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] add qualitative texture on far-right platform strategy—AI-generated multimedia on Instagram/X, and the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored securitizing visuals (TikTok memes using non-domestic footage to manufacture crisis). [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links this electoral performance directly to mass perception, asking whether far-right success shapes voters' disinformation concerns. [[Oprea2025-lf]] narrows to Romania, finding hyperactive, inauthentic-looking accounts inflating shares across all major parties despite platform policies prohibiting this. Collectively, this cluster suggests the EP elections function as a continental stress test of the "radical right gets rewarded" hypothesis seen at the national level in Germany and Brazil, now documented at the level of media systems and platform algorithms simultaneously.

## Comparative frameworks and the cross-national party-level turn

Several papers step back from single elections to build explicitly comparative theory. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] is foundational here, proposing macro-structural resilience clusters (high-resilience Northern Europe, polarized Southern Europe, an outlier US) against which later single-country findings can be read—explaining, for instance, why Italy and Germany diverge in [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]'s cross-national Facebook URL-quality study, which finds untrustworthy-URL shares rising in German and Italian election years while declining in France. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] scales this further to 26 countries and 32 million parliamentary tweets, showing misinformation sharing is driven specifically by the interaction of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone—a party-strategic rather than platform-pathological account that resonates with the German AfD and Brazilian findings above. [[Zhu2026-tn]] offers a structural-network counterpart, showing alternative-mainstream media audience overlap depends on populist parties' institutional access rather than being a fixed property of "alternative" media. [[Heft2021-ky]] extends the comparative lens to transnational right-wing hyperlink networks across six countries, finding the US functions as a structural hub while paradoxically legacy media remain the dominant shared reference point—undercutting the insulated-echo-chamber narrative. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] rounds out the comparative turn with Scandinavia, showing populist parties exploit "Angry" reactions for virality even in ostensibly low-polarization, high-trust democracies—suggesting the dynamics documented elsewhere are not confined to already-polarized systems.

## Authoritarian adaptation, AI, and the research-infrastructure problem

A final set of papers extends the agenda toward two frontiers: authoritarian/Global South contexts beyond Europe and Brazil, and the research-infrastructure conditions that make any of this work possible. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage rather than disinformation per se; [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Makeev2026-ma]] examine generative-AI propaganda and dual-audience wartime messaging across democratically weakened states and Russia; [[Wack2026-bt]] shows experimentally in Kenya that deepfake impact is "socially negotiated" through surrounding comments rather than fixed at production, echoing [[Prochaska2025-ef]]'s US-focused argument that election misinformation lives in collective "deep stories" rather than discrete posts. [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] interrogate platform-side interventions—Meta's own data on deceptive networks' concentrated, reshare-driven reach, and X's Community Notes system structurally undermoderating polarizing (often electoral) content by design. Undergirding all of this, [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] diagnose the post-API, DSA-regulated data environment itself as the binding constraint on comparative election research, with the latter's "price, proficiency, permission" framework explaining why so much of this literature remains WEIRD-centric despite its comparative ambitions—a methodological self-awareness that [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal US-Germany stakeholder study on AI discourse also models, showing how early institutional versus individualized discourse patterns diverge by country and persist into later regulatory outcomes.
