# Generative AI and Media Manipulation

## The Persuasion Machine: Mechanics, Limits, and Levers

A tight, technically sophisticated cluster of experiments has emerged asking not *whether* conversational AI persuades, but *how* and *how much*. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes the baseline mechanism across nearly 20 LLMs and 707 issues: persuasiveness is driven chiefly by information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a model can pack into a conversation — and post-training/prompting interventions move the needle far more than scale or personalization. Crucially, the same levers that maximize persuasion degrade factual accuracy, establishing a persuasion–accuracy trade-off that recurs throughout this literature. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, pitting frontier AI against elite human persuaders (world-champion debaters, professional canvassers) and finding that no human — even after targeted coaching — reliably matches AI, with the advantage collapsing only when AI is artificially constrained to human throughput. This confirms information throughput, not rapport or "humanness," as the operative mechanism, and extends effects from attitude change to real charitable behavior. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this pattern across four live elections, showing brief AI dialogues outperform traditional political ads via central-route, evidence-based argument rather than manipulation — while surfacing a troubling partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy that echoes concerns raised elsewhere about training-data politics.

This persuasive capacity is not intrinsically malign: [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] redirect the same mechanism toward prosocial ends, showing that personalized, evidence-dense AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief and shifts policy attitudes on climate, vaccination, and inequality — with effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science/institutions. Together with [[Hackenburg2025-dj]], these papers reframe AI persuasion as fundamentally an information-throughput phenomenon that can debunk as readily as it can deceive. The regulatory question this raises is addressed directly by [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]], which finds that disclosing mere *AI identity* (per EU AI Act Article 50) does almost nothing to blunt persuasion, whereas disclosing *persuasive intent* cuts effects roughly in half — suggesting governance frameworks modeled on political-advertising transparency (purpose, sponsor, targeting logic) are far more promising than authenticity labeling alone. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complements this from the audience side, showing that task-specific AI disclosure in journalism produces heterogeneous, not uniform, trust penalties — implying that "transparency" is not a single lever but a design space.

## Coordinated Campaigns and Synthetic Information Operations

A second cluster documents how generative AI is operationally embedded into coordinated promotional and propaganda ecosystems, often blurring the line between human and automated participation. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] traces the exponential post-ChatGPT growth of AI-generated visual persuasion in coordinated Facebook gambling networks, showing that generative AI does not invent new persuasion drivers so much as intensify and recombine existing ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO) while exploiting Meta's asymmetric governance of paid versus organic content. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes this into the broader construct of "Deceptive Information Operations," arguing — using the same gambling network alongside a pro-Putin propaganda network and a poorly-moderated adult-content network — that deception, affordance exploitation, and coordination (not falsity per se) are the structural invariants across otherwise disparate campaigns, producing a "Zombie Internet" where human and synthetic activity become indistinguishable. [[Orlando2025-ul]] provides a generative mechanism for this convergence at the agent level: simulating LLM-driven influence operations shows that even minimal "teammate awareness" among agents is sufficient to produce network cohesion, narrative homogenization, and synchronized amplification nearly equivalent to explicit coordination — a sobering demonstration that coordinated inauthentic behavior can emerge without centralized scripting.

[[Schroeder2026-im]] scales this concern to a systemic register, arguing that agentic multi-agent LLM "swarms" constitute a qualitatively new threat to democratic epistemics via manufactured consensus, training-data poisoning ("LLM grooming"), and erosion of institutional trust, and proposing layered technical-governance responses. [[Beacken2026-zb]] pushes back against techno-determinism in this space, showing across six democratically weakened states that GenAI propaganda adoption is shaped by local political-economic structure rather than following a uniform trajectory — a useful corrective to the swarm/synthetic-reality narratives. [[Waight2026-ts]] identifies a more indirect, non-agentive pathway of institutional influence: state-coordinated media is absorbed into training corpora and "laundered" into commercial LLM outputs, producing systematically more pro-regime responses in languages tied to low-media-freedom states — linking coordinated information campaigns not to real-time operations but to the invisible substrate of pretraining data.

## Deepfakes, Fraud, and the Erosion of Shared Reality

A parallel strand examines synthetic media's effects on audiences and its exploitation for financial harm. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] maps the "scam ecosystem" of deepfake-enabled financial fraud, arguing that regulatory responsibility must shift from victims to platform/telecom/financial gatekeepers — a governance argument that resonates with the gambling and DIO papers' emphasis on infrastructural, not just individual, accountability. Experimentally, however, the picture is more contingent than alarmist narratives suggest: [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds that AI-generated and video-based visual disinformation do *not* universally outperform text, with decontextualized real footage only surpassing text when abundant plausible source material exists — a finding that tempers fears of GenAI's unique persuasive threat relative to low-tech manipulation. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates the picture further at the individual level, showing that difficulty authenticating one (deep)fake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to weaken confidence and increase susceptibility to subsequent disinformation — implying cumulative, format-specific erosion of discernment rather than one-off deception. [[Wack2026-bt]], working in Kenya, shows that a deepfake's persuasive impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding commentary — credulous comments amplify harm, skeptical comments partially restore it — reframing deepfakes as flexible cues within participatory sensemaking rather than static artifacts, with clear relevance to low-resource information ecosystems.

[[Emilio2026-ik]] synthesizes many of these threads into the broadest claim in this file: the danger of GenAI is not the isolated deepfake but "synthetic realities" — layered fabrications of content, identity, interaction, and institutions — producing a paradox whereby ubiquitous synthetic media leads societies to rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the "cost of truth" itself. This diagnosis motivates the search-engine audit by [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], which finds that Google's AI Overviews unevenly moderate conspiratorial search practices, debunking established theories like chemtrails but sometimes legitimizing newer ones (15-minute cities) by framing them as "debates" — a concrete instance of the epistemic degradation Ferrara theorizes, occurring within the very AI systems meant to arbitrate truth.

## The Epistemology and Culture of Generative Content

Several papers step back to ask what "fact," "truth," and "content" even mean once generation replaces retrieval. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that GenAI outputs fit none of fact-checking's traditional categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) and proposes "emergent facts" — plausible, context-dependent, opaque outputs requiring relational rather than binary evaluation. This theoretical move underwrites the practical difficulties documented elsewhere (Kasianenko's AIOs, Choi's carryover effects) and dovetails with [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical audit of Grokipedia, which shows an AI-generated encyclopedia that is heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically over-cites unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics — a concrete case of "emergent facts" acquiring institutional authority without institutional verification.

Culturally, [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] examine the aesthetic and labor dimensions of mass-produced synthetic content. Galip theorizes "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's concept of the gimmick — devices that work simultaneously "too little" and "too hard" — locating critical, even resistant, potential in forms usually dismissed as degenerate. Tang's ethnography of Chinese viral-video "slop" creators shows the same phenomenon from the production side: virality is no longer contingency but a managed, benchmarked, industrialized output, unsettling received notions of the "creator." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] provides the field-level frame these observations sit within, arguing that generative AI (alongside algorithmic recommendation and platform privatization) is dissolving the "social media" paradigm itself, producing "platforms without users" and AI-mediated communication as a distinct post-social formation — a thesis against which nearly every empirical paper here (gambling networks, DIOs, swarms, slop production) can be read as case evidence. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a partial counter-note: on TikTok's immigration discourse, generative AI appears only marginally, mainly as a tool for "speculative worldbuilding" in anti-immigration content, suggesting synthetic media's political salience is still issue- and platform-dependent rather than universal.

## Governance, Safety Practice, and Public Infrastructure

A final cluster turns from diagnosis to institutional response. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] jointly reframe AI red-teaming — the field's flagship safety practice — as a sociotechnical, labor-intensive activity riddled with value judgments and psychological costs, explicitly modeled on the troubled history of commercial content moderation; both call for empirical, interdisciplinary study before red-teaming's opaque, market-driven form hardens into unquestioned infrastructure. [[Goldberg2026-eb]], emerging from a large civil-society convening, takes the constructive counterpart to this critique, surveying how LLMs might rebuild "digital public squares" through collective dialogue, bridging systems, and community moderation — explicitly warning that synthetic participation and AI-mediated deliberation must augment rather than replace human judgment, a caution that threads back through nearly every paper in this collection: the tools that let AI persuade, coordinate, and fabricate at scale are the same tools that might, under different incentive structures, be turned toward repair.
