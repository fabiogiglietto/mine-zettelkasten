# Platform Governance and Content Policy

## The Vanishing Feed: From Networked Publics to Managed Visibility

The papers gathered here trace a single, cumulative story: the "newsfeed" as an object of public importance has become simultaneously more consequential and less legible. At the theoretical core, [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] argue that the very substance of "social" media has changed — from networked publics organized around explicit interpersonal ties to algorithmically clustered, parasocial arrangements built on implicit behavioral signals. [[Helmond2026-ll]] extends this arc into an AI-centered phase of "platformization," in which even the social graph itself (epitomized by the death of the Facebook Like button) becomes dispensable infrastructure. Together these pieces suggest that the object political communication scholars once called "the feed" is being hollowed out and rebuilt around inference, recommendation, and infrastructural capture rather than reciprocity — a shift [[De2026-ld]] shows platforms actively narrate through "changecraft," staging incremental, patchworked changes as continuity or creator benevolence rather than acknowledging structural realignment.

## Algorithmic Governance and Its Political Consequences

A substantial cluster of papers empirically probes what algorithmic curation actually *does* to visibility and political attitudes. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] offers causal evidence that X's "For You" feed shifts users rightward and that this effect persists asymmetrically even after the algorithm is switched off, reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies ([[Allcott2025-jb]], critiqued meta-scientifically by [[Munger2025-cz]]). [[Efstinated bias in Musk-era Twitter is corroborated by [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], which finds that apparent right-leaning amplification is largely explained by proximity to Musk and "agitating" content rather than ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lose visibility. Bouchaud2026-um pushes further, showing X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear ideological dimension in its embedding space — evidence that political profiling can emerge unintentionally from engagement optimization. [[Brown2026-br]] complicates the "radicalization pathway" narrative on YouTube, finding rabbit-holing but not algorithmic echo chambers or radicalization per se, while still detecting a platform-wide conservative tilt. Collectively these papers push against both techno-panic and platform-exculpatory narratives, insisting that algorithmic effects are real but conditional, asymmetric, and often mediated by elite actors and following behavior rather than simple exposure.

Two field experiments extend this logic toward intervention: [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] shows that blocking political elites from feeds—rather than suppressing political content wholesale—improves factual knowledge and reduces misinformation susceptibility without affecting polarization, reframing misinformation as a problem of "exposure architecture." [[Brady2026-ln]] similarly demonstrates that a "diversified extremity" algorithm can reduce amplification of moralized/emotional content and improve social-norm perception without sacrificing platform enjoyment, suggesting algorithmic redesign — not just removal — is a viable governance lever. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] frames this whole research program as a call to treat platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than a bundle of isolated causal effects.

## Meta's Political Content Reduction as a Case Study in Opacity

Several papers converge specifically on Meta's 2021–2025 political content reduction policy as an emblem of unaccountable algorithmic governance. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] documents that the policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72% and began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout — with extremist accounts compensating through increased posting volume — while Meta failed to disclose the policy in DSA transparency reports. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complements this with longitudinal evidence that Facebook actively curates visibility, dampening reach for partisan content and rewarding journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate with political crises like the 2020 "break the glass" interventions — direct evidence against any neutral-conduit self-understanding. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects the discursive machinery behind such moves, showing how Meta's Newsroom frames problematic content as originating from "outside" bad actors while eliding platform affordances and hyperpartisan verified accounts. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows the same defensive logic operating around Zuckerberg's 2025 termination of the Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, finding empirically that content removal was already rare and that fact-checkers themselves resist the "censorship" framing while expressing skepticism toward Community Notes as a replacement — skepticism borne out by [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]], which respectively find persistent partisan asymmetries in Community Notes flagging and structural undermoderation of polarizing electoral content by design. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] situates these reversals within a broader three-phase historical arc of platform election policy — protection, civic-integrity expansion, backlash — arguing recent rollbacks reflect "platform illiberalism" rather than principled free-speech recommitment.

## Deplatforming, Bans, and the Walled-Garden Fragmentation of Publics

A parallel set of papers examines the downstream political effects of exclusionary governance — bans, deplatforming, and migration. Ventura2026-um and [[Simeone2025-vo]] show that state-imposed bans (Brazil's X ban) and targeted account suspensions (Arizona election-fraud promoters) produce durable, asymmetric "sorting ratchets" and authority-structure collapses rather than simple content removal, with conservative/right-leaning actors disproportionately persisting or adapting. [[Wang2026-ub]] documents the largely failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon, showing that even highly motivated, networked communities struggle to sustain decentralized alternatives — a finding echoed in [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s broader obituary for Twitter as a space of public debate, which argues successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) replicate Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows deplatforming's limits from another angle: Andrew Tate's YouTube removal did not reduce his visibility but redistributed it into a decentralized "ambient ideology" that outpaces moderation. Together these papers suggest that exclusionary governance reshapes rather than eliminates publics, often consolidating walled-garden fragmentation and enabling the very actors it targets to adapt fastest.

## The Data-Access Crisis and the Politics of Platform Transparency

Underlying nearly all empirical claims in this collection is a second-order problem: researchers' access to the platform data needed to adjudicate these questions is itself contested, uneven, and politically shaped. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] chart the historical arc from open APIs to "walled garden" paywalls, with the EU's Digital Services Act Article 40 emerging as a fragile counterweight. [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Peters2026-mo]], [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] all document the practical shortfalls of DSA-mandated access — misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, inconsistent implementation — while insisting this instability signals regulation beginning to bite rather than failing outright. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to the specific "clean room" model (Meta Content Library), showing how disconnected, code-only research environments structurally exclude qualitative and under-resourced researchers, reproducing Global North/WEIRD biases. [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Munger2025-cz]] both warn that platform-mediated research (like the Meta 2020 partnership) risks structural industry capture and can never keep pace with platforms' rate of change, while [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA transparency data itself to expose disparities — in moderator language coverage and publisher-labeling consistency — that undercut platforms' stated commitments. [[Bechmann2026-dr]], [[Ahuja2025-ku]], and [[Crosset2026-mq]] round out this thread by theorizing how regulatory regimes (DSA, NetzDG, US state laws) translate security and autonomy concerns into bureaucratic proceduralism — "security bureaucratisation" — that governs platforms without directly touching content.

## Moderation's Limits: Evasion, Coordination, and the Manufacture of Attention

Finally, a cluster of papers documents how moderation regimes are outpaced or gamed by adversarial actors exploiting platform affordances. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] detail how jihadist and far-right networks embed propaganda in platform vernacular (memes, coded audio, guest-appearance networks) to evade detection, while [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right Instagram content using positive affect and humor as normalization strategies rather than overt hostility — all suggesting moderation frameworks calibrated to detect "hate" or "extremism" as content categories miss ambient, affectively-coded circulation. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] argue that Meta's own definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has narrowed over time, leaving diverse "manufactured attention" operations — gambling networks, hijacked groups, propaganda — structurally indistinguishable from legitimate activity and thus permitted to persist. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] offers a countervailing, industry-collaborative account: even large-scale deceptive networks reached a relatively modest share of the 2020 US electorate, with effects on political attitudes largely disappearing after adjustment — a reminder that measured exposure and alarmist narratives about platform-enabled manipulation do not always align. Read together with [[Moran2025-qn]]'s account of the collapsing "trust and safety" profession and [[Gillespie2026-aa]]'s sociotechnical critique of AI red-teaming as moderation's next frontier, this cluster suggests that platform governance is not simply under-resourced but structurally reactive — always one step behind the actors and infrastructures ([[Swartz2026-zb]]'s "scam age," [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]]'s deepfake fraud ecosystem) it is meant to contain, a diagnosis that returns us to [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s foundational observation: the rhetoric of the neutral "platform" was always doing political work that obscured the editorial power exercised beneath it.
