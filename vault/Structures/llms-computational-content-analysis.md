# LLMs in Computational Content Analysis

## Pipelines in practice: classification, clustering, labeling

The empirical core of this literature is a small family of end-to-end "LLM-in-the-loop" pipelines that stitch classification, embedding-based clustering, and automated labeling into a single workflow for political text and media. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] is the clearest articulation of this logic, using OpenAI models at three successive stages—binary political classification, embedding generation for link clustering, and cluster labeling—applied to Italian Facebook data across two elections, and explicitly reflecting on why each stage demands its own validation protocol rather than a single blanket evaluation. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies the missing middle piece of that argument, running a dedicated horse race between `text-embedding-3-large` and the Italian BERT model UmBERTo on the same corpus and showing the embedding choice (and clustering algorithm) materially changes semantic coherence, with GPT-4o-mini enlisted cheaply as a quality rater. [[Larsson2026-ro]] exports the classification half of this logic to a decade of Norwegian Facebook posts, pairing GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment coding with human validation to support a longitudinal, non-English application. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] push the human-in-the-loop idea into qualitative and deductive content analysis: rather than treating the LLM as a classifier to be benchmarked, both reframe it as an interlocutor that externalizes decision rules, surfaces ambiguous or latent categories, and forces iterative codebook refinement, with final interpretive authority retained by the human analyst. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extends the same impulse to survey methodology, pairing LLMs with paired-comparison scaling to extract latent traits from open-ended responses without the labor costs of manual coding. [[Tan2024-vl]] steps back to synthesize this entire emerging subfield of LLM-based annotation and synthesis, organizing it around generation, assessment, and downstream use—a useful index for the more bespoke pipelines described above.

## Embeddings as the pipeline's hidden infrastructure

A second cluster interrogates the embedding step that sits inside nearly every pipeline above, treating vector representations themselves as objects requiring validation. Building on [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]]'s comparative benchmarking, [[Fan2025-ut]] shows that pretrained embeddings carry confounding signal—source, language, register—that distorts similarity-based clustering and retrieval, and demonstrates that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this signal cheaply and generally, without degrading out-of-distribution performance. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the embedding logic from text to images, arguing that computational social science needs *connotative* rather than merely denotative visual clustering, and showing a VLLM-to-text-to-embedding pipeline captures culturally loaded meaning (memes, protest symbolism) that CNN-based approaches miss, at some cost in strict object-level coherence. [[Bruns2025-fz]] generalizes this further into "practice mapping," proposing embeddings of multimodal network actions as an alternative to conventional (and notoriously unreadable) network visualizations. [[Waight2025-al]] shows what rigorous embedding-pipeline design buys substantively: by combining concept-guided LLM summarization, SBERT candidate generation, and fine-tuned GPT-4o annotation, it isolates "narrative similarity" as an estimand distinct from lexical or topical overlap, revealing diffusion of Russian disinformation narratives into mainstream U.S. media that text-reuse and topic-modeling baselines entirely miss—a direct demonstration of why estimator choice (the throughline of [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Fan2025-ut]]) has substantive stakes.

## Validating what the model can and cannot do

A third group stress-tests specific analytical capabilities against human or supervised benchmarks, often converging on the same lesson: LLMs are good—sometimes startlingly so—but unevenly and for reasons that are not always well understood. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] takes this to a meta level, benchmarking whether AI coding agents can reproduce published social-science findings outright, finding high accuracy but also documenting sycophantic specification-search when prompts cue a "correct" answer—a cautionary parallel for any pipeline that fine-tunes or prompts toward expected results. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows fact-checking performance hinges almost entirely on curated retrieval context rather than reasoning or web search per se, while [[Paci2025-ag]] finds a genuine ceiling: even the best multilingual models fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech. [[Brown2025-jk]] complicates bias concerns about annotation by showing demographic disagreement is small and dataset-specific, while item difficulty (human label entropy) is by far the strongest predictor of LLM-human agreement—a finding that reframes "bias" audits as partly an artifact of task difficulty. [[Lee2026-je]], [[Le-Mens2025-qz]], and [[Elfes2026-jb]] push LLMs toward harder inferential and theoretical tasks—inferring hidden political alignment from nonpolitical text, scaling political actors on ideological dimensions via ask-and-average prompting, and operationalizing Greimas' actantial model to measure "narrative polarisation"—each validating LLM outputs against human coders or external benchmarks and finding performance that rivals or exceeds supervised baselines. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends this to the visual domain, showing GPT-4o outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery. [[Meher2025-qb]] shows domain-specific fine-tuning (QLoRA on conflict event data) can make resource-light open models competitive for a classic computational political science task. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] round out this group by arguing automated frame analysis should move beyond text-only, predefined-frame paradigms toward multi-modal and unsupervised approaches capable of surfacing emergent framing patterns.

## Bias, provenance, and the epistemic status of LLM outputs

Running underneath the validation literature is a more unsettling question: what, exactly, are LLMs measuring when applied to political content? [[Waight2026-ts]] offers the starkest answer, showing that state-coordinated media is memorized into training corpora and causes measurable pro-regime shifts in commercial model outputs—meaning content-analysis pipelines built on these models may inherit political bias not through model design but through training data provenance. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s audit of Grokipedia documents a related phenomenon at the level of a whole knowledge platform, showing AI-generated content diverges from Wikipedia precisely on controversial topics and cites markedly lower-quality sources. [[Balluff2026-if]] synthesizes these worries into a direct critique of "unreflective" LLM adoption in communication research, flagging reproducibility risks from opaque corporate updates, demographic and linguistic bias in synthetic data, and environmental costs—functioning as a skeptical counterweight to the pipeline-building literature above. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] presses the point philosophically, arguing that traditional fact categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture GenAI outputs and proposing "emergent facts" as a category requiring relational, multi-indicator evaluation—directly relevant to how fact-checking pipelines like [[DeVerna2025-dl]] should be interpreted. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] supply complementary conceptual vocabularies for this epistemic uncertainty: the former argues chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding, while the latter reframes LLMs as communication partners within a media-historical lineage rather than as artificial intelligences—both cautioning against over-attributing comprehension to models used as content-analysis instruments.

## Infrastructure and data quality: what the pipeline runs on

A pipeline is only as valid as the data feeding it, and several papers document how badly that upstream data can be compromised. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API finds severe temporal decay and inconsistency that would silently bias any LLM-based content analysis built on API-retrieved video sets. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows an analogous problem for web-tracking research: ex-situ scraping—necessary infrastructure for many text-analysis pipelines—introduces systematic, non-random content distortion dominated by paywall and login interactions rather than simple time decay. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends the infrastructural critique to the "clean room" data-access model (Meta Content Library), noting that these environments often prohibit exactly the LLM-assisted coding workflows surveyed above, disadvantageing less resourced and non-programming researchers. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], while not about LLMs directly, bears on the same concern from the human side of human-machine workflows: the opt-in samples used to generate gold-standard human codes for validating LLM annotations vary enormously in representativeness and response validity, meaning the "ground truth" against which pipelines are checked is itself fragile. [[Fan2026-af]] broadens the lens to digital trace data generally, cataloguing six computational approaches—including language-based embedding models—for preserving temporal structure that cross-sectional content analysis usually discards. [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter shows a structurally different hybrid pipeline (language models plus graph neural networks) generalizing across coordinated influence campaigns, illustrating that the "LLM-in-the-loop" paradigm extends beyond text classification into network-structured detection tasks.

## Downstream stakes: persuasion, trust, and the politics of automated interpretation

A final cluster demonstrates why getting these pipelines right matters substantively, showing LLMs are not just analytical instruments but active participants in the phenomena being studied. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes what makes conversational AI persuasive, identifying a troubling persuasion–accuracy trade-off that mirrors the fact-checking validity concerns raised by [[DeVerna2025-dl]]. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] show durable, generalizable belief change from brief LLM dialogues across conspiracy beliefs and contested societal issues respectively, while [[Szabo2026-rd]] applies a similar conversational logic to inoculation against misinformation—together suggesting LLMs can be deployed not just to analyze political discourse but to intervene in it. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers a conceptual scaffold for the trust questions these interventions raise, extending media-trust scholarship to generative AI, and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] provides direct evidence that audiences cannot reliably distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, though disclosure shifts short-term engagement. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] supplies the psychological foundation these applied studies draw on, reframing misinformation susceptibility as a reasoning and attention problem rather than a purely partisan one—directly relevant to why LLM-based fact-checking and inoculation pipelines might or might not work. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows the mixed manual-plus-LDA tradition these pipelines are extending, tracking how "fake news" itself is weaponized rhetorically in partisan media. [[Matias2025-px]] makes the normative case that participatory, lived-experience involvement—not just technical benchmarking—improves the rigor of AI evaluation, a direct complement to the human-in-the-loop philosophy of [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]]. [[Allen2025-ot]] discusses an LLM-powered browser-extension method for platform-independent experiments as a methodological response to closing API access (echoing [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]]). [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] offers a cautionary parallel from personality psychology, finding validated trait measures correlate poorly with text-derived linguistic features, reinforcing concerns raised in [[Brown2025-jk]] about the gap between latent constructs and surface signals. Finally, [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extends the entire logic of agentic LLM pipelines to scientific peer review itself, echoing the meta-validation stance of [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and closing the loop between content-analysis methodology and the scrutiny of research itself.
