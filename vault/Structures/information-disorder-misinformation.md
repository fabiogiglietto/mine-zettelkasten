# Information Disorder & Misinformation

## From Hybrid Media Systems to Cultural Narratives

The line of inquiry filed here begins from a deceptively simple provocation: that "fake news" is the wrong unit of analysis. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] argues, via second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system, that creator-intent typologies collapse once attention shifts to *propagation*—to the chain of observer-dependent judgments that carry a claim from injector to viral cascade. This move echoes back to the field's founding case, [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s analysis of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie, where dissent from a dominant hashtag narrative already showed how meaning is renegotiated as content travels across a networked public. A cluster of more recent papers pushes this anti-atomistic instinct further by reconceiving disinformation as *narrative* rather than discrete claims: [[Marwick2026-qd]] shows how Pizzagate-adjacent stories succeed as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic plots rather than falsifiable propositions; [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies a hermeneutic-realist vocabulary (drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan) for judging such stories as referentially and ethically suspect without reducing to true/false; and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] finds, in a four-state U.S. survey, that repeated cross-outlet exposure to a recurring narrative—not any single false claim—is what drives belief. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] formalize this insight methodologically, adapting Klein's data-frame theory and Hochschild's "deep story" to show how quote-tweet ecologies let influencers supply ambiguous "evidence" while audiences supply the interpretive frame—a collaborative sensemaking dynamic that also structures how Kenyan audiences negotiate the believability of AI deepfakes in [[Wack2026-bt]]. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] give this narrative turn empirical teeth: the former builds an LLM pipeline to trace how Russian "biolab" claims migrated from state media into low-quality and even mainstream U.S. outlets, and the latter shows that true mainstream articles are routinely co-opted to support misleading narratives—both demonstrating that source-level blocklists systematically undercount the phenomenon. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] complete the reframing by showing that the term "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization on both left and right, eroding Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance rather than describing any stable referent.

## Platforms as Distinct Ecosystems

A second thread insists that "social media" is not a single object. TikTok emerges as a particularly generative site: [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-theorized vector of Ukraine-war propaganda, [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents Filipino "dogshows" as a culturally specific genre of legal-but-harmful humor, [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds—against expectations set by X-based studies—that Canadian immigration content skews pro-immigration while sharing a common affordance-vernacular across ideological camps, and [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows ConspiracyTok is populated disproportionately by young, non-white, and female creators enacting a "generous epistemology" of populist knowledge production. Instagram receives comparable platform-specific treatment: [[Nangle2026-yo]] traces an "affective-discursive ecology" of far-right softening through humor and algorithmic proximity, [[Gardam2025-er]] proposes a visual-first methodology for image-centric conspiracy discourse, and [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that prebunking ads can be scaled at Instagram's advertising infrastructure with durable effects. Telegram and Bluesky anchor a parallel strand on encrypted/decentralized spaces: [[Rothut2026-or]] traces how Querdenken's Telegram channels became structurally embedded in transnational far-right networks, [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] finds coordinated forwarding among pro-government channels in Russia and Belarus, and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] proposes real-time bot detection for Bluesky as an emerging decentralized alternative. Facebook remains the most heavily instrumented platform, largely through the URL Shares dataset: [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends U.S.-style newsfeed audits to Germany, France, and Italy; [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], and [[Holt2026-zq]] converge on a sobering empirical finding—untrustworthy content is concentrated among a small subset of older users, exposure reductions produce few attitudinal effects, and user "False News" reports track polarized disagreement more than falsity—while [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] shows how a "Zombie Internet" of engagement-optimized, poorly moderated groups blurs coordinated and organic activity across three structurally similar but motivationally distinct operations. Cross-platform comparison is itself becoming a genre: [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] track divergent partisan/low-quality news engagement and rising TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence across platforms, while [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] shows that the field's dominant "direct referrer" measurement radically underestimates platforms' true counterfactual contribution to misinformation exposure.

## Coordination, Bots, and State-Sponsored Propaganda

Beneath platform-level description sits a persistent question of orchestration. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers the field's most systematic theoretical corrective, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" wrongly conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent, and proposing a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to a principal's stakes and resources. Empirical work fleshes out this landscape from multiple angles: [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg Internet Research Agency shows it was less a Kremlin-directed unit than an outsourced media-labor operation embedded in Russia's ordinary PR economy, recruited around the 2014 Crimea annexation rather than the 2016 U.S. election; [[Kim2026-br]] applies explainable ML to two decades of Korean news comments and finds suspected trolls rely on morally condemning rhetoric ("Condemning Korea") more than overt foreign-agenda promotion; and [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a deliberate contest over attention and narrative ownership, often bot-amplified. [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin case study and [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience model of Russian wartime propaganda both emphasize that contemporary state communication exploits, rather than evades, the platform infrastructures meant to certify authenticity and legitimacy. [[Beacken2026-zb]] extends this to generative AI, finding that GenAI propaganda adoption across six democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) is shaped by local structural conditions rather than following a uniform technological trajectory, and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] observes that manipulation campaigns persist by opportunistically appropriating new crises rather than dying out.

## Mainstreaming and the Far Right

A closely related strand examines how radical content migrates from the margins toward the mainstream. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization research reframes the internet as catalyst rather than cause, and argues that online/offline separation is no longer analytically tenable. [[Rothut2026-or]] operationalizes "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" through the Querdenken case, showing anti-elite framing—not explicit ideology—as the bridge connecting moderate sympathizers to far-right and conspiracist milieus, a dynamic echoed in [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s account of humor-softened extremism on Instagram and [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s ethnography of "redpilling," which reframes far-right radicalization as gradual socialization sustained by disinformation self-presented as rational "evidence." [[Copland2025-em]] shows an analogous mainstreaming dynamic at the outlet level, tracing how Sky News Australia became a node bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing propaganda ecosystems via a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy, while [[Righetti2025-slf9]] documents a transnational anti-gender organization, CitizenGO, using centrally coordinated multilingual networks to mainstream and globalize anti-gender and pro-life narratives—directly relevant to the PROMPT project's LGBTQI+ narrative strand.

## Elections, Elites, and Democratic Erosion

Electoral contexts sharpen the stakes of these dynamics. In Brazil, [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel data to show that belief in electoral fraud claims independently erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging-app news use (WhatsApp) working indirectly through misinformation belief—a finding given causal weight by [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation field experiment. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends the hostile-media-effect tradition into misinformation research, documenting a robust "hostile misinformation effect" across the 2024 European Parliament elections whereby citizens see their own side as more targeted than their opponents, moderated by interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation. Party-level analyses complement these citizen-level findings: [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 32-million-tweet cross-national study finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing—only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does—while [[Tai2026-qk]] examines how institutional role and ideology check or enable politicians' own sharing behavior, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to citizens' disinformation concerns. [[Renault2025-uh]] tests the partisan-symmetry question under X's Community Notes and finds Republicans still flagged 2.3 times more often than Democrats, undercutting claims that crowdsourced moderation would neutralize alleged fact-checker bias. On the intervention side, [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] demonstrates experimentally that simply hiding elite-authored (and elite-referencing) content on X reduces low-credibility sharing and misinformation susceptibility without denting engagement—reframing misinformation as an exposure-architecture problem—while [[De_Leon2025-qn]] proposes "networked exposure" to capture how even ordinary content becomes politically weaponized once embedded in partisan circulation networks. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] situates all of this within a broader argument that platforms bear direct responsibility for global democratic backsliding.

## Conspiracy Belief: Psychology, Vernacular Epistemology, and Search

A parallel line examines conspiracy belief as a psychological and infrastructural phenomenon. [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel-plus-web-tracking design shows COVID conspiracy belief arising from a "marriage" of alternative-media contagion and mainstream-media mitigation effects, filtered through populist selective exposure and counterarguing. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts converges on a pessimistic, multi-level diagnosis—governance and journalism-ecosystem interventions rated more promising than individual media literacy—while [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] asks whether journalistic framing itself inadvertently seeds new conspiracy beliefs. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates assumptions about conspiracist defensiveness, finding that high-conspiracy-belief users trust "balanced" climate chatbots *more* than low-belief users, and [[Lieu2025-nl]] shows content category (attacks on climate solutions specifically) matters more than logical-fallacy type for perceived veracity and polarization. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] extends the inquiry to search engines, finding Google's AI Overviews debunk the established chemtrails conspiracy fairly reliably but sometimes legitimize the newer "15-minute cities" narrative by framing it as legitimate "debate."

## Countermeasures: Prebunking, Fact-Checking, and Platform Governance

The corpus devotes substantial attention to what can be done. Inoculation research has moved decisively into the field: [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]'s Instagram ad campaign and [[Szabo2026-rd]]'s LLM-powered "Conversational Inoculation" chatbot both show measurable, sometimes durable resistance gains, while [[Ramos2026-qo]] proposes narrative monitoring and argument-checking as necessary complements to claim-by-claim fact-checking, illustrated through Maldita.es's handling of the Valencia flood conspiracy. Fact-checking's institutional position is itself under strain and scrutiny: [[Farkas2026-lr]] analyzes how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funded partnerships via differentiation and transcendence strategies, [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] finds that Meta's post-Zuckerberg framing of fact-checkers as censors is empirically unfounded (removal occurs in only ~30% of cases), and [[Rogers2026-cy]] offers fact-checkers a mapping methodology for locating "post-truth spaces" via network centrality, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook ahead of the 2024 elections and EU referendum—directly bearing on PROMPT's Ukraine-war narrative interest. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] pushes fact-checking epistemology itself to adapt, proposing "emergent facts" as a category for generative AI outputs that fit none of the classical evidence-based, interpretative, or rule-based frameworks.

## Generative AI and the Synthetic Turn

A rapidly growing subset addresses generative AI's disruption of the field's basic categories. [[Emilio2026-ik]] articulates the most ambitious framing—a four-layer "synthetic reality" stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and a "Generative AI Paradox" whereby ubiquitous synthetic media may lead societies to rationally discount all digital evidence. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLMs are poor political fact-checkers absent curated retrieval, though search-enabled models exhibit a left-leaning citation skew; [[Choi2026-bz]] documents a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes confidence toward subsequent same-modality fakes; and [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds visual disinformation is not universally more persuasive than text—only issue-salient, plausible-footage contexts (Ukraine) favor video over text or AI-generated stills. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenyan deepfake experiment reinforces the narrative-turn's core insight even here: audience commentary, not production quality, determines persuasive impact.

## Measurement, Method, and Meta-Science

Running beneath all these substantive threads is sustained methodological reflexivity. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as measurable supply-demand imbalances predicting misinformation prevalence; [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard itself, showing continuous scores are far more stable and defensible than binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs; [[Nenno2025-xa]] tests news-values theory across 24 WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, finding the U.S. and Brazil as polarization-driven outliers; and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's WEIRD-centrism. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] both call for complexity-aware, portfolio-style thinking—the former critiquing the virus/warfare/pollution analogies that dominate policy discourse, the latter importing six lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology, including skepticism toward cognitive-only interventions and company-funded research. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] extend measurement into affect and toxicity, showing fact-checks are themselves often emotionally charged and that influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct, more homogeneous toxic backlash than user-sourced misinformation. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] closes the loop empirically, using digital diaries to show that everyday "misinformation" as lay audiences experience it is textual, mainstream-sourced, and concentrated in mundane domains like cost-of-living advice—a reminder that the sprawling theoretical and technical apparatus surveyed here must ultimately answer to how ordinary users actually encounter, judge, and act on the difference between true and false in daily life.
