# LLMs in Content Analysis

## Coding pipelines: from fine-tuned classifiers to prompt-based measurement

The corpus documents a rapid shift in how political-communication scholars operationalize content analysis, moving from supervised fine-tuning of encoder models toward prompt-based use of generative LLMs, often within composite pipelines. [[Bailard2024-pj]] represents the earlier paradigm, fine-tuning DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames in Proud Boys Telegram messages at scale, and pairing the resulting time series with offline event data via Granger causality — a reminder that "LLMs in content analysis" need not mean generative prompting at all. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] mark the transition point: the former validates GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification against human coders for a decade of non-English Facebook posts, while ConflLlama shows that QLoRA-based fine-tuning of an open Llama model on GTD event types dramatically improves rare-class recall over both zero-shot LLMs and prior BERT-style tools (ConfliBERT), arguing for domain-adapted open models as more accessible successors to both manual coding and encoder-based automation. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends this logic to the visual domain, showing GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, Google Vision) for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery — a finding that, alongside [[Arminio2025-tw]]'s demonstration that vision-LLMs generate connotatively richer, more interpretable image clusters than CNN embeddings, suggests multimodal LLMs are displacing task-specific vision models much as text LLMs displaced bag-of-words classifiers. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] push the paradigm further into measurement territory: the former shows that simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses recovers established scaling benchmarks, while the latter proposes LLM-paired comparisons for scaling open-ended survey text into latent traits, together reframing LLMs less as annotators of pre-defined categories and more as flexible measurement instruments. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this broader shift toward LLM-based annotation and synthesis as a coherent methodological subfield.

## Validating pipelines: embeddings, granularity, and epistemic labor

Several papers zoom in on the embedding layer specifically, treating it as an object of validation in its own right rather than a black box. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] directly compares OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian BERT-derived UmBERTo for clustering Italian political news, finding the LLM-based embedding consistently more semantically coherent — a result [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds on in describing a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (fine-tuned political classifier → embeddings → cluster labeling) for the same Italian election corpus, and whose central methodological contribution is less the pipeline itself than the difficulty of validating it: LLM-generated narrative clusters vary in granularity in ways that destabilize the very definition of "narrative," and the authors argue crowdsourced validators are inadequate once LLMs routinely outperform low-skilled human coders, necessitating expert annotators instead. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a related but distinct embedding pathology — confounding by source or language — showing that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove spurious structure from pretrained embeddings without degrading downstream performance, a low-cost fix directly relevant to any pipeline (like Giglietto's or Marino's) that pools texts across sources. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Elfes2026-jb]] show what becomes possible once embedding and LLM-based distillation are combined for higher-order constructs: Waight uses LLM-distilled claims plus SBERT retrieval to measure "narrative similarity" (distinguishing it from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity) and traces Russian "biolabs" narrative diffusion into mainstream U.S. outlets, while Elfes operationalizes Greimas' actantial model via an open LLM to detect "narrative polarisation" in YouTube discourse on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, finding — against the echo-chamber expectation — that comments *reduce* surface narrative divergence between partisan groups even as deeper narrative motifs persist. [[Ober2026-vd]] offers a qualitative-methods counterpart, embedding topic modeling and LLM prompt engineering into a human-driven codebook-refinement process for interview transcripts, explicitly foregrounding human oversight as the safeguard against hallucination and construct drift that Marino's essay identifies as a general pipeline risk.

## Reliability, disagreement, and what LLMs actually infer

A cluster of papers interrogates coding reliability and human–machine disagreement directly. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that LLM annotator bias on contentious labeling tasks (toxicity, offensiveness, politeness) is small, dataset- rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (human label entropy) as a predictor of LLM–human agreement — a relatively reassuring result about demographic bias in annotation. [[Paci2025-ag]] complicates the reassurance considerably: even strong LLMs fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, with only modest gains from chain-of-thought prompting, suggesting that pragmatic, context-laden coding tasks remain a genuine frontier of disagreement rather than a solved problem. Two papers push the inference question beyond intended coding categories into what LLMs can extract that human coders were never asked to find: [[Lee2026-je]] shows GPT-4o and Llama can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized lexical cues, outperforming supervised baselines especially once predictions are aggregated per user — a capability the authors frame explicitly as a privacy risk as much as an analytic tool. [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter, combining language-model and graph-neural-network signals, similarly detects coordinated inauthentic behavior across information operations with strong out-of-distribution generalization, reinforcing that LLM-based inference in this domain increasingly targets latent, adversarial, or undisclosed properties of texts and accounts rather than manifest content categories alone.

## Fact-checking, epistemics, and the limits of AI-generated truth

A distinct but connected strand addresses LLMs' role in verifying rather than merely classifying political claims. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] provides the most systematic empirical test: standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly at fact-checking PolitiFact claims from internal knowledge or generic web search, but a curated retrieval-augmented pipeline improves macro F1 by over 200% — with the caveat that search-enabled models disproportionately cite left-leaning (if credible) sources. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] steps back to ask what "a fact" even means once generative systems produce plausible-but-unanchored outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a fourth epistemic category alongside evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts — a theoretical complement to DeVerna's empirical demonstration that curated evidence, not model scale, is the binding constraint on AI fact-checking reliability. This epistemic caution sits in some tension with a more optimistic strand on AI as a corrective communicative agent: [[Costello2024-bg]] shows durable (≥2-month), generalizing reductions in conspiracy belief from personalized GPT-4 dialogues; [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this to climate, vaccination, and inequality attitudes, finding effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science; and [[Szabo2026-rd]] demonstrates a chatbot-based "conversational inoculation" outperforming static reading/writing inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled. [[Dubey2026-bl]], however, complicates the trust story: "balanced" chatbots presenting mainstream and conspiratorial views side by side are trusted *more* by high-conspiracy-belief users, contrary to expectation, while [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely labeling a model as biased against one's party — regardless of actual content — suppresses persuasion by roughly a quarter, implicating perceived neutrality as a lever independent of argument quality. Together these papers suggest that LLM-mediated persuasion and fact-checking are not simply about model capability but about perceived source credibility, curated evidence access, and user disposition — concerns that also surface in audience-facing work on AI transparency: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds disclosure of AI authorship paradoxically *increases* short-term reading willingness without shifting general acceptance, while [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows trust penalties for AI-disclosed journalism vary sharply by task (fact-checking penalized most) and reader profile.

## Meta-level reckonings: critique, reproducibility, and the understanding question

The topic closes on a self-reflexive register. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers a direct methodological corrective to the enthusiasm evident elsewhere in this set, cataloguing reproducibility, environmental, and bias trade-offs of unreflective LLM adoption and urging researchers toward smaller, open, task-appropriate models where feasible — a critique that indirectly bears on nearly every pipeline paper above (Marino's cost transparency, Meher's emphasis on consumer-hardware accessibility, and Giglietto's cheap fine-tuning gesture toward exactly this "least resource-intensive method" ethic). [[Allen2025-ot]] situates a related methodological innovation — LLM-based reranking for platform-independent experiments — within the broader crisis of researcher access to platform APIs, arguing such tools are becoming necessary rather than merely convenient. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] provides the deepest theoretical challenge to the entire enterprise, arguing via Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding — a claim that reframes every validation exercise in this corpus (Marino's, Ober's, Brown's) as fundamentally about managing a non-understanding system rather than calibrating an imperfect understander. Finally, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends the reflexive turn to the research process itself, showing that frontier coding agents can reproduce a large share of published social-science findings yet are also susceptible to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting — a fitting capstone finding, since it suggests that LLMs' promise and peril for content analysis (capability, bias, and manipulability) recur even when the object being coded is science itself.
