# Digital Methods for Computational Social Science

# Digital Methods for Computational Social Science

## The infrastructure problem: from API openness to walled gardens

The papers filed here narrate, collectively, the collapse of a research paradigm and the scramble to build a new one. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] documents the "Golden Age" of Twitter research empirically — over 33,000 studies, 25% annual growth through 2022 — and then its abrupt reversal after the $42,000/month Enterprise pricing wall went up. Bastos2025-ya offers a companion elegy, tracing how Twitter's suite of APIs constitutes a specific, now-lost research infrastructure that shaped an entire subfield ("Twitter studies"). [[Freelon2024-sc]] historicizes this trajectory across six platforms, periodizing data access from "prehistory" through "academic cooperation" to today's fragmented mix of pay-to-play APIs, walled gardens, and unofficial scraping — arguing access is now permanently contingent on platform whim rather than researcher need. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extends this into the regulatory present, showing how the EU's Digital Services Act (Article 40) attempts to convert data access from platform philanthropy into legal obligation, but with highly uneven compliance. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review supplies the macro-empirical confirmation: social-media-based social science research tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s, then plateaued and declined in 2022–2024, precisely tracking API closures — with Twitter/X and Facebook overrepresented relative to actual platform use, and TikTok/YouTube/Reddit underused despite their popularity.

Several papers examine what has replaced open APIs. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] both interrogate the "clean room" model — Meta Content Library, CrowdTangle's afterlife — as an epistemically constraining infrastructure that privileges code-fluent quantitative researchers, forbids longitudinal or cross-platform work, and (per Cullen's "seeing like an API" framework) actively shapes what can be asked, not merely what can be seen. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that even within the DSA's promise of researcher access, "data quality" itself is a contested, political category — something academics fought to get written into the Delegated Regulation over platform resistance. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency mandates themselves as a research object, revealing systematic underinvestment in non-English, Global South moderation. Against this backdrop of closing doors, [[Zheng2026-bi]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] represent constructive methodological responses: TubeStats/TokStats build genuinely random samples of YouTube/TikTok content to escape popularity bias, while Rieder's audit of YouTube's search API demonstrates just how "forgetful by design" even sanctioned tools are — a systemic problem for any DSA-era researcher relying on it. [[Wang2026-ub]]'s account of academic Twitter's failed migration to Mastodon closes this thread on a sober note: even highly motivated communities cannot easily reconstitute lost infrastructure elsewhere.

## Ontological and conceptual reckonings: is "social media" still the object?

A second cluster asks whether the very category "social media" — and the methodological toolkit built around it — remains fit for purpose. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] makes the boldest claim: platforms have shifted from social-graph circulation to algorithmic broadcasting, generative AI is producing "platforms without users," and users are retreating into semi-private spheres, dissolving the conditions that made network analysis, content analysis, and API harvesting meaningful in the first place. [[Oswald2025-km]] complements this from the demand side, arguing the "production-consumption gap" — a small minority producing most visible content — means researchers reading platform data as a proxy for public opinion are staring at an iceberg's tip. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] intervenes at the conceptual level, proposing that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" — staples of the digital-methods vocabulary — be replaced by a feedback-loop framework that better explains why self-reinforcing dynamics rarely reach the catastrophic extremes these metaphors imply. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s review of algorithm literacy scholarship diagnoses similar conceptual fragmentation in that adjacent field. Together these papers suggest the toolkit's crisis is not only infrastructural (lost APIs) but ontological (lost object).

## Data quality, artifacts, and the traps of platform-provided data

A recurring, more granular concern is that platform data is not a transparent window but an artifact-laden object requiring forensic scrutiny before substantive claims can be built on it. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] is exemplary: the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset creates a spurious platform-wide signal that mimics genuine cross-national trends, a trap for any comparative or longitudinal misinformation study. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows analogously that web-scraping methodology itself — not just time delay — introduces large, non-random distortions in measuring news consumption, systematically biasing certain content categories. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the workhorse source-trust database underlying much misinformation research, finding that continuous scores are reasonably stable but binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip results depending on database vintage. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and Gaisbauer2025-br (filed as Gerard2025-br and Gaisbauer2025-er)... rather, Gaisbauer2025-br critiques oversimplified, outlet-only, single-country approaches to news-sharing research and proposes a multi-level "political cartography," while [[Lyons2026-ca]] models how linking survey data to passive tracking can correct for the biases of platform-only samples. Read together with [[Peters2026-mo]], this cluster insists that "data quality" in computational social science is not incidental housekeeping but a first-order methodological — and often political — concern.

## Detecting coordination: from heuristics toward general theory

A dense technical vein concerns coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) detection, tracing a clear arc from ad hoc heuristics toward generalized frameworks. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] is the capstone survey, synthesizing fragmented platform and academic definitions into a unified framework built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, and insisting authenticity and harmfulness are orthogonal axes — coordination can be authentic-but-harmful or inauthentic-but-harmless. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet operationalizes a minimal, platform-agnostic definition (repeated near-synchronous sharing of any identifiable object), validated against the South Korean NIS dataset. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] push into multimodality, showing that coordination across retweets, mentions, hashtags, and URLs is only partly redundant and that naive network-flattening (the field's dominant practice) discards structurally important signal — multiplex, temporally-weighted approaches perform best. [[Yang2025-iv]] proposes evasion-resistant detection based on statistical regularities in sharing speed rather than manipulable timing signals, while [[Pante2025-pq]] turns a critical eye on prior claims of inter-state CIB collaboration, arguing they don't survive rigorous control-dataset benchmarking. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE framework reimagines cross-platform influence entirely, modeling users as distributions over latent narrative clusters rather than direct interactions — identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed most cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X. Bastos2025-ol extends detection into the visual register, probing whether troll-farm accounts carry identifiable visual signatures. [[Graham2026-fb]]'s Reddit "botting" study is the productive counterpoint to this whole cluster: it shows that the human/bot binary underlying most CIB detection work is itself unstable, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property — a caution against over-confident automated coordination detection.

## Embedding, clustering, and multimodal meaning-making

Alongside network-level detection, several papers advance the embedding and clustering techniques used to make sense of large corpora of text and images. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a subtle but pervasive problem — confounds like source or language dominating embedding-based similarity — with linear concept erasure (LEACE), showing dramatic gains in multilingual and multi-source clustering/retrieval tasks without harming out-of-distribution performance, and proposing it as a default preprocessing step for any CSS project pooling heterogeneous text. [[Arminio2025-tw]] tackles the analogous problem for images, arguing CNN-based visual clustering captures only denotative content and misses culturally embedded (connotative) meaning; routing images through VLLM-generated textual descriptions before clustering substantially improves semantic coherence and interpretability — a Barthesian move that bridges computational and interpretive traditions. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues from the qualitative side that Instagram conspiracy discourse demands visual-first methods rather than text-centric ones, reinforcing the same underlying point from a discourse-analytic register. [[Bruns2025-fz]]'s "practice mapping" proposes vector embeddings of user actions as a way past the notorious "hairball" problem in multimodal network visualization. [[Fan2026-af]] pushes temporality into this toolkit, arguing communication research has neglected the fine-grained sequential structure of digital trace data and demonstrating six computational approaches (sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining, embedding models) on cross-platform donated data — showing, among other things, that language-based embedding models are most promising but data-hungry.

## LLMs entering the methodological toolkit

A fast-growing cluster asks what large language models can and cannot responsibly do as research instruments. [[Ober2026-vd]] proposes a human-in-the-loop pipeline combining topic modeling with LLM-assisted labeling for qualitative interview analysis, arguing this preserves interpretive transparency that pure neural approaches lack. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates LLM-as-annotator bias directly, finding that demographic bias in subjective classification tasks is dataset-specific, small in magnitude, and dwarfed by item difficulty (label entropy) as a predictor of LLM-human agreement — a reassuring but nuanced result for anyone using LLMs to scale annotation. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent measures, addressing the classic trade-off between closed-ended constraint and open-ended labor-intensiveness. Most ambitiously, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] benchmarks whether AI coding agents can reproduce published computational social science findings outright, finding surprisingly high performance (Claude Code: 93% task accuracy) while also documenting real dangers — PDF context inducing bias on non-reproducible tasks, and confirmatory prompting inducing "sycophantic" specification search. Read together, this cluster suggests LLMs are becoming simultaneously a research tool, an object of methodological scrutiny, and a threat to replication integrity, all at once.

## Survey recruitment and validity in the platform era

A more classical methods vein addresses survey research conducted through or alongside social media. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] is foundational here: it develops and validates a Facebook ad–based recruitment procedure using Pixel tracking and custom-audience exclusion to reach niche, stigmatized populations (Italian conspiracy believers) cheaply and efficiently — though its test of whether Facebook "interest" targeting actually captures ideologically distinctive respondents proves inconclusive, a caution echoed later in the corpus. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] broadens the lens to nine opt-in online panels, showing representativeness and response validity trade off against each other and that two trivial attention checks meaningfully improve data quality — essential groundwork for anyone recruiting samples through platform ads. [[Hinck2026-yj]] delivers a foundational methodological provocation applicable across this whole vein: survey researchers rarely provide evidence that respondents understand questions as intended, an evidentiary gap that undercuts validity claims regardless of recruitment channel. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows the flip side of platform-based methods' promise — using Instagram's own ad and poll-sticker infrastructure to field a scaled, real-world psychological inoculation intervention with lasting effects, demonstrating that platform advertising tools can serve not just recruitment but experimental delivery at population scale.

## Ethics of computational social science and platform labor

Running beneath the technical toolkit is a persistent ethical throughline. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues AI red-teaming — increasingly central to generative AI safety — replicates the value-laden, outsourced, psychologically costly labor structure of early content moderation, calling for the same kind of empirical, interdisciplinary scrutiny CSS eventually brought to moderation work. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] models an ethically grounded empirical approach to online toxicity, linking validated psychometric self-reports to actual Reddit behavior and finding that stable personality traits are a weak predictor of surface linguistic toxicity — a caution against over-interpreting computational proxies for psychological constructs. [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s controversy-mapping study of the Sam Altman firing, and [[Fattorini2026-bo]]'s survey-based account of Italian AI attitudes, both extend the toolkit toward emerging objects (AI industry discourse, public AI perception) using established digital-methods techniques (controversy mapping, representative survey analysis), showing the toolkit's portability beyond social media proper.

## Substantive applications as toolkit demonstrations

Finally, several papers function less as methodological interventions than as applied demonstrations of the toolkit in action across MINE's substantive concerns — platform culture, health information, intimacy, and gatekeeping — and are filed here because they model how the methods above get deployed. [[Volpe2026-um]]'s digital ethnography of micro-influencers, [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s interview-based study of "networked intimacy," [[Wedel2026-ix]]'s qualitative decoding of audience "informativeness," [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]'s gatekeeping analysis of Google News/Facebook health propagation, [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]'s cybernetic reframing of "fake news" propagation, [[Xue2025-bp]]'s study of emotionality in fact-checking, [[Lai2024-to]]'s Reddit-based estimation of YouTube video ideology, and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s essay on big data's transformation of political communication practice all instantiate, in different registers, the same underlying tension that structures this whole topic: platform data affords powerful new empirical purchase on social phenomena, but only if researchers remain acutely aware of the infrastructural, ethical, and epistemic scaffolding — increasingly contested, increasingly fragile — that makes such data legible as evidence at all. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]]'s survey of research APIs and tools in the DSA era serves as a fitting bookend, cataloguing precisely the landscape of instruments this entire structure note has traced from openness to enclosure and back toward regulated, partial repair.
