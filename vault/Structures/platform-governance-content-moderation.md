# Platform Governance & Content Moderation

# Platform Governance & Content Moderation

## From Content Removal to Systemic Governance

The corpus traces a decisive shift in how platform governance is theorized: from content-level moderation decisions to systemic, infrastructural questions of visibility, access, and accountability. [[Donovan2025-ws]] frames this transition historically, showing how "misinformation-at-scale" emerged as platforms migrated from non-intervention to behavioral moderation (coordinated inauthentic behavior, demotion, labeling) — a shift that was itself a business-model-compatible workaround for avoiding direct truth-adjudication. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the conceptual bedrock for this whole literature, showing how the very term "platform" was constructed discursively to obscure the editorial interventions platforms make. [[Crosset2026-mq]] extends this into comparative legal analysis, showing that EU and US legislation—despite ideological divergence—converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" in which transparency, auditing, and reporting become the mechanisms through which platforms are governed as quasi-public infrastructures. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] documents this same arc empirically across five platforms' election policies, identifying three phases (protection → civic-integrity expansion → backlash/rollback) that mirror the DSA-era's own trajectory from optimism to contestation.

This systemic reframing is echoed by [[Bechmann2026-dr]], who argues political communication research itself must move beyond causal, individual-level effects toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure — a call that resonates with [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Simeone2025-vo]], both of which show that individual content actions (removal, deplatforming) ripple through networks in ways that exceed simple subtraction. Simeone's study of the Arizona Election Review community demonstrates that targeted deplatforming can flip a community's authority structure entirely, while Rieder's audit of the "Tate-space" shows the opposite failure mode: recommendation-driven circulation outpaces removal, rendering moderation structurally too slow to contain ambient ideological content.

## The Discursive Management of Platform Power

A cluster of papers interrogates how platforms *narrate* their own governance. [[De2026-ld]] coins "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage policy change — rendering infrastructural shifts aesthetically visible while reframing ideological pivots as continuity. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] complements this with a discourse analysis of Meta's Newsroom, showing how terms like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" function as "floating signifiers" that let Meta position problematic content as external to the platform while it plays capable problem-solver — terminology that, notably, diffused into the very regulatory instruments (EU CPD, Australia's DIGI CoP) meant to hold it accountable. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows empirically how this rhetorical narrowing plays out: Meta's definition of CIB has progressively contracted from "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors," leaving substantial manufactured-attention activity (media coordination, gambling networks, political meme communities) on-platform under an increasingly permissive definitional net. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Google's framing of AI Overviews — corporate discourse emphasizing seamlessness and hedged "experimental" framing, journalistic discourse emphasizing risk, with the user's own voice and societal costs conspicuously absent from all sides. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] pushes back against one specific discursive move — Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors — with empirical evidence that removal was always rare and that fact-checkers themselves largely reject an "anything goes" free-speech position, while expressing serious skepticism about Community Notes as a replacement mechanism.

## Algorithmic Curation as Political Actor

A substantial thread demonstrates, with increasing methodological sophistication, that recommender and ranking systems are not neutral conduits but active political agents. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] provides perhaps the field's most consequential finding: X's algorithmic feed causally shifts users rightward via a persistence mechanism — the algorithm changes who users *follow*, and those follows outlast the algorithm itself, reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complements this by showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage on pre-rebrand Twitter was substantially explained by proximity to Musk and by rewarding "agitating" content rather than partisanship per se — with legacy-verified institutional accounts (news, government) the clear algorithmic losers. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] moves inside the black box entirely, reverse-engineering X's embedding space to show it inadvertently encodes users' ideological position along a single linear direction, raising direct tensions with GDPR-style profiling protections. [[Brown2026-br]] complicates the "radicalization pathway" narrative on YouTube, disentangling algorithmic from user-choice effects to find real but modest rabbit-hole dynamics, no independent radicalization effect, but a platform-wide tilt toward moderately conservative content for all users regardless of starting point. [[McNally2025-dn]] insists the News Feed is "not a black box" at all — its strategic interventions leave detectable, lagged statistical signatures — directly motivating the case for systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4).

Two large field experiments bookend this thread with contrasting interventions. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky Registered Report shows engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, emotional, and toxic content and distorts perceived social norms (crucially, more than it directly changes attitudes), while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing user enjoyment — reframing misinformation/polarization as a problem of algorithmically distorted social learning rather than individual susceptibility. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] reaches a structurally similar conclusion via a different lever: blocking political elites from feeds (rather than suppressing political content wholesale) reduces low-credibility exposure and improves factual knowledge without moving affective polarization — evidence that exposure *architecture*, not just content, is the appropriate governance target. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] reinforces this architectural framing at the multi-platform level, showing that the "direct referrer paradigm" dominant in misinformation research radically underestimates platforms' true causal contribution to exposure once indirect, counterfactual pathways are modeled — while also cautioning that platform bans would disproportionately curtail high-quality news alongside misinformation.

## Meta's Political Content Reduction as Case Study

Two papers converge on Meta's political content reduction policy as a paradigmatic instance of consequential, undisclosed algorithmic governance. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses breakpoint detection on Italian parliamentarians' Facebook data to show the policy's effects began roughly ten months *before* Meta's announced global rollout, produced a 72% reach reduction for re-elected MPs, and — crucially — was never disclosed in Meta's DSA transparency reporting, even as extremist accounts adaptively offset per-post losses through sharply increased posting volume. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] generalizes this logic across the US context, showing that Facebook's translation of shares into views is neither linear nor stable: partisan-audience content is systematically penalized and journalistically high-quality content systematically rewarded, with both effects fluctuating sharply in sync with known "break the glass" interventions around the 2020 election — direct evidence against treating platform governance as a stable background condition in causal research designs. Both papers use the Meta Content Library itself as method and object, illustrating cautious optimism about DSA-enabled infrastructure even while documenting its transparency failures.

## Community Notes and the Crowdsourced Alternative

As professional fact-checking comes under political attack, Community Notes emerges as the industry's preferred substitute — and a body of work interrogates whether the bridging-algorithm design delivers on its neutrality promise. [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged as misleading 2.3 times more often than Democrats even under this cross-partisan-agreement mechanism, undermining the claim that crowdsourcing eliminates the asymmetries attributed to fact-checker bias. [[Bouchaud2026-np]], analyzing the full corpus of 1.9 million notes, argues the bridging design structurally *undermoderates* precisely the polarizing content most consequential for elections — different diagnosis, same underlying concern about design-level limitations rather than implementation bugs. [[Farkas2026-lr]] reveals the professional fact-checking establishment's own anxious positioning vis-à-vis this shift, documenting how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform funding dependencies through differentiation and transcendence strategies, revealing internal disagreement (e.g., over accepting TikTok or state funding) that exposes fact-checking's still-emergent institutional legitimacy.

## The DSA and the Politics of Data Access

The largest thematic cluster addresses the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 as the central regulatory mechanism attempting to convert platform transparency from discretionary philanthropy into legal obligation. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] collectively narrate this transition's promise and friction: Article 40 represents a genuine paradigm shift, but implementation is beset by misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, inconsistent platform response times, and — per de Vreese and Tromble's deliberately non-catastrophist framing — turbulence that signals the regulation is *finally* constraining entrenched power rather than failing outright. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] historicizes this arc most fully, tracing platform data access through "Wild West," "Golden Age," and "Walled Garden" phases and diagnosing a bifurcated global regime that produces a "data abyss" excluding Global South and under-resourced researchers.

Several papers document empirical failures in the DSA's practical machinery. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality" — central to whether Article 40 delivers usable data — was largely absent from official EU documents and had to be forced into the Delegated Regulation by academic/NGO advocacy against platform resistance (Meta notably inverted the argument, claiming poor data quality made access futile). [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] provide platform-specific technical audits: TikTok's expanded Research API remains inconsistent and limited despite DSA-driven expansion, while YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," exhibiting severe temporal decay that undermines retrospective research and DSA-mandated systemic-risk study alike. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] extends the transparency critique to moderation labor itself, using DSA-mandated disclosures to reveal that Global South languages (Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese) receive dramatically fewer moderators per unit content than English — down to 7.5% of English's allocation on Twitter/X — exposing a structural inequity hidden beneath aggregate transparency compliance. [[Jurg2025-ur]] rounds out this cluster with a critical audit of YouTube's election-period moderation, finding inconsistent application of publisher-context labels across languages and opaque removal communications, and proposing "observability" as a richer standard than mere transparency.

[[Ahuja2025-ku]] approaches DSA enforcement from the design side, building a "law-to-design" framework mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto Article 25's autonomy-violation categories — demonstrated on the live X blue-checkmark case — illustrating how HCI expertise can operationalize legal categories for regulatory auditing. [[Bruns2026-yv]] and Bastos2025-ya offer elegiac historical bookends specific to Twitter/X, chronicling the platform's decline as both public-debate infrastructure and research infrastructure, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely replicate Twitter's form without solving its underlying governance failures.

## The Post-API Age and the Political Economy of Research Access

A closely related but analytically distinct thread documents the empirical consequences of platform data closures for the *field* of social media research itself. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history most comprehensively (prehistory → laissez-faire → authentication → limited options → academic cooperation), arguing data access is now permanently contingent and proposing concrete reforms. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the human cost in Twitter studies specifically: a 25% annual growth rate in Twitter-based publications through 2022, stagnating in 2023 and declining 13% in 2024 following the $42,000/month Enterprise API pricing. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this pattern across social science broadly, finding empirical social-media research plateaued and declined in 2022-2024 after decades of growth, with Twitter/X and Facebook chronically overrepresented relative to platforms like YouTube and TikTok that users actually prefer. [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] apply this diagnosis specifically to election research: Lukito's "price, proficiency, or permission" framework catalogs 72 access tools and finds consumption-side data severely underrepresented relative to production data, while Schulte's German election case study treats platform observability itself as an underdeveloped research infrastructure problem, documenting how platform-specific scraping constraints and algorithmic filtering bias what can even be studied. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] offer complementary snapshots — a survey of the API/tools landscape and an early independent-auditing precedent from Brazil's 2018 elections — both gesturing toward the same conclusion: independent, non-platform-mediated methods remain indispensable.

Underlying all of this is a methodological warning documented sharply by [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]]: even ostensibly privacy-preserving dataset engineering choices (Meta's 100-share threshold) can introduce platform-wide artifacts that masquerade as social signal, meaning researchers must treat platform-provided data itself as an object of critical scrutiny, not a transparent window onto reality.

## Industry Capture and the Crisis of Independent Knowledge

A pointed metascientific critique runs through several papers, arguing that platform dependency doesn't just constrain research access but actively compromises research findings. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together mount the field's most systematic case: drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and pharmaceutical industry science, they document that roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, that these ties are concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged cohort who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, and that combined author-editor-reviewer "industrial saturation" reaches roughly 80% of the corpus. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the underlying structural problem — platforms' exclusive data control makes this field uniquely vulnerable relative to fields like pharmaceuticals where independent data generation remains possible — and calls for both regulatory reform and "research on research." [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers perhaps the sharpest single verdict: even the best-resourced, most methodologically careful platform-academic collaboration (Meta2020) is structurally incapable of producing durable knowledge, because platforms evolve faster than social science can validate, meaning proactive disclosure mandates — not reactive study — are the only viable path. This critique reframes several ostensibly null or reassuring findings elsewhere in the corpus (e.g., [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s null effects of political ad removal) as artifacts of exactly the constrained, platform-permission research conditions these metascientific papers warn against.

## Adjacent Frontiers: AI, Extremism, and the Erosion of "Social"

Several papers extend platform governance concerns into emerging or adjacent domains. On generative AI, [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] frame red-teaming as the AI-era heir to content moderation's sociotechnical and labor problems, while [[Weinbrand2026-sf]], [[Dodds2026-df]], [[Richter2026-bt]], and [[Mota2026-sl59]] variously trace how AI discourse itself is shaped by hype, stakeholder composition, and Global North/South asymmetries — extending governance analysis to the meta-level of who gets to narrate AI's political meaning. On extremism, [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] both document how adversarial actors exploit platform vernaculars and algorithmic amplification to outpace moderation, converging on a shared conclusion that content-level removal is structurally insufficient against infrastructural and cultural embedding. On fraud, [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] reframe scams as constitutive of platform economics rather than peripheral abuse, arguing anti-fraud infrastructure itself functions as a governance and surveillance mechanism.

Finally, a set of anniversary reflections — [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] — provide the corpus's longue-durée framing, arguing that "social media" itself is undergoing categorical transformation: from networked to "clustered" publics (Gerbaudo), from reciprocal sociality to "parasocial" consumption (boyd), and toward AI-centered infrastructural logics that increasingly bypass the open web altogether (Helmond). Read alongside [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi consensus that platform and journalism governance are the two most urgently-rated intervention priorities for the next decade, these essays suggest the field's center of gravity has decisively shifted: the object of governance is no longer discrete pieces of content but the infrastructural, algorithmic, and economic architectures that determine what becomes visible at all.
