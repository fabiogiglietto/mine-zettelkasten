# Platform Governance & Content Moderation

## The shape of the argument

This collection charts a field in the middle of an infrastructural rupture: platforms that once organized themselves around the social graph are re-architecting toward algorithmic recommendation and, increasingly, toward closed ("walled garden") data regimes just as regulators — chiefly through the EU's Digital Services Act — attempt to mandate the opposite. Read together, the papers filed here trace three interlocking arcs: (1) a conceptual reckoning with what "social media" even means anymore; (2) an empirical and regulatory struggle over data access and transparency; and (3) a set of case studies — most concretely Meta's political content reduction policy — showing what happens to political actors' visibility when platforms quietly re-engineer the rules of circulation.

## Is "social media" still the right object of study?

A cluster of anniversary essays converges on the claim that the founding vocabulary of the field has expired. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that the social-graph paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — while [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media" to capture the shift from reciprocal exchange to one-sided consumption of professionalized content. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own coinage of "platformization" a decade on, using Meta's retirement of the Like button as a signal that behavioral inference has replaced open-web signal capture, and gestures toward an AI-centered "Big AI" phase of platform expansion into new sectors (space, defense, energy). [[Swartz2026-zb]] extends this reckoning laterally, arguing that scams — not sociality — are now a central organizing logic of platform economies, entangled with the same anti-fraud infrastructures that authorize surveillance. Together these pieces set the conceptual stakes for everything that follows: if the object has changed, so must the methods and normative frameworks used to hold it accountable — a claim [[Bechmann2026-dr]] makes directly, arguing that political communication research must move from individual causal effects toward theorizing platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure.

## From open API to walled garden: the fight over data access

A second, more empirical thread documents the "Wild West to walled garden" trajectory in granular detail. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodizes Twitter/X's data-access history — informal philanthropy, a brief Academic API "golden age," then a Musk-era commercial paywall — and reads the DSA's Article 40 as an attempt to convert access from platform generosity into legal right, though implementation remains uneven, a diagnosis echoed by [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], who argue that current turbulence signals regulation finally biting rather than regulatory failure. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows empirically how "data quality" itself became a contested, negotiated concept in the DSA rulemaking process, with academics and NGOs pushing (with partial success) against platforms that strategically inverted quality arguments to resist scrutiny. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] extend this into methodological critique, detailing how clean-room environments like the Meta Content Library constrain who can do research and what kinds of analysis are possible, reinforcing Global North/quantitative biases even as they nominally comply with transparency mandates. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out further, treating EU and US content-moderation legislation as converging on a shared logic of "security bureaucratization" — transparency, auditing, reporting — regardless of whether the underlying regime favors free circulation, patrolling, or flow optimization. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes one corner of this regulatory apparatus, building a "law-to-design" framework for auditing dark patterns under DSA Article 25 and applying it to X's paid verification badges.

## Meta's political content reduction policy and the politics of platform change

The topic's centerpiece case study is Meta's multi-year retreat from amplifying political content, examined from several angles. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] provides the most granular empirical account: using breakpoint detection on 2.5 million posts, it shows the policy's Italian rollout preceded Meta's announced global timeline by roughly ten months, cut re-elected MPs' reach by 72%, and — crucially — left extremist accounts able to compensate through sharply increased posting volume, actually gaining aggregate visibility even as per-post reach fell. Its companion piece [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] generalizes the mechanism, showing that Facebook's share-to-view amplification is not a neutral network property but fluctuates with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures, with partisan content systematically penalized and journalistically rigorous content rewarded — evidence of active curation, not passive conduit. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] supplies the discursive complement, showing how Meta's Newsroom rhetorically externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors while positioning itself as the capable technological solver, a framing pattern that [[De2026-ld]] theorizes more broadly as "changecraft" — the strategic narration of platform change across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X as continuity, benevolence, and incremental patchworking rather than power consolidation. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] situates the Meta case within a cross-platform pattern of election-policy phases (protection → civic integrity → rollback), arguing recent retrenchment reflects "platform illiberalism" rather than principled free-speech commitments. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] complicates the censorship narrative Zuckerberg used to justify ending third-party fact-checking, showing empirically that content removal was always rare and that fact-checkers themselves favor labeling and counter-speech over deletion — a finding that dovetails with [[Farkas2026-lr]]'s account of how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform-funded partnerships against accusations of censorship and collusion.

## Algorithmic curation and the visibility of political actors

A related empirical strand tests, causally, what feed algorithms do to political attitudes and account visibility. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that X's "For You" feed shifts users' political attitudes rightward asymmetrically — the effect persists after the algorithm is switched off because it reshapes who users follow — helping reconcile earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] shows that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage on post-Musk Twitter is better explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk than by ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lose visibility. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding strong "rabbit holes" but only mild echo chambers and no radicalization pathway, alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates that X's recommender inadvertently encodes a strong linear left-right political signal in its embedding space, raising GDPR-relevant questions about inadvertent profiling. [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] both intervene experimentally: the former shows engagement-based feeds amplify moralized/toxic content and distort perceived social norms (mitigable via a "diversified extremity" algorithm), while the latter shows that simply blocking political elites from feeds reduces misinformation susceptibility without denting engagement — reframing misinformation as an exposure-architecture problem rather than an individual failing. [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the visibility question to state-level bans, showing Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users circumvented the ban while liberals exited. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] round out this cluster with evidence that ad-delivery algorithms and opaque political advertising further mediate what publics and campaigns can see of each other.

## Moderation in practice: labor, evasion, and uneven enforcement

Beneath the policy and algorithm level sits a granular literature on how moderation actually functions — or fails to. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Gillespie2026-aa]] document the fragility and human cost of the trust-and-safety profession, the latter drawing direct parallels between AI red-teaming and the earlier, already-precarious history of commercial content moderation. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data to expose stark cross-lingual disparities in moderator allocation, with Global South languages chronically under-resourced relative to English. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's stated commitments during EU elections and finds inconsistent application of authoritative-source labeling and deliberately vague removal communications. Several papers examine adversarial evasion and the limits of deplatforming: [[Karo2026-dn]] catalogs five strategies jihadist supporters use to evade TikTok moderation; [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persisting and even growing on YouTube post-ban through diffuse replatforming; [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a hybridized, monetized Swedish far-right influence network on YouTube; [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows Instagram's far-right actors using humor and affect for normalization; and [[Simeone2025-vo]] offers a counter-case where targeted deplatforming of a small number of accounts did structurally collapse a network's authority and mobilization capacity — suggesting network-level interventions can work even where content-level ones fail. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] complicates the coordinated-inauthentic-behavior concept itself, showing Meta's definition has narrowed over time from broad "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors," letting much manufactured attention persist unlabeled. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[McNally2025-dn]] supply measurement infrastructure for this whole cluster — the former tracking rising mis/disinformation prevalence on TikTok, the latter showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm is empirically detectable rather than a true "black box."

## Crowdsourced and algorithmic alternatives to professional moderation

As platforms retreat from professional fact-checking, several papers scrutinize Community Notes as its replacement. [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under this cross-partisan bridging system, undercutting the bias rationale used to justify the shift, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows the bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design, with electoral risks across 13 countries. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] takes the more constructive, forward-looking view, surveying how LLM-based bridging, collective-dialogue, and community-moderation systems might be designed to strengthen rather than erode digital public squares — a normative counterpoint to the largely diagnostic tone of the rest of the topic.

## Discourse, regulation, and the wider information ecosystem

Framing all of this is a genealogical thread on how platforms talk about themselves and are talked about by regulators. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] remains the conceptual anchor, showing how the very word "platform" performs discursive work eliding editorial responsibility — a move [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] show persisting into the present. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as structurally built into engagement-driven business models rather than an aberration, while [[Mahl2026-hc]] synthesizes expert consensus (via Delphi study) that platform and journalism governance, not individual-level literacy fixes, should be prioritized — a view [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] sharpens into an explicit claim that platforms bear responsibility for democratic backsliding. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examine how propaganda and health information respectively exploit platform and gatekeeping infrastructures designed for legitimate circulation. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] extend the governance question into AI-driven harms — deepfake financial fraud and persuasive chatbots respectively — both concluding that meaningful regulation requires targeting intermediary infrastructure and disclosed intent rather than individual users or surface-level AI labeling. [[Richter2026-bt]] offers a longer-run, comparative view of who has shaped public AI discourse (and thus future AI governance) in the US versus Germany, while [[Volpe2026-um]] provides a ground-level account of how ordinary creators absorb and adapt to platform algorithmic demands — a reminder that the governance dynamics documented at the policy level throughout this topic are lived, daily, as precarity and adaptation by the platforms' own users.
