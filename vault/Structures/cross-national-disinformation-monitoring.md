# Cross-National Disinformation Monitoring

## Cross-National Disinformation Monitoring

The papers filed here trace the outward expansion of Italian-rooted disinformation research into a genuinely comparative, multi-country enterprise, built substantially through vera.ai and PROMPT collaborations. Rather than treating "disinformation" as a uniform object, these studies collectively insist that coordinated communication and political information campaigns take radically different shapes depending on national media systems, platform affordances, and regime type — and that only comparative infrastructure can reveal this variation. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] makes this argument explicit as an editorial manifesto, critiquing the WEIRD-centric bias of misinformation scholarship and calling for cross-platform, cross-context inquiry; [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes the critique empirically, showing that news-value signatures of flagged misinformation differ across 24 countries, with the US and Brazil emerging as outlier "polarization clusters" rather than universal templates.

### Germany 2021 and the infrastructure of observation

A cluster of papers uses the 2021 (and 2025) German federal elections as a testbed for methodological innovation in multi-platform monitoring. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs against specialized computer-vision pipelines for analyzing candidate visibility on Instagram, finding GPT-4o dramatically outperforms bespoke tools — a methods contribution enabling visual political communication research at scale. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] examines coordinated behavior and advertising in the same electoral run-up, while [[Schulte2026-df]] extends the lens to the 2025 election, using parallel scraping of X and TikTok to demonstrate that the central bottleneck in cross-platform electoral research is no longer analytical technique but *infrastructure*: inconsistent data access, algorithmic filtering, and lack of reusable observability tools. This infrastructural turn is generalized by [[Lukito2026-nb]], which taxonomizes 72 data-access tools along price/proficiency/permission axes and diagnoses the field's structural dependence on ephemeral, platform-granted access — a diagnosis that directly motivates why projects like vera.ai and PROMPT must build their own comparative pipelines rather than rely on platform goodwill. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] exemplifies the payoff of sustained data partnerships, extending Guess et al.'s US-based newsfeed-quality study to Germany, France, and Italy and finding election-year spikes in untrustworthy URL shares in Germany and Italy but not France — a finding only visible through coordinated cross-national data access.

### Brazil as a post-Bolsonaro laboratory

Brazil emerges as the topic's most densely studied "digital battleground," with papers tracking the arc from the 2018 elections through the January 2023 coup attempt and beyond. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] establishes an early auditing precedent with crowdsourced ad monitoring in the 2018 election. The 2022 election and its aftermath generate a tight empirical cluster: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use companion panel-survey designs to show that electoral misinformation belief erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) acting as an indirect pathway rather than a direct driver — a finding that [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Kansaon2025-id]] corroborate and extend through platform-side evidence: the former via a WhatsApp deactivation field experiment, the latter via network detection of coordinated WhatsApp groups linking misinformation dissemination directly to real-world mobilization (the doxxing of Supreme Court justices, the January 8 attacks). [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates the echo-chamber assumption underlying much of this work, showing that affective engagement within a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network was highly unstable and event-driven rather than a fixed ideological enclave. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shifts registers to elite communication, showing how parliamentarians competed for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID collapse — pro- and anti-Bolsonaro factions pursuing divergent legitimation strategies vis-à-vis journalism. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] closes the arc with Brazil's 2024 X ban, demonstrating a partisan "sorting ratchet" whereby platform-level deplatforming durably reshaped the political information environment rightward — a cautionary note about the unintended consequences of the very interventions disinformation researchers often recommend.

### Coordinated inauthentic behavior as a comparative genre

A second throughline concerns coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) as a methodological and conceptual category tested across regime types. The US 2020 election studies ([[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]]) reframe CIB to include financially motivated operations, showing reach is concentrated in a few networks and driven mostly by organic resharing rather than direct network posting — a caution against overstating the causal power of "networks" per se. This conceptual expansion travels: [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a more mimetic, harder-to-detect Serbian variant of CIB tied to the ruling SNS party; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] traces coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] finds dual-use coordination — both protest mobilization and state-aligned repression — during China's COVID-19 protests; and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] pushes detection into new platform terrain with real-time bot-coordination detection on Bluesky. [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies an "Authenticity Matrix" to Romanian EU election pages, finding hyperactive-user amplification across the political spectrum despite platform policy commitments — a finding that resonates with [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade South Korean study, which shows suspected state-linked trolls rely less on direct foreign-agenda promotion than on domestically resonant "condemning Korea" rhetoric that maximizes engagement. Together these papers suggest CIB is not a monolithic Russian-troll-factory phenomenon but a repertoire that adapts to each information ecosystem's affordances and detection regimes — a point [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] historicizes directly through an OSINT reconstruction of the IRA's organizational labor structure, showing its recruitment spiked with Crimea rather than the 2016 US election, complicating the origin myth that anchors much CIB scholarship.

### Beyond Europe and the Americas: influence operations as political economy

[[Gaw2025-ru]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] extend the comparative frame to the Global South's political economy of manipulation, respectively reconceptualizing Philippine influence operations as brokerage arrangements between political clients and covert campaigning firms, and comparing GenAI-enabled propaganda across six democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) to argue against techno-deterministic accounts of AI's political effects. [[Wack2026-bt]] complements this with a large Kenyan survey experiment showing deepfake impact is not fixed at production but "collectively negotiated" through audience commentary — a crucial corrective for monitoring projects that treat synthetic media as inherently and uniformly harmful. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes the dual-audience logic of Russian state propaganda around the Ukraine invasion, while [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a methodological tool — LLM-based narrative-similarity measurement — validated precisely on Russian "biolabs" narrative diffusion into low-quality and mainstream US outlets, demonstrating how far textual reuse-based methods undercount actual narrative influence.

### Platform-, party-, and media-level comparative studies

A final set of papers scales the comparison to entire party systems and platform ecologies. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] analyzes 32 million parliamentary tweets across 26 countries to show that misinformation-sharing is not explained by populism or right-left ideology alone but by their interaction — radical-right populism specifically. [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] both interrogate the 2024 European Parliament elections: the former documenting systematic radical-right media overrepresentation across five countries, the latter linking far-right electoral performance to voters' disinformation concerns — together suggesting a feedback loop between visibility, performance, and public anxiety about information integrity. Platform-specific studies deepen this picture: [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring visual securitization discourse per-platform (argumentative on Facebook/X, memetic and youth-targeted on TikTok, using decontextualized non-Swedish footage); [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a transnational right-wing propaganda node bridging Australian and US audiences differently by platform; [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube that hybridizes activism with influencer commercial logics; [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an underexamined vector of TikTok disinformation around the Ukraine invasion; and [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows the transnational anti-gender organization CitizenGO using centrally coordinated multilingual networks to mainstream its agenda across borders. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's own content-moderation claims during the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application of authoritative-source labeling that undermines the platform's stated commitments — a fitting bookend, since it turns the monitoring gaze back onto the platforms' own transparency infrastructure, echoing [[Lukito2026-nb]]'s call for regulator-driven rather than platform-driven data governance.

### Adjacent currents: AI discourse as comparative object

A smaller thread extends the comparative-monitoring logic to AI narratives themselves, treating public discourse about generative AI as a parallel site of national divergence worth tracking with the same tools built for disinformation research. [[Wang2025-zy]], [[Richter2026-bt]], and [[Mota2026-sl59]] each compare AI framing or stakeholder composition across national press and Twitter discourses (UK/US/China/India; US/Germany; Global North/South), finding systematic divergence in optimism, risk-framing, and who gets to speak — suggesting that the infrastructures and methods developed for cross-national disinformation monitoring are increasingly being redeployed to track the next generation of contested sociotechnical narratives, generative AI foremost among them, with [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] marking the point where these two research streams — disinformation monitoring and AI-discourse analysis — visibly converge.
