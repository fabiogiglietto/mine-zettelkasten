# Italian Election Media Studies

## The Italian backbone

The spine of this topic is a single, evolving dataset: Italian political news and social-media activity around the 2018 and 2022 general elections (with roots reaching back to the 2013 campaign). [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] opens the arc empirically, showing how populist parties' supporters cluster around insular Twitter-attention news sources and how Facebook engagement diverges into "amplification" versus "contestation" patterns depending on partisan alignment — a finding that frames the Italian case as a strategic test of hybrid-media and populism theories outside the Anglophone default. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] pushes the same 2018 corpus (plus 2019 European election data) toward an ecological, behavior-first reframing of disinformation, introducing "coordinated link sharing behavior" as a detectable signature of manipulation independent of content veracity. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this logic into 2022, turning a static list of coordinated actors into a live-monitoring workflow and surfacing concrete case studies — an M5S hyperpartisan network, clickbait-via-religious-pages, and a religious-proselytism bot operation — that make visible how heterogeneous "coordination" actually is in practice. Read together, these three papers trace a methodological maturation: from correlational insularity measures, to network detection of coordination, to continuously updated monitoring infrastructure, all anchored in the same two Italian elections. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] sits as a prehistory to this arc, documenting the 2013 TV/Twitter "second screen" campaign and establishing the hybrid-media lens (Chadwick) that later Facebook-centric papers implicitly inherit, even as the platform of interest shifts from Twitter-as-companion-to-television toward Facebook-as-primary-arena.

## From networks to language: the methodological turn

A second, more recent cluster returns to the *same* 2018/2022 Italian corpus but re-processes it through NLP and LLMs rather than network topology. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for clustering political news, finding the general-purpose LLM embeddings consistently more coherent — a small but telling data point in the broader "LLMs vs. specialized transformers" debate. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] operationalizes this at pipeline scale, chaining fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated cluster labels across both election years, and using the exercise to argue that validating "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipelines requires bespoke, expert-driven protocols rather than inherited crowdsourcing norms. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], though set in the German 2021 campaign rather than Italy, belongs to the same methodological lineage: it shows GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision models for face recognition and person-counting in Instagram political imagery, extending the group's turn toward general-purpose multimodal LLMs as an alternative to bespoke pipelines — and implicitly previewing where the Italian-election toolkit might go next (visual, not just textual, content). Together these three papers mark a shift in the programme's center of gravity: the underlying substantive questions (visibility, personalization, coordinated amplification) stay constant, but the instruments used to detect them are being systematically re-engineered around LLMs, with validation itself becoming an object of methodological reflection.

## Visibility, capture, and the fake-news frame

A third thread interrogates *why* certain actors or narratives become visible, treating visibility itself as a governed, sometimes manipulated, resource. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] returns squarely to Italy, using the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by up to 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's own announced timeline, and — crucially — left extremist accounts *better off* in aggregate reach because they compensated with posting volume. This finding resonates with [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which shows (in the US context) that Facebook actively dampens reach for partisan-audience content and rewards journalistic-quality sources in ways that fluctuate with platform governance events like the 2020 "break the glass" interventions — together the two papers argue that platforms are active curators whose calibrations are themselves a variable in election research, not a stable background condition. [[Balluff2026-bv]] finds a structurally similar dynamic through an entirely different mechanism — government-advertising-for-favorable-coverage in the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" — demonstrating that "media capture" of visibility is not confined to platforms or to hybrid/Central-European regimes but can occur within an ostensibly consolidated democratic-corporatist press system. [[Iris2026-pg]] scales the visibility question to the 2024 European Parliament elections, finding disproportionate Radical Right coverage across five countries regardless of outlet orientation, prior polling, or actual electoral results — a cross-national companion to the insularity/amplification dynamics already documented for Italian populists in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends this comparative lens to news quality itself, showing untrustworthy-URL shares rising in Germany and Italy specifically in election years, with older users disproportionately responsible for both exposure and sharing — directly complementing the Italian-election data underlying [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]. Finally, [[Mosca2026-yh]] reframes the "fake news" question rhetorically rather than epidemiologically, proposing that accusations of fake news in Italian newspapers function as weapons of strategic delegitimization selectively amplified by media outlets — a theoretical counterpoint suggesting that not all "disinformation" phenomena are best studied as detection problems at all.

## Coordination and inauthenticity beyond Italy

The workflow-based detection logic pioneered in the Italian context travels outward as a template for other national campaigns. [[Oprea2025-lf]] applies a hyperactive-user/inauthenticity lens to Facebook pages of Romania's leading parties during the 2024 EP campaign, finding substantial shares from accounts with the behavioral signatures first catalogued in the Italian coordinated-sharing literature, while also noting that restrictive API access blocks fully automated replication of the Italian workflow elsewhere. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] gestures toward a parallel investigation of political advertising and coordinated behavior around the 2021 German federal election, suggesting the same behavioral-detection paradigm is being tested as a comparative instrument beyond its original Italian setting, even if its own empirical yield remains thin in the available record.

## Infrastructure and access as the emerging meta-topic

A final cluster, concentrated in the most recent work, turns the lens onto the research infrastructure itself — the precondition for everything above. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the EU Digital Services Act has reopened (unevenly) platform APIs after years of data-access contraction; [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes this landscape further, mapping 72 access tools along "price, proficiency, permission" axes and diagnosing a structural neglect of consumption-side data and non-Western researchers; [[Schulte2026-df]] demonstrates the practical costs of these constraints first-hand, running a multi-platform (X/TikTok) analysis of the 2025 German election and concluding that the field's real bottleneck is the absence of reusable, standardized infrastructure rather than a shortage of studies; and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] historicizes the whole regime, tracing platforms' election-policy trajectories through protective, civic-integrity, and rollback phases largely reactive to US election cycles. Read against [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s direct empirical use of the Meta Content Library, this cluster reframes the entire Italian-election research programme: the coordinated-behavior and visibility findings documented since 2018 were only possible because of a particular, historically contingent data-access regime (CrowdTangle, then Meta Content Library), and the field's next challenge is less about new elections than about securing durable, comparable access to study them.

## The arc as a whole

Across nineteen papers, the Italian 2018/2022 elections function as the constant substrate against which methodological innovation (network detection → embeddings → multimodal LLMs), theoretical concerns (insularity, coordination, capture, visibility governance), and infrastructural conditions (CrowdTangle's collapse, the DSA, the Meta Content Library) are tested and refined — with Austria, Germany, Romania, and pan-European elections serving as recurring comparative controls that show which Italian findings generalize (populist media capture, platform-driven visibility asymmetries, radical-right overrepresentation) and which remain artifacts of a specific national media ecology.
