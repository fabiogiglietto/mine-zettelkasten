# Social Media Platform Affordances & Engagement

## Affordances as a Starting Point

The foundational move across this literature is to treat platforms not as neutral conduits but as structured environments whose technical features — feeds, buttons, recommendation systems, ad-targeting dashboards — actively shape what becomes visible and who gets heard. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] supplies the conceptual scaffolding: social network sites are "networked publics" whose affordances (persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability) generate distinctive social dynamics — invisible audiences, collapsed contexts — that users must learn to navigate. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] extends this into practice, showing how Twitter users manage "context collapse" by imagining audiences and performing a strategically curated authenticity, anticipating a recurring theme in this collection: that engagement with platforms is never simply spontaneous expression but a continuous, affect-laden negotiation with an imagined or inferred audience and algorithm. [[Bucher2017-qh]] pushes this negotiation into the realm of feeling, arguing that users' "algorithmic imaginaries" — moods, suspicions, folk theories about how Facebook's feed works — are themselves generative, feeding back into platform optimization as users strategically post, like, and time content to become "algorithmically recognizable." [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] rounds out this foundational cluster by showing how Facebook's affordances (tagging, liking, implicit sharing) are repurposed for "networked intimacy," in which private disclosure becomes a publicly visible, collaboratively managed performance — an early illustration of how feature-level design choices ramify into the texture of social life.

## Facebook's Feed: Gatekeeper, Black Box, or Curator?

A central empirical throughline concerns whether Facebook's News Feed algorithm meaningfully shapes exposure to political content, and how. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] offered an early, influential rebuttal to strong filter-bubble claims: algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, but user choices about what to click matter more, and friend-network composition matters most of all. [[Messing2014-jc]] complements this by showing that social endorsement cues (likes, shares) can override partisan source cues entirely, undercutting fears that social curation inevitably narrows political diets. This relatively sanguine picture was significantly complicated by the 2020 election-cycle Meta collaborations: [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] finds that ideological segregation on Facebook is far higher than web-browsing studies suggested, intensifies down the "funnel of engagement," and is sharply asymmetric — concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem that also houses most fact-checked misinformation — while [[Nyhan2023-gb]] shows that even a substantial algorithmic reduction in like-minded exposure produces no detectable shift in polarization, suggesting feed composition reflects identity more than it drives attitudes. Read together, these two studies model the tension this topic keeps returning to: algorithms visibly shape *what* people see, but the causal chain from exposure to attitude change is far less certain.

Where these studies treat Facebook's governance as a relatively stable backdrop, [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[McNally2025-dn]] insist on its volatility and intentionality. Giglietto's analysis of 130,000 heavily circulated URLs shows that the share-to-view amplification relationship is dynamically dampened for partisan content and boosted for journalistically credible content, with these penalties and rewards fluctuating in lockstep with known governance interventions (notably the 2020 "break the glass" measures) — direct evidence that Facebook operates as an active curator rather than a passive pipe, and a implicit challenge to treating the Meta-collaboration studies' snapshots as representative of a constant regime. McNally's decade-long time-series analysis of Guardian engagement data similarly argues that the News Feed is "not a black box": documented ranking changes produce detectable, lagged (~19-24 day) effects specifically on hard news, differentiating it from opinion, lifestyle, and sport content. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] provides the conceptual vocabulary that unifies these curatorial findings, arguing that "reduction" — algorithmic demotion without removal — constitutes a pervasive, under-scrutinized form of content moderation that platforms deliberately keep less visible (and less accountable) than outright deletion. [[Schulz2026-ts]] adds a final twist to the exposure debate, relocating the source of perceived polarization away from algorithms and friend networks and onto users' own self-selected posting behavior: people with stronger attitudes post more, biasing the visible discourse toward extremity regardless of what feeds show.

## The Emotional Economy of Reactions

A second strand zooms into a single Facebook affordance — the 2016 Reactions feature — as a site where platform design operationalizes emotion into measurable, consequential engagement signals. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesizes 64 studies to show that Reactions function as low-effort "paralinguistic digital affordances," with lifestyle content skewing positive and political content eliciting a broader, more negative spectrum, and with "Angry" reactions consistently correlating with higher engagement — a pattern especially exploited by populist and far-right actors. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] supplies original comparative evidence for this dynamic across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections: populist parties' confrontational posts reliably draw "Angry" reactions, and anger (unlike "Love") positively predicts sharing, suggesting that Facebook's affective affordances structurally reward adversarial political communication — a finding that dovetails with [[Rodarte2026-sl16]]'s account of Brazilian parliamentarians, who likewise perceive (correctly, per the computational data) that antagonistic, news-cycle-aligned content is rewarded while substantive legislative communication is penalized, producing a genuine "democratic cost" as politicians adapt their behavior to platform logics.

## Beyond Facebook: Comparative Algorithmic Effects

Several papers extend the Facebook-centered questions about algorithmic curation to other platforms, often via randomized field experiments that sharpen causal claims Facebook-based observational work could only gesture toward. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] offers the most direct causal evidence yet that an engagement-based feed shifts political attitudes: switching X users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed moved attitudes rightward and increased engagement, with effects persisting asymmetrically because the algorithm induces users to follow conservative accounts whose influence outlasts the algorithm itself — a mechanism the authors argue helps reconcile the earlier Meta null results with their own positive findings. [[Brady2026-ln]] similarly uses a Bluesky field experiment to show engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms (though not necessarily attitudes directly), while a purpose-built "diversified extremity" algorithm mitigates these effects without sacrificing user enjoyment — direct, prosocial-design evidence that algorithmic consequences are contingent on design choices rather than inevitable. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] dissects a parallel case on post-acquisition Twitter, finding that apparent right-wing algorithmic favoritism is better explained by rewards for "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk's own interactions than by partisan bias per se — an important methodological caution against conflating correlation with deliberate ideological tuning. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] brings a sock-puppet audit design to TikTok, finding disproportionate incidental exposure to pro-AfD content among politically disengaged German users, attributable to supply-side virality among unofficial "multiplier" accounts rather than overt platform favoritism. [[Wan2026-ai]] extends the question of algorithmic exposure to fully-automated feeds (Google Discover), examining how political efficacy conditions individual selection behavior over time. Theoretically undergirding this comparative turn, [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues TikTok represents a genuine "second generation" of social media, replacing Facebook-style "networked publics" organized around explicit ties with algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" — a shift with consequences (depersonalization, opacity, fragmentation) that help explain why audit and field-experiment findings on TikTok and X increasingly diverge from the earlier Facebook-centered literature.

## Visibility Games: Creators, Gaming, and Platform Paternalism

A parallel body of work examines affordances from the producer's side — how creators strategically interpret and exploit algorithmic visibility, and how platforms police that exploitation. [[Cotter2019-js]] reframes "gaming the system" as "playing the visibility game," showing Instagram influencers developing competing folk theories (relational vs. simulated influence) to pursue algorithmic favor, while [[Petre2019-zh]] documents the flip side: platforms' moralized, shifting discourse of "platform paternalism," which frames creators' visibility tactics as illegitimate manipulation in ways that mask platforms' own commercial interests and disadvantage resource-poor producers. [[Volpe2026-um]] extends this logic to micro-influencers in the sustainable fashion niche, showing how care-and-authenticity branding functions simultaneously as genuine ethical commitment and as reputational labor adapted to algorithmic demands for constant, niche-consistent output — a vivid illustration of how platform infrastructures shape even ostensibly altruistic self-presentation. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s integrative review of "algorithm literacy" research maps the broader scholarly attempt to understand how ordinary users (and, by extension, creators) come to comprehend the very systems Bucher, Cotter, and Petre describe them improvising against, noting the field's conceptual fragmentation.

## Cross-Platform Political Communication and Populism

Closely related is a cluster examining how political actors tailor content to platform-specific affordances to maximize visibility, often converging on the finding that populist, emotionally charged, or confrontational styles are disproportionately rewarded. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] traces this through the Sweden Democrats' 2024 EU campaign, which deployed argumentative text on Facebook and X but memetic, decontextualized "securitizing" content on TikTok aimed at younger voters. [[Darius2026-xl]] generalizes the pattern across 27 EU member states, finding populist radical-right (and, to a lesser extent, far-left) parties receive disproportionate audience support — especially on TikTok — driven by communicative style (emotional appeals, anti-elitism, Euroscepticism) rather than sheer posting volume. Together with Kalsnes's Scandinavian Reactions data and Rodarte's Brazilian case, these studies build a consistent cross-national, cross-platform picture: engagement-optimized affordances systematically privilege adversarial and populist communicative styles over institutional, substantive political speech.

## Governance, Regulation, and Platform Exit

A smaller but important set of papers addresses the regulatory and infrastructural conditions surrounding these dynamics. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] builds a "law-to-design" framework mapping HCI dark-patterns taxonomies onto the EU Digital Services Act's autonomy-violation categories, demonstrating how design-level reasoning can ground regulatory enforcement (illustrated via the X blue-checkmark case) — a complement to McNally's own invocation of the DSA as grounds for systemic algorithmic auditing. [[Wang2026-ub]] examines the limits of platform governance from the user side, tracking academic Twitter's largely failed migration to Mastodon after Musk's acquisition, with retention hinging on discipline-specific servers and cross-server engagement diversity rather than network size alone. [[Swartz2026-zb]] broadens the governance lens furthest, arguing that scams have become a central, not peripheral, organizing logic of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves functioning as a new mode of governance and economic citizenship — situating affordance design within a wider political economy of platformized deception.

## Methodological Innovations: Facebook Ads as Research Infrastructure

Finally, several papers treat Facebook's advertising and targeting tools not just as objects of study but as research infrastructure. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] develops and validates a Facebook ad-based recruitment procedure — using Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion, and conversion metrics — for reaching niche, hard-to-survey populations (conspiracy-theory believers), demonstrating the method's efficiency even as its ability to isolate ideologically distinctive respondents remains inconclusive. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] pushes this further, showing that Instagram's Story-ad and poll-sticker functionalities can be repurposed to scale psychological "prebunking" interventions to hundreds of thousands of users with measurable, persistent effects — evidence that platform ad-targeting tools, originally built for commercial engagement optimization, can be redirected toward public-interest behavioral research at a scale and cost unattainable through conventional survey methods.
