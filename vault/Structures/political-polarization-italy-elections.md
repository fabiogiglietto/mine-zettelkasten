# Political Polarization & Italian Election Coverage

## The Italian core and its comparative extensions

The backbone of this collection is the MINE research program's decade-long study of Italian elections, running from [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s early account of the 2013 "second screen" hybrid between television talk shows and Twitter through to the most recent audits of Meta's platform governance. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the conceptual anchor for the whole line: partisan "insularity" of news sources on Twitter combines with distinct Facebook engagement signatures (shares vs. comments) to reveal how the Five Star Movement, uniquely among Italian parties, converted a fragmented hybrid media ecology into a strategic asset for amplifying favorable coverage and contesting unfavorable coverage. This attention-economy logic is elaborated methodologically in [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframes disinformation detection around *coordinated link sharing behavior* rather than content or actor labels, and is extended forward in time by [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which turns the static list of coordinated accounts into a continuously updating workflow, uncovering hyperpartisan M5S networks, clickbait operations exploiting religious pages, and even a religious-proselytism network using Messenger bots — demonstrating that coordination detection generalizes well beyond party politics.

Methodological innovation is itself a throughline of the Italian corpus. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] jointly chart the shift from BERT-style embeddings to LLM-in-the-loop pipelines for clustering and labeling Italian political news, with the latter paper's reflections on validation (granularity of "narrative," inadequacy of crowdsourcing against LLM competence) anticipating a broader methodological turn visible elsewhere in the topic, e.g. the actantial-model narrative-polarization work of [[Elfes2026-jb]] and the unsupervised framing pipeline of [[Sarmiento2025-as]]. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] applies these tools substantively to COVID-era Facebook, showing how an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster and legacy journalists jointly remediate expert dissent into problematic amplification — a finding that echoes [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]]'s UK-based demonstration that newspapers' political orientation predicts which (mainstream) scientists get cited on NPIs, underlining that polarized science coverage need not involve fringe actors at all. Most recently, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] pivots the program toward platform accountability itself: using DSA-mandated Meta Content Library data, it shows that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed mainstream Italian parliamentarians' reach far earlier than announced while extremist accounts compensated through posting volume — a governance-focused capstone that dovetails with [[Jurg2025-ur]]'s YouTube moderation audit and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s five-platform historical account of election-policy phases (protection → civic integrity → rollback), together tracing an arc from studying *what happens on* platforms to auditing *how platforms govern themselves*.

## Cross-partisan engagement, insularity, and the echo-chamber debate

A second cluster interrogates whether "echo chambers" and partisan insularity are robust empirical phenomena or artifacts of measurement. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the classic counterpoint, showing that individual click choices constrain cross-cutting exposure on Facebook more than the News Feed algorithm does — a finding in tension with the insularity results from the Italian election studies. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review argues that much of the field's disagreement about whether echo chambers exist is an artifact of divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than divergent realities, a diagnosis that resonates with [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s granular Reddit analysis: cross-ideological overlap is vanishingly small even among "discursively resonant" subreddits, undermining r/politics's presumed role as a cross-partisan town square, and locating polarization not in discourse alone but in an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and user agency ("sedimentation"). [[Green2025-ap]] complicates source-level insularity measures further, showing that individual stories from ostensibly moderate outlets can serve sharply divergent partisan audiences — a "curation bubble" argument that has direct implications for how the Italian insularity metrics in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] might be refined at the story rather than outlet level, an agenda explicitly proposed by [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s "political cartography" framework spanning story, outlet, and content levels. [[Eady2025-vm]] supplies a parallel measurement contribution for elites, jointly scaling politicians, media, and the public on ideology from sharing behavior, and finding that electorally safe politicians share more polarized content — a structural, incentive-based explanation for insularity that complements the platform-affordance explanations elsewhere in this set. [[Schulz2026-ts]] adds a crucial corrective: much of what looks like polarized discourse online may be "falsely polarized," an artifact of self-selection into posting by ideologically stronger-attitude users rather than evidence of a more divided public — directly qualifying claims (including MINE's own) that engagement asymmetries reflect real partisan division. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] extend this measurement skepticism cross-platform and cross-nationally, respectively showing divergent engagement patterns with partisan/low-quality news across seven platforms, and demonstrating that alternative-mainstream audience overlap is generally low but highly conditioned by national media systems and populist parties' institutional access — a comparative lens that situates the Italian case (a "polarized pluralist" system per Hallin and Mancini, as [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] notes) within a wider typology.

## Coordinated inauthenticity, elite narrative contests, and epistemic authority

A substantial thread extends the coordination-detection paradigm developed for Italy into other national contexts, especially Brazil, while probing how elites themselves compete for narrative and epistemic authority. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts rapid-spread-network methods to WhatsApp, tracing coordinated Brazilian groups from misinformation dissemination to real-world mobilization (doxxing of Supreme Court justices, the January 8 events), while [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification on Facebook during Romania's EP election, and [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] describes Serbia's SNS party mimicking authentic grassroots support in ways that evade standard bot-farm detection — together suggesting that coordinated inauthentic behavior is increasingly diffuse, mimetic, and platform-specific rather than centralized. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework to authoritarian "connective counteraction," contrasting the centralized, synchronized pro-Navalny network with the modular, preventive pro-government astroturfing on Russian Twitter.

Parallel to coordination research, several papers examine how political elites contest journalistic gatekeeping directly. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians pursuing "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis through three distinct strategies — derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and proximity-based brokerage — while [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" traces how the CRT issue let a Republican faction build reciprocal, engagement-rewarded ties to hyper-partisan media, radicalizing intra-party politics. [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes this insight theoretically, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal networks) are the missing organizational link between disinformation research and institutionalist accounts of democratic backsliding — a framework whose transnational ambition is empirically cashed out by [[Groebner2026-pc]], which finds a genuinely transnational far-right YouTube audience anchored around US channels, and by [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]], tracing the cross-platform politicization of social justice discourse into "culture wars" framing. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both treat "fake news" itself as a rhetorical weapon for delegitimization rather than a description of falsehood, showing convergent strategic logics across US hyperpartisan outlets and Italian newspapers respectively — a discursive analogue to the elite epistemic-authority contests documented in Brazil.

## Brazil as a second national laboratory

Brazil emerges as a second deep case study, almost mirroring the Italian program's structure: platform-level structural shifts, misinformation's attitudinal consequences, and affective dynamics within partisan communities. [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows that Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users circumvented the ban while liberals exited — a stark illustration of how state intervention, not just platform design, can reshape partisan information ecologies, resonating with [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s Meta policy analysis in Italy. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] together map how belief in electoral misinformation depresses both political tolerance and trust in election results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) working indirectly through misinformation belief rather than directly — nuancing simple "echo chamber" stories with attitudinal and legitimacy-specific mechanisms. [[Marino2026-slef]] pushes back against static echo-chamber assumptions within a single pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network, showing highly unstable affective engagement patterns around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt, jointly shaped by *which* actors are mentioned and *when*. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] zooms out to a structural, Global-South-focused critique, arguing that platform privatization of public discourse and "data colonialism" disproportionately disadvantage Brazil and Latin America, using the failed Brazilian "Fake News Bill" as exhibit — a normative frame that complements the empirical Brazilian studies and gestures toward [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s broader call for globally equitable polarization research beyond US/European dominance.

## Depolarization, normative stakes, and open methodological frontiers

A final cluster steps back to ask what all this measurement is *for*. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review finds that depolarization recommendations in the literature are largely disconnected from the evidence base scholars themselves generate, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals — a sobering diagnosis echoed normatively by [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theorization of "destructive polarization" and deliberative reciprocity as an antidote, and popularized by the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] calling on scientists to test proximate framings as depolarizing interventions. Against this backdrop, several papers document specific mechanisms with direct policy relevance: [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows X's Community Notes bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing (and electorally relevant) content by design; [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, moderated by interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland; and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows experimentally that *narratives* about election interference — independent of interference's actual effectiveness — can themselves raise perceived and affective polarization, suggesting that alarmist coverage may be self-fulfilling. [[Iris2026-pg]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extend the 2024 European Parliament election as a shared empirical anchor, respectively documenting disproportionate radical-right media visibility across five countries and linking far-right electoral performance to voters' disinformation concerns. Finally, a cluster of infrastructural papers — [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Schulte2026-df]], [[Kristensen2025-ni]] — converges on the same practical conclusion that motivates much of the Italian program's own methodological innovations: that DSA-era data access, multi-platform observability, and alternative-platform dynamics remain fragmented research-infrastructure problems rather than solved measurement questions, leaving the field's next task as much about building shared tools as generating new findings.
