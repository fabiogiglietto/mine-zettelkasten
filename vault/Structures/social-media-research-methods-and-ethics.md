# Social Media Research Methods & Ethics

## The Post-API Age: Infrastructure and Precarity

A dominant throughline across this collection is the recognition that social media research has always been hostage to infrastructure it does not control. Bastos2025-ya offers a retrospective on Twitter's suite of APIs as the accidental backbone of an entire subfield of "Twitter studies," while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and [[Yang2026-tq]] quantify what that infrastructure produced and what its closure has cost: decades of steady growth in platform-based scholarship, concentrated overwhelmingly on Twitter/X and Facebook, plateauing and then declining once free API access ended. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history across six platforms, arguing that data access is now permanently contingent on platform decisions rather than stable scholarly infrastructure—a claim [[Cullen2026-cb]] examines from the inside, showing via interviews with CrowdTangle users how an API's technical design and governance rules constrain not just what researchers can collect but what they can even imagine asking. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to the successor "clean room" model (Meta Content Library, TDM Studio), arguing that Web-disconnected, code-only environments systematically exclude qualitative and cross-platform researchers and widen inequities between well-resourced and marginal institutions. [[Rieder2025-ju]] demonstrates empirically that even where access nominally persists, it may be unreliable: YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," exhibiting severe temporal decay and inconsistency that undermines replicability and DSA-mandated systemic-risk research. Against this backdrop of enclosure, [[Zheng2026-bi]] and [[Wang2026-ub]] examine attempts at workaround and escape—random-sampling tools built outside official APIs, and academic Twitter's largely failed migration to Mastodon—both illustrating how difficult it is to reconstitute open research conditions once platform-mediated access closes.

## Platform Power, Industry Entanglement, and Regulation

A second cluster interrogates the structural dependency this infrastructure creates. [[Heiss2026-qv]] makes the case most directly, drawing parallels to tobacco, pharma, and food industry science to argue that platforms' exclusive control of data creates unusually deep opportunities for industry influence over ostensibly independent research, from selective funding to institutional entanglement. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], read as a foundational precedent, shows how the very term "platform" performs ideological work—claiming neutrality while platforms make substantive editorial and algorithmic interventions—helping explain why firms can simultaneously court researchers and constrain what research reveals. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] takes a more applied regulatory angle, building a "law-to-design" framework mapping dark patterns onto EU Digital Services Act autonomy violations, with X's paid verification badges as a live test case, illustrating how HCI method can operationalize legal concepts for platform accountability. [[Moran2025-qn]] rounds out this cluster by documenting the internal collapse of the "trust and safety" profession itself, suggesting that political and economic pressures are eroding the very practitioner infrastructure that once mediated platform accountability from within.

## Survey Recruitment and Respondent Validity on Ad-Driven Platforms

A more methods-proximate thread concerns using platforms not just as passive data sources but as active recruitment infrastructure. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] develops and validates a Facebook ad-based procedure—leveraging Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion, and URL parameters—for reaching niche, hard-to-reach populations (here, conspiracy-theory sympathizers), finding it efficient and affordable but inconclusive as to whether interest-based targeting truly isolates ideologically distinctive respondents. This sits productively alongside [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], which compares nine opt-in online panels and shows a persistent trade-off between demographic representativeness and response validity, with simple attention checks meaningfully improving quality without sacrificing representativeness. [[Hinck2026-yj]] presses on an upstream assumption shared by both papers: that survey researchers rarely provide evidence that respondents actually understand questions as intended, an evidentiary gap that undercuts inference regardless of how sophisticated recruitment or panel curation becomes.

## Measurement Validity: What Do Digital Traces Actually Tell Us?

Several papers turn from access to the more corrosive question of whether platform data, once obtained, measures what researchers assume. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that ex-situ web scraping—the default method in most web-tracking studies—introduces substantial, systematically biased distortion relative to in-situ capture, driven mainly by paywalls and login gates rather than temporal decay. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit of the NewsGuard trustworthiness database, finding continuous scores reasonably stable since 2022 but binary "trustworthy/untrustworthy" classifications highly sensitive to a handful of border-crossing sources, with clear best-practice implications. [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Hartmann2025-px]] generalize the concern theoretically: the former argues that a small, unrepresentative minority of highly active posters structurally distorts both citizens' and researchers' inferences about public opinion, while the latter's systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that much of the field's apparent empirical disagreement is actually a product of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent underlying reality. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] contributes a complementary corrective from the qualitative side, using diary methodology to show that lay perceptions of "misinformation" diverge sharply from both scholarly framings (fixated on politics/health) and from independently verified falsity—a reminder that trace-based and definition-driven methods alike risk mistaking the researcher's categories for the phenomenon itself.

## New Analytical Methods for Trace and Behavioral Data

A more constructive methodological thread proposes ways of working within these constraints rather than merely diagnosing them. [[Bruns2025-fz]] introduces "practice mapping," using vector embeddings of interaction data to overcome the "hairball" problem of multimodal network visualization. [[Fan2026-af]] argues for a parallel reorientation toward temporal dynamics, reviewing six computational approaches—from sequence analysis to language-based embedding models—for analyzing donated, timestamped cross-platform trace data without collapsing it into cross-sectional aggregates. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] tests the limits of linking self-report to observable trace directly, finding that validated dark-personality measures correlate with self-reported toxic behavior but not with computationally extracted linguistic toxicity markers—a caution against assuming psychometric constructs translate cleanly into textual signal, one very much in the spirit of Hinck's comprehension-evidence critique above.

## Authenticity, Audience, and the Human–Machine Boundary

A further set of papers interrogates what platform data actually represents once collected, focusing on self-presentation and authenticity as constructed rather than given. [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s foundational account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience on Twitter establishes that users' visible behavior is already a strategic performance shaped by uncertainty about readership—a caution that long predates, but directly informs, later concerns about representativeness of platform traces. [[Graham2026-fb]]'s study of Reddit "botting" pushes this further, arguing that the human/bot distinction so central to platform trust-and-safety and to computational detection is itself culturally negotiated rather than technically fixed. [[Volpe2026-um]]'s ethnography of micro-influencers extends the same logic to platform labor, showing how ostensibly ethical, community-oriented content performances are inseparable from algorithmically incentivized self-branding—authenticity again revealed as strategic accommodation to platform demands rather than an essential property researchers can simply read off the data.

## Ethics of Studying (and Building) Sociotechnical Systems

The collection's engagement with research ethics extends beyond data access into the labor and governance of the systems being studied. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues that AI red-teaming, now central to AI safety policy, repeats the underexamined labor and psychological harms of early content moderation, calling for a coordinated empirical research agenda before opaque, market-driven practices become entrenched. [[Matias2025-px]] complements this from the opposite direction, arguing that public and lived-experience involvement improves—not just legitimates—the scientific rigor of AI evaluation, with case studies (including a Reddit field experiment) directly relevant to social media research ethics and methodology.

## The Wider Information Ecosystem

Finally, two papers step back from platform-specific methods to theorize the broader informational context that motivates so much of this empirical work. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] critiques creator-intent-centered definitions of "fake news," proposing a cybernetics-inspired, propagator-focused model of how false information actually circulates through the hybrid media system. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run global analysis of trust in news—spanning nearly half a century and predating the internet—cautions against treating any perceived "golden age" of trust, or its social-media-era decline, as more than one strand in a longer, context-dependent story. Together, these two papers frame the entire methods-and-ethics agenda above: the platforms scrutinized throughout this topic are not neutral windows onto public opinion or misinformation, but historically situated, commercially governed infrastructures whose data must be interpreted—and whose access must be fought for—accordingly.
