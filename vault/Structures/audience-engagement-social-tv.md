# Audience Engagement, Virality and Social TV

## From Second Screens to Clustered Publics: An Arc of Inquiry

The papers gathered under this heading trace a decade-long trajectory in how researchers have conceptualised audience engagement online — moving from the hybrid TV/Twitter dyad of the early 2010s, through the affective micro-economy of Facebook Reactions, to the algorithmically clustered publics of the TikTok era. What binds them is a shared preoccupation with *how visibility is produced*: by whom, through which affordances, and with what consequences for the shape of the public sphere.

### The hybrid-media baseline

[[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] sets the earliest reference point. Studying Twitter as a "second screen" during Italian political talk shows in 2013, it insists that social media cannot be analytically detached from broadcast logic. The finding is deflationary: despite millions of tweets, participation was narrow, largely reactive, and dominated by opinion and requests for interaction rather than genuine agenda-building. This work stakes out a position — audiences are structurally shaped by TV's rhythms — that later papers implicitly revisit as platforms grow more autonomous from legacy media and more governed by their own algorithmic logics.

### The affective turn: Reactions as engagement currency

A cluster of Facebook-focused work then reframes engagement as an *affective* problem. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review consolidates the field around Reactions as "paralinguistic digital affordances", documenting the robust cross-study finding that anger drives engagement, especially for populist and far-right actors, and that sociopolitical content produces a broader, more negative emotional spectrum than lifestyle content. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] extends this comparatively into Scandinavian elections, refining the picture: 'Angry' reactions positively predict shares while 'Love' reactions actually *depress* sharing, and populist parties disproportionately mobilise both angry reactions and downstream diffusion. Together these papers move the discussion from *whether* audiences engage to *which emotions travel* — negative valence emerges as a structural feature of platform-mediated political virality, not a contingent quirk of any single campaign.

### Mechanisms of amplification

Where the Facebook studies infer virality from aggregate reaction/share correlations, [[Smith2025-kc]] pushes into causal mechanism. Its concept of the *tertius amplificans* — the "attention broker" whose attributed resharing accelerates tie formation among their followers — offers a micro-level explanation for how virality actually restructures networks. Using difference-in-differences on Twitter/X follow events around Jorts the Cat and J.K. Rowling, the paper identifies a local causal process behind the well-documented macro tendency toward triad transitivity. This bridges the affective-engagement literature (which explains *what* travels) with network-structural accounts (which explain *how* attention consolidates), and does so in a way that generalises to any system with attributed resharing.

### The algorithmic rupture

[[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] then reframes the entire arc. If Iannelli's tweeters, Kalsnes's reacting citizens, and Smith's amplifying brokers all inhabit "networked publics" organised around explicit interpersonal ties, TikTok — and the "TikTokification" of rival platforms — inaugurates a qualitatively different formation: *clustered publics*, statistically constructed from implicit signals like watch time, item-centric rather than people-centric, and opaque to their own participants. This is a direct challenge to the assumptions of the earlier work: follower counts weakly predict reach, brokerage-through-attribution matters less, and the affective affordances that structured Facebook engagement are subsumed into a black-boxed recommender.

### Points of convergence and tension

Read together, the papers converge on three claims and one open tension. They agree that (i) emotional intensity, particularly negative affect, is the reliable currency of virality across platforms and periods; (ii) engagement is structurally asymmetric, concentrated among narrow user segments and amplifying actors ([[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], [[Smith2025-kc]], [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]); and (iii) populist and far-right actors have been unusually effective exploiters of these dynamics ([[Anwar2024-34dba628]], [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]). The tension lies in the *ontology of the public* being described. The Facebook and Twitter literatures still presume a networked public in which brokers, parties, and affective cues do identifiable work; [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues that this framework is being superseded by an algorithmic sociality where publics are assigned rather than assembled. Whether the mechanisms identified by [[Smith2025-kc]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] survive translation into clustered-public regimes — or whether virality itself must be re-theorised for the For You era — is the live question this topic now confronts.
