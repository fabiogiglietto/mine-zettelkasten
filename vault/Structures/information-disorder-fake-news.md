# Information Disorder and Fake News Conceptualization

## Mapping the Terrain: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

The structure note for this topic traces an arc from the field's founding, largely definitional controversies through to an increasingly fractured, system-level, and now AI-inflected understanding of mediated falsehood. The starting point is the post-2016 moment, when scholars scrambled to characterize an apparently novel phenomenon. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Guess2019-ym]] collectively establish the empirical baseline: fake news is real, partisan-skewed, and concentrated among small, often older and more conservative subpopulations, but its aggregate reach is modest relative to the overall media diet ([[Allen2020-nj]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Allcott2019-gn]]). This empirical sobriety becomes a recurring counterpoint throughout the corpus — most recently restated in [[Budak2024-ef]], which explicitly argues that public discourse about online misinformation's harms has drifted from what the evidence supports, and in [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], which complicate simple exposure metrics with counterfactual and causal designs. The psychological substrate for these patterns is laid by [[Kunda1990-cg]] on motivated reasoning and consolidated by [[Pennycook2021-jq]], whose "classical reasoning" account displaces the partisan-motivation thesis that early observers like [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] had made central to their structural diagnoses of an "epistemic crisis" and "disinformation order."

## Conceptual Critique: Beyond Creator Intent and the True/False Binary

A second, more theoretically ambitious strand directly interrogates the adequacy of the "fake news" / misinformation-disinformation vocabulary itself. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] is foundational here, using second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system ([[Chadwick2013-ns]]) to relocate analytical attention from creator intent to the emergent dynamics of propagation — a move echoed in [[Freelon2020-yp]]'s careful definitional work distinguishing disinformation, misinformation, and propaganda for the field's first dedicated special issue. This critique deepens across more recent contributions: [[Frischlich2025-vn]] shows how dominant analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) each capture only part of a multilevel complex system; [[Sadler2025-vu]] argues that narrative theory and ethical appraisal must supplement or replace the true/false binary; and [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], [[Starbird2025-jj]], and [[Waight2025-al]] converge on a "narrative turn," reconceptualizing disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, emotionally cathartic stories rather than discrete false claims — a conceptual shift with direct methodological payoff in tools like [[Scalco2026-bd]]'s information-void detection and [[Goel2025-iq]]'s demonstration that mainstream, factually accurate journalism can itself be repurposed into misleading narratives. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] push this further by analyzing "fake news" itself as a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization rather than a descriptor of content quality, while [[Dierickx2026-tw]] extends the conceptual stretching into the generative-AI era by proposing "emergent facts" as a category inadequately served by evidentiary, interpretive, or institutional notions of factuality.

## Platform Architectures, Coordination, and the Hybrid System

A substantial cluster examines how platform-specific affordances structure disinformation's circulation, extending Chadwick's hybrid media system thesis into empirically grounded, often platform-comparative work. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Yang2023-cg]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], and [[Guess2021-ym]] map diffusion and exposure on Facebook; [[Bosch2024-hj]], [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], and [[Marwick2025-ov]] theorize TikTok's algorithmically clustered publics as a qualitatively distinct, "second-generation" media environment conducive to populist knowledge production; [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] extend the analysis to encrypted messaging in the Global South. [[Boyd2026-op]] offers a provocative terminological intervention paralleling the "fake news" debates, arguing "social media" itself should be renamed "parasocial media." Running alongside this is a dedicated literature on coordination and attribution: [[Starbird2019-qv]] reframes disinformation as participatory, collaborative "work"; [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Mannocci2026-kc]] formalize typologies and definitions of coordinated inauthentic behavior; and case studies such as [[Poliakoff2026-fa]], [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]], and [[Graham2025-gp]] examine specific operational infrastructures, from troll factories to "deceptive information operations" that need not even rely on false content. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] provides a unifying conceptual corrective to this cluster, replacing the overused echo-chamber/filter-bubble metaphors with a feedback-loop framework that reconciles weak average effects with real, self-reinforcing harms.

## Elites, Parties, and the Political Economy of Falsehood

A parallel body of work shifts attention from audiences to elites and institutional actors as the strategic drivers of information disorder. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] demonstrate that misinformation sharing is best explained by elite partisan strategy (specifically radical-right populism) rather than generic platform pathology, complementing [[Gaber2022-bk]]'s account of "strategic lying" as an intensification of political spin and [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience model of state propaganda. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] probe the psychological and platform-governance correlates of partisan asymmetry, while [[Berlinski2023-rj]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] examine downstream consequences for democratic trust, the latter introducing a novel "hostile misinformation effect" paralleling the classic hostile media effect. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] offers causal, experimental evidence that elite-centered exposure architecture — not individual susceptibility — drives belief inaccuracy, directly complicating the correction-and-literacy paradigm that dominated earlier work like [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]].

## Interventions, Fact-Checking, and Institutional Response

Running throughout is a normative and applied literature on countermeasures, which itself has evolved conceptually. Early debunking and correction research ([[Lewandowsky2012-vn]]) gives way to scaled, applied inoculation ([[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]]) and to critical interrogations of the fact-checking institution itself: [[Farkas2026-lr]], [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], and [[Holt2026-zq]] examine fact-checkers' precarious legitimacy amid platform withdrawal and Community Notes, while [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues for narrative- and argument-level interventions beyond claim-by-claim correction. [[Crosset2026-mq]] and [[Gillespie2022-jx]] zoom out to platform governance and regulatory architecture, showing how "moderation" increasingly operates through invisible reduction and bureaucratic transparency regimes rather than removal — a theme that dovetails with [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]]'s arguments that engagement-driven business models make misinformation-at-scale a structural feature, not a bug, of platform capitalism.

## The Generative AI Inflection Point

The most recent layer of the corpus registers generative AI as a potential rupture in the field's foundational assumptions. [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s "synthetic realities" framework, [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical audit of Grokipedia, [[DeVerna2025-dl]]'s evaluation of LLM fact-checking, [[Choi2026-bz]]'s work on deepfake authentication fatigue, and [[Crichton2026-kn]]'s tracing of a "re-generative pipeline" for far-right propaganda collectively ask whether AI intensifies existing dynamics (cost collapse, scale, provenance erosion) or demands wholly new conceptual categories — echoed in [[Suk2026-ai]]'s call for a multilevel trust framework spanning mass media, social media, and AI. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] offers a useful corrective here, finding that AI-generated visual disinformation does not uniformly outperform low-tech decontextualization, tempering alarmist extrapolation.

## Toward Comparative and Everyday Framings

Finally, several papers push the field past its WEIRD, elite-political, and crisis-driven defaults. [[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Humprecht2020-gd]], and [[Nenno2025-xa]] advance cross-national comparative frameworks of resilience and news-value expression; [[Hourigan2026-oc]], [[Wedel2026-ix]], and [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] recenter ordinary audiences' everyday, often non-political encounters with misinformation and their own vernacular definitions of "news" and "informativeness" — a corrective to top-down, institutionally anchored conceptualizations that closes the loop back to [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]]'s original call to study propagation and judgment from the ground up. Capping the topic are two reflexive, field-level interventions: [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of expert consensus on future trends and interventions, and [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s argument that misinformation research should borrow methodological and organizational maturity from climate psychology — both signaling a field increasingly aware of its own fragmentation and reaching for synthesis, coordination, and anticipatory rather than reactive theorizing.
