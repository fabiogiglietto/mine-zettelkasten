# Information Disorder & Fake News

## The Line of Argument: From Creator Intent to Circulation, Narrative, and Infrastructure

The topic's foundational move is captured in [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], which breaks decisively with the creator-centric typologies that dominated early "fake news" scholarship. Rather than asking whether a piece of content was created with deceptive intent, the paper redirects attention to *propagators*—the chain of judgments through which information cycles through a hybrid news system (Chadwick's concept, itself represented here by [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]]). This propagation-centric, second-order-cybernetic framing is the hinge on which much of the later work turns: if false information is observer-dependent and emergent at the macro level, then "fake news" cannot be studied as a fixed category of content but must be traced as a social and infrastructural *process*. [[Bennett2018-ys]] and [[Benkler2018-lw]] supply the macro-institutional complement to this move, arguing that disinformation is not a scattered set of incidents but a symptom of institutional decline and, in the American case, an asymmetric radicalization of the right-wing media ecosystem rather than a generalized technological pathology.

### Measuring the Problem: Scale, Concentration, and the Revisionist Turn

A second throughline is empirical and corrective: a sequence of large-N studies interrogates just how big the "fake news" problem actually is. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Guess2020-rr]] converge on a consistent finding—exposure to and sharing of fake news is real but heavily concentrated among small, older, more conservative subpopulations, while the average citizen's media diet is overwhelmingly composed of mainstream content. [[Allen2020-nj]] generalizes this into an ecosystem-wide argument: news itself, let alone fake news, is a vanishingly small share of Americans' total media consumption, dominated by television. [[Allcott2019-gn]] and [[Guess2021-ym]] extend this descriptive tradition using large platform datasets, while [[Eady2023-xg]] applies the same concentration logic to the Russian IRA's 2016 Twitter campaign, finding negligible attitudinal effects despite substantial reach. This revisionist thread crystallizes explicitly in [[Budak2024-ef]], which names the gap between public/scholarly alarm and the empirical record as "misunderstanding the harms of online misinformation." Later work complicates this consensus from both directions: [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that link-based methodologies have drastically underestimated *visual* misinformation, while [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argues that the dominant "direct referrer" measurement paradigm itself undercounts platforms' true causal contribution by ignoring indirect and delayed exposure pathways—estimates of platform effects turn out to be 1.5 to 15 times larger once embedded, counterfactual circulation is modeled. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] continue this measurement tradition into the 2020s, the latter using DSA-mandated transparency data to track rising TikTok prevalence, and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] examines diffusion trees at platform scale during the 2020 US election.

### The Cognitive and Psychological Register

Running parallel to the measurement literature is a psychological account of why corrections fail and falsehoods spread. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] establishes the "continued influence effect" and catalogs the narrow set of techniques (alternative causal accounts, repeated retraction) that actually work. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] supplies the landmark diffusion finding that falsehood travels farther, faster, and deeper than truth—driven by novelty and human behavior, not bots—while [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] models echo-chamber formation as a percolation process. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] mounts an influential rebuttal to politically-motivated-reasoning accounts, showing that poor truth discernment stems more from inattention and lack of reflection than partisan bias, and that the sharing/belief gap is itself a product of inattention rather than malice. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] complicates this by showing that *sharing* behavior (as opposed to belief) is strongly driven by partisan animus—out-party hatred specifically. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] closes the loop by showing mainstream media themselves are a major (if unintentional) amplifier of fake news they cover to debunk, prefiguring a recurring finding across this corpus—most recently in [[Goel2025-iq]], which shows that factually accurate mainstream articles are disproportionately co-opted to support misleading narratives, meaning domain-level "fake news" metrics systematically miss a large mode of misinformation circulation.

### The Narrative Turn

A major late development, strongly associated with this author's trajectory, is the shift from treating disinformation as discrete false claims toward treating it as *narrative*. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this explicit, arguing disinformation is best understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic stories recognizable through catchphrases rather than verifiable facts. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical apparatus (Ricoeur, Phelan) for evaluating such narratives ethically and referentially without collapsing into true/false binaries. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop this empirically for US election rumors, showing that misleadingness lives in the interaction between (often accurate) evidence and partisan framing, and that storytelling *style*—not just content—constitutes meaning; [[Prochaska2026-pr]] pushes this further into a formal "grammar" of recurring election-rumor tropes (Actor-Object-Action). [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] confirms experimentally that repeated narrative exposure across the hybrid media ecosystem increases belief. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a computational method (LLM-based narrative similarity) finally capable of tracking such narratives at scale, applied to Russian "biolabs" claims. [[Tuters2026-vo]] extends the narrative/infrastructure synthesis furthest, arguing that conspiratorial alignment around the "Great Reset" precedes narrative coherence altogether—patterned platform circulation itself produces suspicion, a "high-tech paranoid style."

### Elite Strategy, Platform Infrastructure, and Propaganda

Several papers relocate agency from individual psychology to elite and platform-level strategy. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation-sharing, but their conjunction—radical-right populism—does; [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional checks on elected officials' sharing; [[Mosca2026-yh]] theorizes "fake news" accusations themselves as rhetorical weapons of delegitimization, echoed in [[Yoo2026-ev]]'s finding that both left- and right-wing US outlets deploy "fake news" as an empty, weaponized label. [[Gaber2022-bk]] and [[Berlinski2023-rj]] examine strategic lying and unsubstantiated fraud claims (Brexit, 2019 UK election, US voter fraud) as deliberate elite tactics whose corrections often fail to land. On infrastructure, [[Keller2019-nk]] and [[Thiele2025-ol]] develop attribution frameworks for coordinated inauthentic campaigns, while [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category broader than misinformation itself, built on affordance exploitation and coordination regardless of truth-value. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] grounds this in organizational sociology via the IRA's actual labor-market structure. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Copland2025-em]] trace how propaganda (pro-Kremlin, Sky News Australia) parasitizes platform trust infrastructures and network propaganda pathways, while [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes dual-audience wartime state propaganda.

### Platform Governance, Moderation, and Its Retreat

A substantial cluster examines content moderation as a site of contested governance. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] establish foundational accounts of "reduction" and "platform paternalism" as under-examined governance modes. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as structurally built into engagement-driven business models, tracing the rise and 2021+ retreat of trust-and-safety investment—a retreat chronicled further by [[Moran2025-qn]], [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] (on Meta's end of third-party fact-checking), [[Holt2026-zq]] (on user-flagging replacing professional verification), and [[Renault2025-uh]] (testing partisan asymmetry claims against Community Notes data). [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's two-decade terminological drift from "fake news" to "borderline content," linking shrinking definitions to shrinking data access. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] documents linguistic inequity in moderation-workforce allocation, and [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] shows platforms discursively manage climate misinformation through "soft" interventions and responsibility-shifting rhetoric rather than enforceable rules. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Bennett2025-xs]] push toward explicitly democratic-backsliding framings, with the latter proposing "digital surrogate organizations" to bridge technocentric and institutionalist accounts of illiberal mobilization.

### Platform-Specific and Cross-National Extensions

The corpus increasingly diversifies across platforms and geographies. TikTok receives sustained attention: [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist evasion tactics as "everyday extremism," [[Bosch2024-hj]] examines sonic propaganda around Ukraine, [[Marwick2025-ov]] reframes ConspiracyTok as "generous epistemology" and populist knowledge production defying the white-male-conspiracist stereotype, and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds pro-immigration content actually dominates Canadian TikTok. Geographic expansion beyond the US/WEIRD core is itself thematized in [[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Humprecht2020-gd]] (cross-national resilience typology), [[Nenno2025-xa]] (news values across 24 countries), and [[Fletcher2026-lv]] (near half-century global trust trends). Brazilian and Global South cases recur: [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], and [[Ventura2025-sw]] trace electoral misinformation, trust erosion, and WhatsApp's distinct role in Brazil's 2022 election. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect" across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland.

### Radicalization, Extremism, and the Generative-AI Horizon

A distinct but convergent strand connects disinformation to radicalization processes: [[Rothut2026-wt]] synthesizes 25 years of online-radicalization scholarship into a hybrid online/offline model; Rothut2026-op (Querdenken) and [[Marwick2025-vx]] (redpilling narratives) both emphasize gradual, discursive mainstreaming over sudden conversion. Finally, generative AI reopens foundational questions about truth itself: [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category; [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorizes "synthetic realities" and an "epistemic tax"; [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLM fact-checkers are unreliable without curated retrieval; [[Triedman2025-uy]] audits Grokipedia's ideological divergence from Wikipedia; and [[Crichton2026-kn]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] trace AI's entanglement with extremist imagery and conspiratorial search practices—bringing the topic's original question (who decides what counts as false, and how does it travel?) into an era where the machinery of circulation itself increasingly produces the content in question. Methodological and conceptual housekeeping papers—[[Luhring2025-od]] on NewsGuard's reliability as infrastructure, [[Frischlich2025-vn]] on the inadequacy of viral/warfare/pollution analogies, [[Trilling2024-slc8]] on feedback loops replacing echo-chamber metaphors, [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi synthesis, and [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel/tracking study of COVID conspiracy contagion—mark the field's ongoing effort to discipline its own vocabulary even as the phenomena it tracks keep mutating.
