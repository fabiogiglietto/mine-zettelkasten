# Digital Authoritarianism & State Propaganda

## Coordination as the analytic core: methods and their limits

The empirical backbone of this collection is coordinated inauthentic behaviour (CIB) detection, and several papers push directly on the methodological state of the art. [[Pante2025-pq]] is the most self-consciously corrective of these: it re-examines earlier claims of inter-state collaboration on Twitter/X and finds that, once modern multi-signal coordination detectors and proper control datasets are applied, much of the reported inter-state cooperation does not hold up—a methodological caution that should discipline every other coordination claim in this set. Bastos2025-ol approaches the same detection problem from an unusual angle, using computer-vision analysis of profile imagery in the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset to ask whether troll farms have identifiable *visual* signatures by country of origin, extending attribution work beyond text and network features. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles a parallel measurement problem for narrative rather than account-level coordination, arguing that "narrative similarity" is conflated in existing literature with lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and building an LLM-based pipeline that outperforms text-reuse, topic-modeling, and Relatio baselines—demonstrating, in the Ukrainian "biolabs" case, that low-quality U.S. outlets shared Russian state narratives at rates invisible to naive reuse detection. Together these three papers argue that the field's tools have been under-specified relative to its claims, and that better estimators change substantive conclusions.

Applied coordination detection dominates the empirical papers on state-aligned Telegram and Twitter activity. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] maps forwarding-based coordination clusters among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] identifies over 13,000 coordinated Twitter accounts active during China's 2022 COVID-19 protests, framing the platform as dual-use infrastructure for both protest and repression; and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework into "connective counteraction," showing that pro-Navalny networks were centralized and voluminous while pro-government astroturf networks were more modular and preemptive—a structural signature of top-down mimicry rather than organic mobilization. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] pushes this line further by arguing that Serbia's SNS-linked networks represent an even subtler evolution: decentralized, mimetic CIB that evades detection precisely because it does not resemble the centralized bot farms the field's tools were built to find. Read together, these papers trace a cat-and-mouse arc: as detection methods sharpen, sanctioned regimes appear to adapt toward less structurally conspicuous coordination.

## The human infrastructure behind coordination

Where the detection literature treats trolling as a signal to be found in data traces, [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] reconstructs it as *labor*. Its OSINT analysis of 350 IRA employee CVs shows the St Petersburg troll factory as an ordinary-looking, five-tier media/PR organization, staffed by young, inexperienced graduates, integrated into Russia's broader media job market, and mobilized in response to the Crimea annexation rather than primarily the 2016 US election. This organizational lens complicates the more automated, bot-centric imaginary implicit in much CIB detection work and dovetails with [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]]'s point that not all state-aligned influence operations are technically centralized—some are outsourced, human, and market-embedded.

## Platform-specific propaganda: affordances and vernaculars

A second cluster examines how propaganda exploits the particular affordances of individual platforms rather than generic network structure. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist actors on TikTok using audio camouflage, meme infiltration, blurred imagery, emoji codes, and bait-and-switch editing to embed "everyday extremism" within the platform's participatory vernacular, evading keyword- and image-based moderation. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound itself as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda vector in TikTok's coverage of the Ukraine invasion, where #Ukraine content amassed tens of billions of views. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] both examine YouTube as a bridging infrastructure: Sky News Australia runs a dual strategy of parochial Facebook content and internationally viral YouTube content that inserts Australian material into the US right-wing propaganda pipeline, while Sweden's "alternative influence network" of far-right YouTubers fuses activist messaging with influencer microcelebrity and monetization, using YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech platforms. [[Graham2025-gp]] generalizes the underlying logic: contemporary propaganda (illustrated via #IStandWithPutin) does not evade platforms' truth-verification infrastructures so much as parasitize them—hashtags, algorithmic amplification, and verification cues become vectors of legitimacy rather than obstacles.

## From protest to radicalization: mainstreaming and self-legitimation

A third strand connects this platform-level analysis to radicalization theory, situating extremist communication within longer discursive and psychological processes rather than discrete events. [[Rothut2026-wt]], a scoping review of 382 publications, argues that the internet functions as an accelerant rather than a root cause of radicalization and that online/offline separation is analytically untenable—a framing claim that anchors the whole cluster. [[Rothut2026-or]] operationalizes this with the concept of "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," showing how Germany's Querdenken movement, though not itself extremist, became structurally embedded in far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks via shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology. [[Marwick2025-vx]] examines the same process from the participant's-eye view, showing that Far-Right "redpilling" narratives mostly describe gradual socialization through disinformation-as-evidence rather than sudden conversion, with self-styled rationality legitimizing extremist belief. [[Bailard2024-pj]] then closes the loop between online discourse and offline violence empirically, using Granger causality on Proud Boys Telegram data to show that diagnostic grievance framing and motivational solidarity framing—not explicit calls to action—predict subsequent offline violence, in a recurring online–offline mobilization cycle.

## Democratic backsliding and the platform–institution nexus

Two more reflective pieces zoom out to the macro-political stakes. [[Bennett2025-xs]] argues that technocentric disinformation research and institutionalist democratic-backsliding research have developed in isolation, and proposes "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Proud Boys, Stop the Steal) as the missing organizational link that pulled the Republican Party toward illiberalism—directly complementing [[Bailard2024-pj]]'s finding that solidarity-building online discourse feeds offline mobilization. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] presses a stronger accountability claim, holding platforms directly responsible for global democratic retreat, citing thousands of documented authoritarian-echoing actions by the second Trump administration.

## State narratives across borders and audiences

A final empirical cluster examines how authoritarian and illiberal states manage narrative for domestic versus foreign consumption. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a "dual-objective" model in which Russian wartime media pursues distinct, sometimes conflicting messaging tracks—domestic legitimacy maintenance versus foreign persuasion—that helps interpret why [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs narrative diffused unevenly into low-quality versus mainstream U.S. outlets. [[Kim2026-br]] extends the geographic and temporal scope dramatically, analyzing two decades of Korean news comments to show that suspected foreign-linked trolls rely predominantly on morally condemning rhetoric ("Condemning Korea") targeting domestic political figures across the spectrum, which garners disproportionate engagement—an important corrective to assumptions that influence operations mainly promote a foreign patron's narrative rather than corrosively amplify domestic division.

## The generative-AI frontier

The collection's newest and most forward-looking thread turns to generative AI as a propaganda vector in its own right. [[Beacken2026-zb]] surveys GenAI-enabled propaganda across six democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey), arguing against techno-deterministic assumptions of uniform AI impact and insisting that local political-economic structure shapes adoption. [[Waight2026-ts]] provides the sharpest mechanistic account of how this can happen indirectly: state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized in LLM training corpora, additional pretraining on scripted news measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence, and a 37-country audit shows commercial LLMs are systematically more favorable to low-media-freedom states when queried in their languages—effectively "laundering" state media control into ostensibly neutral AI outputs. Read against the rest of the topic, this paper suggests that the coordination-detection and platform-affordance frameworks developed for troll farms, Telegram amplification, and TikTok vernaculars will need new analogues for a media environment where the propaganda vector is not a fake account or a viral hashtag but the training data and weights of the models increasingly mediating information itself.
