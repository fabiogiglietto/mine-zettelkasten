# Social Media Platform Affordances & Engagement

## From Networked Publics to Clustered Publics: The Arc of Platform Affordance Research

The papers filed here trace a long arc from the foundational theorization of "networked publics" to a present in which algorithmic curation has arguably displaced the network itself as the organizing logic of social media. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] establishes the baseline: SNSs are defined by structural affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—that generate predictable dynamics (invisible audiences, context collapse, blurred public/private boundaries) which users must navigate. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this theoretically, showing empirically how Twitter users construct an "imagined audience" and perform strategic authenticity under conditions of context collapse. Three of the newest essays in this set—[[Boyd2026-op]], [[Marwick2026-ss]], and [[Helmond2026-ll]]—return to this foundational moment explicitly as retrospectives, arguing that the affordances they once theorized (reciprocal connection, friending, intimate disclosure) have been structurally supplanted. boyd's provocation that "social media is now parasocial media" and Marwick's elegy for LiveJournal-era reciprocity both read as bookends to the networked-publics paradigm, while Helmond reframes platformization itself as entering an AI-centered phase in which the Like button—the very signal that once indexed the social graph—becomes obsolete. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the clearest theoretical hinge for this transition, formalizing the shift from people-centric "networked publics" to item-centric "clustered publics" organized by algorithmically inferred interest rather than declared connection—a shift empirically visible in [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], where TikTok's content-centric architecture produces discourse dynamics (shared affordance "vernacular" across ideological lines) distinct from the network-centric logics of X and Facebook.

## Reactions, Emotion, and the Economy of Visibility

A second cluster examines how specific engagement features—reactions, shares, likes—operationalize visibility as an affective economy. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook Reactions research establishes that these "paralinguistic digital affordances" systematically reward negative emotional valence, especially anger, in political contexts—a finding sharpened empirically by [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]'s Scandinavian election study, which shows populist parties disproportionately eliciting Angry reactions that then predict higher sharing, while Love reactions suppress it. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an earlier, more intimate register for the same low-cost affective signal: Liking as a mechanism of "networked intimacy," collaboratively sustaining friendship rather than political mobilization. Read together, these papers suggest reactions are affordance-agnostic in design but context-dependent in function, amplifying whatever emotional logic a platform's dominant use-case rewards. [[Guess2023-ai]] complicates the virality story at the mechanism level, showing experimentally that removing Facebook reshares—the structural engine of emotional amplification—shifts content composition substantially without producing measurable attitudinal effects, a sobering empirical counterweight to assumptions that affective engagement features straightforwardly drive polarization.

## Algorithmic Feeds as Governance: Contested Causal Evidence

The algorithmic-feed literature filed here is marked by a genuine empirical tension about causal effects, and the papers productively argue with one another. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]] set an early baseline: algorithmic ranking modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure but less than individual choice does, and the internet's effects on segregation are "nuanced and modest." [[Guess2023-ur]], part of the Facebook/Meta 2020 election collaboration, extends this with a true experimental manipulation (chronological vs. algorithmic feed), finding large effects on engagement and content mix but no detectable downstream political effects—a null result that becomes the field's reference point. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] directly contests this null finding with a comparable design on X, showing real attitudinal shifts, and crucially proposes a *mechanism*—algorithmically induced following of conservative accounts, whose effects persist after the algorithm is switched off—that reconciles the discrepancy: Meta's null result may reflect measurement of symmetric on/off switching rather than this asymmetric persistence dynamic. [[Brady2026-ln]] pushes the reconciliation further, reframing the causal pathway through *social norm misperception* rather than direct attitude change, and demonstrating that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content measurably, with a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm able to mitigate this without sacrificing engagement—moving the literature from diagnosis toward algorithmic intervention design. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] adds an institutional-political layer, showing that under Musk's ownership the X algorithm's apparent right-wing bias is substantially explained by behavioral proxies (agitation, proximity to the platform owner) rather than ideology per se—implicating platform governance and owner-level incentives as co-determinants of algorithmic visibility. [[Brown2026-br]]'s YouTube audit further disentangles supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user choice, finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization effect—reinforcing the field's growing methodological sophistication in separating what algorithms do from what users do. [[McNally2025-dn]] contributes a meso-level complement, showing through longitudinal time-series methods that Facebook's News Feed, far from an unknowable "black box," exhibits detectable, differentiated treatment of hard news versus soft content—evidence marshaled explicitly in support of DSA-style algorithmic auditing.

## Curation, Fragmentation, and the News Ecosystem

A related strand examines how curation—whether social (friend sharing), algorithmic, or multi-platform—reshapes news diversity and partisan exposure. [[Messing2014-jc]] shows social endorsement cues override partisan source cues in news selection, suggesting an early optimistic reading of socialized curation that [[Green2025-ap]] substantially revises: partisan "curation bubbles" operate at the *story* level rather than the *domain* level, meaning moderate-seeming sources can harbor highly polarized sharing patterns invisible to standard measures. [[Mosleh2024-op]] extends the critique of single-platform generalization, documenting divergent engagement with partisan/low-quality news across seven platforms, a concern echoed methodologically in [[Lukito2026-il]]'s study of cross-platformization among right-leaning outlets moving between Twitter and Truth Social, and in [[Kakavand2026-kt]]'s demonstration that far-right networks in Germany take qualitatively different structural shapes on each of five platforms—evidence that affordances are relational and emergent rather than fixed platform properties. [[Wan2026-ai]] brings an individual-difference angle to algorithmic feeds specifically, examining how political efficacy conditions selection behavior within Google Discover's fully algorithmic environment, tying macro-level curation debates back to psychological variation at the user level.

## Second Screens, Hybrid Media, and the Social-TV Lineage

The social-TV tradition that anchors this topic's older half is represented chiefly by [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], which examines Twitter as a second screen during Italian political talk shows and finds that despite enormous tweet volumes, genuine audience agenda-setting was minimal and TV logic continued to dominate hybrid media dynamics. This paper's concern with the asymmetry between platform affordance (the *possibility* of participation) and its actual uptake anticipates the newer literature's concern with algorithmic gatekeeping: just as talk-show producers and politicians largely failed to activate connected audiences, contemporary platforms' algorithmic systems determine whose voice is amplified regardless of nominal openness. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extends the hybrid-platform-adaptation logic into the cross-platform populist-campaigning context, showing how the Sweden Democrats tailored securitizing visual rhetoric differently across Facebook, X, and TikTok—persuasive argumentation on legacy platforms versus affective, memetic virality on TikTok—demonstrating that affordance-sensitivity is now a core strategic competency for political communicators, not an incidental effect.

## Platform-Specific Vernaculars: TikTok, Moderation, and Ambient Ideology

A distinct cluster interrogates TikTok and YouTube's specific technical affordances as enabling new modes of visibility that elude conventional moderation. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-examined, platform-specific propaganda vector in TikTok's Ukraine disinformation ecosystem; [[Cabbuag2024-me]] shows how TikTok's affordances amplify culturally specific incivility ("dogshows") among Filipino Gen Z influencers; and [[Rieder2026-pp]] demonstrates on YouTube that recommendation-driven circulation structurally outpaces moderation, sustaining an "ambient ideology" around a deplatformed figure (Andrew Tate) through diffuse, decentralized remix and reaction content. These studies collectively suggest that affordances designed for engagement (autoplay, remixability, sound-driven virality) create moderation blind spots that content-level removal cannot address, a governance problem [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s review of "algorithm literacy" frames as partly a user-side knowledge deficit and partly a structural opacity problem.

## Labor, Identity, and Platform Culture Under Algorithmic Pressure

Finally, several papers examine how individual creators and ordinary users internalize and adapt to platform affordances as conditions of labor and self-presentation. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers deploying niche content (secondhand fashion) as a survival strategy against algorithmic demands for constant production, blending ethical performance with reputational labor. [[Wedel2026-ix]] finds that young audiences extend journalistic norms of informativeness to non-journalistic actors based on platform-specific selection logics (self-selected on YouTube, actor-mediated on Instagram, algorithmic on TikTok). [[Wang2026-ub]]'s study of the failed academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration shows how affordances around discoverability, server specialization, and federated interaction—rather than ideological commitment alone—determine whether alternative platform ecosystems can sustain engagement. Methodologically, [[Bruns2025-fz]] and [[Fan2026-af]] point toward the field's next instruments: multimodal "practice mapping" and fine-grained temporal sequence analysis of digital trace data, both aimed at capturing engagement dynamics that aggregate, cross-sectional measures obscure. [[Manovich2026-ih]]'s analysis of generative AI as a medium with its own cognitive affordances gestures toward where this inquiry is headed next: platforms increasingly mediated not just by algorithmic curation of existing content but by AI systems that generate the content itself, a frontier this topic's papers are only beginning to map.
