# Sociocybernetics & Digital Society Theory

## Systems-theoretic foundations: observing the observers

The theoretical backbone of this cluster is the classical sociocybernetic move of treating social phenomena as self-referential, observer-dependent systems rather than stable objects awaiting transparent description. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] makes this move explicit, drawing on von Foerster's and Bateson's second-order cybernetics to argue that "fake news" cannot be defined by creator intent alone: truth is "a difference that makes a difference" constituted anew at each point of propagation, and what looks like a single phenomenon is actually an emergent, multi-level cascade of micro-judgments. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] extends the Luhmannian strand directly into the generative-AI moment, showing empirically that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work — the system performs communication without understanding it, which forces a reframing of "intelligence" from a cognitive to a communicative-systems register. Both papers insist that the proper unit of analysis is not the sender or the artifact but the communicative operation itself, observed recursively.

[[Castells2009-sle8]] supplies the macro-structural counterpart to this operational focus: the "network society" thesis that digital networks reorganize economy, space, time, and power into a logic of inclusion/exclusion that eludes nation-state control. Where Castells offers a structural account of networked informationalism as an epochal social form, [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] attempts to operationalize a related insight at the infrastructural level, arguing that Internet architecture itself should be redesigned around social-capital properties (trust, reciprocity, bridging) rather than purely technical metrics — an early, still-technocentric attempt to fold sociological concepts (Bourdieu, Coleman, Putnam) into system design that anticipates later platform-studies concerns. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] closes this foundational arc by explicitly invoking cybernetics (Ashby) and complexity theory to dissolve the overused "echo chamber"/"filter bubble" metaphors into a typology of reinforcing and self-regulating feedback loops — formalizing, in effect, the very observational logic that Giglietto and Ptz apply informally, and demonstrating why linear, acyclic causal models are inadequate to digital social dynamics.

## The unraveling of the platform/social-media paradigm

A second, tightly interlinked group of papers — mostly drawn from a 2026 anniversary moment in platform/media studies — applies this systems-theoretic sensibility to diagnose the collapse of "social media" as a coherent object of observation. [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] converge on the same empirical signal (declining posting, rising scrolling, algorithmic rather than social-graph circulation) but propose different second-order vocabularies for it: boyd's "parasocial media" names the shift from reciprocal sociality to one-sided attention to influencers, while Törnberg's "post-social media" dissolves the unifying concept altogether into three coexisting formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. Both are conceptual self-corrections by scholars who helped coin the terms now being retired, exemplifying the reflexive, self-observing character of a field recognizing that its own descriptions have become inadequate to their object — itself a sociocybernetic gesture.

[[Helmond2026-ll]] supplies the infrastructural correlate: "platformization," her own earlier coinage, is reworked as the field migrates from social-graph capture toward AI-centered "transcoding," with Big Tech repositioning as "Big AI." [[Chancellor2026-sld9]] performs an analogous conceptual retheorization at a more meso level, arguing that "context collapse" has become stretched past usefulness and must be rebuilt as a structural grammar of user/platform/data dimensions to remain adequate to algorithmically curated, consumption-oriented platforms. [[Rieder2020-sl35]] anticipates and partly explains why such concepts keep breaking: platforms are not stable, inspectable "black boxes" but distributed, continuously changing sociotechnical processes, so regulatory and scholarly accountability must shift from transparency to "observability" — a term that, read sociocybernetically, names precisely the problem of how a second-order observer can track a system whose operations and boundaries are themselves constantly reconstituted. Together these five papers trace a single arc: the social-media paradigm's constitutive concepts (sociality, context, platform, transparency) are each shown to have decoupled from the systems they once described, demanding new observational vocabularies rather than patched-up old ones.

## Toward an AI society: new objects for sociocybernetic inquiry

A final set of papers extends this reflexive diagnostic work to generative and agentic AI as an emerging social system in its own right. [[Tornberg2026-im]] theorizes a tendential shift "from the platform society to the AI society," arguing that platform-era mechanisms of monopoly and behavioral capture are being supplanted by "cognitive lock-in" and that the predictive logic of platforms is giving way to a generative logic in which publics are simulated rather than sampled — a direct extension of the "post-social" and "platformization" diagnoses above into the domain of epistemic and political economy. [[Ng2026-og]] pushes further toward formal modeling, proposing that multi-agent AI systems be treated as genuine social systems (a triple of information exchange, influence dynamics, and network structure) requiring social-theoretic — rather than purely engineering — structural priors; its empirical demonstration that AI-agent populations exhibit strategic heterogeneity, network-dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability effectively operationalizes, in a machine-agent population, the same emergent, self-reinforcing dynamics that Trilling's feedback-loop typology and Giglietto's cascade model describe for human communication. [[Hepp2026-oi]] rounds out this arc ethnographically, showing that the social imaginaries driving AI's development (Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring") are themselves figurational and self-referential systems of meaning-making, entangled with loud public discourse in a circular, mutually amplifying relationship.

Read together, these thirteen papers stage sociocybernetics' characteristic double movement: concepts built to observe social systems must themselves be treated as observations, subject to obsolescence and revision as the systems they describe mutate — from network society to platform society to parasocial and post-social formations to an incipient AI society — while the formal tools of second-order cybernetics, feedback-loop analysis, and multi-agent systems theory offer a continuous methodological thread for tracking that mutation without presuming any final, stable description of "the digital social."
