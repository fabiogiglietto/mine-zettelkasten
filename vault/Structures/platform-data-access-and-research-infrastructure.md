# Platform Data Access & Research Infrastructure

## The Regulatory Turn: Article 40 as a Legal Right to Data

The pivot point for this entire literature is Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act, which for the first time makes researcher access to platform data a binding legal entitlement rather than a matter of platform discretion. [[Ohme2026-nv]] frames this as a genuine change in the "practical landscape" of platform research, while [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] insists that the friction accompanying implementation — platform stalling, opaque procedures, inconsistent timelines — should be read not as evidence of regulatory failure but as proof that the DSA is finally constraining an entrenched informational asymmetry. [[Pierri2025-hm]], written from inside an ERC-DG CONNECT pilot, corroborates this optimism-with-caveats view empirically: misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries between platforms and academics, and a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers all threaten to blunt Article 40's promise, and the authors flag large language models as an emerging regulatory blind spot. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] historicizes this moment as the latest phase in a longer arc — from "Wild West" API openness, through a brief "Golden Age" of sanctioned academic access, to commercial paywalls — arguing that the DSA represents a shift from platform philanthropy to regulatory obligation, but one still undermined by uneven compliance (the Commission's own findings that TikTok and Meta breached Article 40.12) and by the DSA's EU-only reach, which produces a global "data abyss." [[Philipp2026-tl]] narrows this general picture to election research specifically, cataloguing the new APIs and portals platforms have stood up under DSA pressure and showing how uneven eligibility rules and API design choices determine what kinds of campaign research remain feasible. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] rounds out this cluster as a broader survey of the API/tool landscape in the DSA era.

A more granular strand interrogates what "access" actually means once granted. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that *data quality* — as distinct from mere access — was almost entirely absent from official DSA text and its Delegated Regulation, surfacing only through sustained advocacy by academic and NGO stakeholders in the Commission's consultation process (with Meta notably inverting the argument to claim low platform data quality makes access futile). This paper's finding that data quality is a *contested, political* construct rather than a neutral methodological one sets up a throughline for the rest of the topic: every subsequent audit of platform APIs is implicitly a struggle over what counts as adequate, trustworthy data. [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers a wider theoretical lens on this regulatory moment, arguing that the DSA belongs to a family of "flow optimisation" regimes (alongside the UK Online Safety Act) that govern platforms through bureaucratic transparency and auditing rather than direct content control — a framing that helps explain why access battles under Article 40 are fought in procedural, administrative terms rather than substantive ones.

## The Post-API Age: A History of Contingent Access

Behind the current regulatory moment lies a longer history of platform-controlled data access repeatedly opened and foreclosed. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history across six platforms, arguing that access has become "permanently contingent" on platform decisions shaped by scandal, leadership change, and commercial strategy — a diagnosis borne out concretely by Bastos2025-ya's retrospective on Twitter's suite of APIs (REST, Search, Streaming, Academic, Compliance) as the infrastructural backbone of an entire "Twitter studies" subfield, and by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]]'s bibliometric eulogy documenting a 25% annual growth rate in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline in 2024 after Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. Both papers read the API's closure not as a technical footnote but as an exercise of platform power with measurable consequences for what society can learn about itself — a claim [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] uses explicitly to argue for legislative mandates like the DSA and the (US) Platform Accountability and Transparency Act.

## CrowdTangle, Clean Rooms, and the Politics of Sanctioned Access

CrowdTangle's 2024 shutdown functions across this corpus as the paradigmatic case of platform-permission-based access collapsing at the worst possible moment (during the run-up to the 2024 US election). [[Cullen2026-cb]] draws on interviews with CrowdTangle users to develop a "seeing like an API" framework, arguing that the tool's technical design, governance rules, and knowledge networks jointly constrained not just what researchers could retrieve but what they could ask and conclude — APIs as active epistemic mediators, not neutral pipes. [[Bruns2026-pn]] follows CrowdTangle's replacement, the Meta Content Library, into its "clean room" successor model, showing how Web-disconnected, code-only analysis environments privilege quantitative/programming-skilled researchers, exclude qualitative and cross-platform work, and mandate data deletion cycles that make longitudinal research nearly impossible — a shift the authors read as widening inequities between well-resourced Global North institutions and everyone else, even as it nominally satisfies DSA Article 40 obligations. [[Lukito2026-nb]] generalizes this dynamic across the wider tool ecosystem, coding 72 access programs along axes of price, proficiency, and permission, and showing that Meta alone has cycled through four distinct access regimes (Social Science One, CrowdTangle, FORT, Meta Content Library) — direct evidence for their claim that platform-led access is inherently "independence by permission" and structurally unreliable as a foundation for cumulative scholarship.

## The URL Shares Dataset and Its Thresholds

Meta's URL Shares Dataset, born from the Social Science One collaboration, receives sustained methodological scrutiny in its own right. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] demonstrates that the dataset's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold is not a benign privacy safeguard but an artifact-generating mechanism: near-identical monthly trajectories of shares and comments across 46 wildly different countries point to a platform-wide force — most plausibly changes to Feed ranking and "Meaningful Social Interactions" optimization — that systematically alters which URLs cross the threshold, confounding any cross-country or longitudinal comparison that fails to account for it. This finding is a cautionary methodological complement to [[Holt2026-zq]], which uses the same dataset to study what Facebook users flag as "False News" in the post-fact-checking era, finding that user reports skew toward polarized political content and contextualizing coverage rather than outright falsehoods — a substantive finding whose validity depends on exactly the kind of threshold-artifact awareness [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] insists on.

## Auditing the New APIs: Compliance Without Adequacy

A cluster of papers empirically tests whether DSA-mandated researcher APIs deliver on their transparency promise, and largely finds them wanting. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] shows TikTok's Research API, expanded in 2023 to satisfy DSA obligations, riddled with unexplained limitations and inconsistencies that undermine systemic-risk research. [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts a six-month audit of YouTube's Data API search endpoint and finds it "forgetful by design" — a severe temporal decay in findable videos after 20–60 days, inconsistent results across identical repeated queries, and large divergences between ranking parameters — concluding the tool functions more like a recommendation engine than an information-retrieval system and is inadequate for DSA-related systemic risk research. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content-moderation practices around the 2024 EU elections, documenting inconsistent application of "News Funding Notice" labels (absent in seven EU languages) and vague removal statements, and calls for reconceiving transparency as "observability" — attention to the infrastructural conditions that make platform knowledge production possible at all. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency-reporting data itself to expose disparities in human moderation workforce allocation across languages, finding that Global South languages (Arabic, Spanish, Portuguese) receive as little as 7.5% of English's moderator allocation on Twitter/X, and that DSA reporting requirements remain too coarse to allow independent normalization by content volume. Together these audits suggest that DSA compliance is frequently gestural: platforms satisfy the letter of transparency mandates while the resulting tools remain unfit for the systemic-risk research the regulation was designed to enable.

## Industry Influence and the Limits of Platform-Mediated Science

A parallel line of argument steps back from specific tools to ask whether platform-dependent research can ever be trusted as independent evidence. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] both draw explicit analogies to tobacco, pharmaceutical, and fossil-fuel industry science, cataloguing mechanisms — selective funding, suppressed internal research, performative collaboration, restricted data access — through which tech companies shape the evidence base used for their own regulation. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] supplies the empirical backbone for this argument, quantifying industry ties across high-profile social media research in Science, Nature, and PNAS: roughly half of papers carry disclosable industry ties that mostly go undisclosed, ties are concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, and industry-tied work is skewed toward user-focused topics (like misinformation sharing) at the expense of platform-dynamics research — yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the literature once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined. [[Munger2025-cz]] applies this skepticism to the single most prominent platform-academic collaboration, the Meta 2020 Election Study, arguing that even its methodologically ambitious field experiments suffer from low "temporal validity" (platforms change too fast for one-off findings to generalize) and inconsistent "poetic validity" (core concepts like "echo chamber" are operationalized differently across papers), concluding that empirical social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms and that proactive regulatory disclosure mandates are therefore necessary. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a partial methodological response, situating Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension-plus-LLM "platform-independent" experimental design as a middle path between lab control and platform-collaboration ecological validity — precisely the kind of adaptable, non-platform-dependent tool that becomes necessary once API access and collaborative goodwill can no longer be assumed.

## Infrastructure, Equity, and Alternative Methods

A final set of contributions treats research infrastructure itself as the object of inquiry, arguing that the field needs durable, reusable observability rather than one-off workarounds. [[Schulte2026-df]] uses the 2025 German federal election to show that multi-platform electoral research is bottlenecked less by a shortage of studies than by the absence of standardized, reusable infrastructure for cross-platform, cross-temporal comparison — a diagnosis that dovetails with [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats tools, which build genuinely random samples of YouTube and TikTok content to correct for the popularity-biased, denominator-blind samples that opportunistic API queries typically produce. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] represents an earlier-generation adaptive response to tightening API access, repurposing Facebook's advertising infrastructure (Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion) to recruit hard-to-reach survey populations — a reminder that platform-independent innovation predates the DSA and will likely continue regardless of its success. Read together with [[Lukito2026-nb]]'s "price, proficiency, or permission" framework, this cluster makes the topic's central normative argument explicit: sustainable, equitable platform research requires a plurality of access modes — regulatory mandate, independent tooling, and multi-institutional archiving — because reliance on any single platform-controlled channel, however well-regulated, reproduces the precarity that has characterized the entire post-API era.
