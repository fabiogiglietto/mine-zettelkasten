# Platform Data Access and Research Infrastructure

## The closing of the open API era

The historical throughline for this collection is a single, well-documented arc: platforms went from open data spigots to guarded walls. [[Freelon2024-sc]] supplies the master periodization — API prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation — and shows how Meta's 2018 post-Cambridge Analytica lockdown, CrowdTangle's rise and 2024 shutdown, the Meta Content Library's "clean room," and X's pay-to-play pricing are all instances of a general pattern rather than isolated platform decisions. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] retells this story specifically for Twitter/X — Wild West, Golden Age of the Academic API, post-Musk paywall — and generalizes it across Meta, Reddit, and TikTok, arguing that "data philanthropy" and voluntary access are structurally fragile because they can be dismantled by a single change in ownership or strategy. Bastos2025-ya eulogizes the scholarly subfield ("Twitter studies") this openness enabled, and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] turns eulogy into bibliometrics: 33,306 Twitter-based studies, 25% median annual growth through 2022, then stagnation and a 13% decline by 2024 after the $42,000/month Enterprise API. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this trajectory across the whole of social-media-based social science, showing that reliance on Twitter/X and Facebook is disproportionate to their public usage and that empirical output plateaued and declined in 2022–2024 precisely as API restrictions bit — a macro-level confirmation of what the platform-specific papers document in detail.

## Quantifying the loss, theorizing the mediation

Several papers move from documenting decline to theorizing what exactly was lost. [[Cullen2026-cb]] interviews CrowdTangle users to develop a "seeing like an API" framework: the tool's technical design, governance rules, and knowledge networks did not just constrain data access but actively shaped what could be seen, asked, and concluded — a reminder that even in the "Golden Age," access was never neutral. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to CrowdTangle's successor, the Meta Content Library, arguing that the "clean room" model — Web-disconnected, code-only, monthly data deletion, no cross-platform combination — systematically excludes qualitative and mixed-methods researchers, smaller institutions, and Majority World scholars, even as it claims to satisfy privacy and transparency obligations. [[Lukito2026-nb]] formalizes the resulting inequities into a "price, proficiency, or permission" typology across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent (6 of 72 tools) and access infrastructure overwhelmingly WEIRD-centric — a structural diagnosis that echoes both Cullen's and Bruns's qualitative findings.

## Industry capture and the limits of platform-mediated collaboration

A parallel strand asks not just whether access exists but whether platform-brokered research can ever be independent. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] both draw the tobacco/pharma/food analogy explicitly, cataloguing mechanisms — restricted access, selective funding, design bias, performative collaboration — by which platforms shape the evidence base used for their own regulation. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] operationalizes this empirically: roughly half of high-profile social media papers in Science/Nature/PNAS have disclosable industry ties (mostly undisclosed), industry-tied work is cited twice as often, and aggregating author, editor, and reviewer ties yields an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Munger2025-cz]] takes the Meta2020 election partnership — arguably the high-water mark of platform-academic collaboration — as a case study in why even well-resourced, well-intentioned partnerships fail: rapid platform change undermines temporal validity, inconsistent operationalization of concepts like "echo chamber" undermines "poetic validity," and strict multiple-comparison corrections biased the design toward the null findings that happen to suit Meta. Together these pieces argue that the problem of access cannot be separated from the problem of who controls it.

## The DSA as regulatory answer — and its friction

The Digital Services Act's Article 40 is the collection's central regulatory hope, and papers here document both its promise and its very uneven delivery. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frames the current turbulence — platform resistance, procedural delay, geopolitical contestation — not as regulatory failure but as evidence the regulation is finally biting on real asymmetries of power, urging a "marathon" posture of implementation rather than redesign. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] assess what Article 40 concretely changes in practice, the latter specifically for election research, finding substantial platform-to-platform variation in API design and eligibility that reproduces old inequities in new legal clothing. [[Pierri2025-hm]], drawing on an 18-month ERC pilot with DG-CONNECT, catalogues implementation bottlenecks — resource asymmetries, a circular problem where funding requires data access and data access requires funding, and a regulatory blind spot around LLMs — while [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality," barely mentioned in the DSA's official text, became a contested object in stakeholder consultations, with Meta strategically inverting researchers' arguments to claim low data quality makes access futile. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] both treat the post-DSA landscape as fundamentally an infrastructure problem: the latter's multi-platform German election study argues explicitly that platform observability should be built as reusable research infrastructure rather than re-invented per study. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes the normative implication furthest, arguing the DSA era demands moving beyond individual-level causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure.

## Clean rooms, walled gardens, and what gets excluded

Several papers zoom in on the concrete architecture of the "post-post-API" access regimes DSA compliance has produced. [[Bruns2026-pn]] is the fullest account of the clean-room model's exclusions; [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how "data quality" language was fought over precisely because it determines what independent audits of these environments can claim. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] demonstrates what DSA-mandated transparency data can reveal even under these constraints — stark disparities in human moderator allocation by language, with Twitter/X allocating Global South languages as little as 7.5% of English's per-content moderator ratio — while also noting that platforms withhold the content-volume denominators needed to properly normalize such comparisons, itself a data-access limitation. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] apply audit methodology directly to YouTube: Jurg's browser-scraping-plus-API study of the 2024 EU election finds inconsistent application of "News Funding Notice" labels and vague removal communications, explicitly calling for expanded YouTube Research Program access to make such audits scalable; Rieder's systematic audit of the YouTube Data API's search endpoint finds severe "forgetfulness" — a temporal decay curve that makes videos older than ~60 days largely unfindable even when still public — concluding the API is inadequate for DSA systemic-risk research. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] delivers a parallel verdict on TikTok's expanded Research API, finding persistent limitations that undercut its DSA-compliance rationale. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds constructively to this same diagnosis by building TubeStats and TokStats, open tools that exploit platform ID-indexing schemes to generate genuinely random samples independent of restrictive research APIs, explicitly framed as a public-interest alternative to popularity-biased convenience samples.

## Building around the walls: independent and hybrid methods

A cluster of papers develops methodology that routes around platform gatekeeping rather than negotiating it. [[Allen2025-ot]] frames Piccardi et al.'s LLM-based browser-extension reranking of real X feeds as an emblematic "platform-independent" experimental design, positioned against the backdrop of closed APIs and rare platform collaborations (explicitly contrasted with the Meta2020/FIES model Munger critiques). [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that even scraping-based alternatives carry hidden costs: ex-situ web scraping introduces ~33.8% content disparity relative to in-situ browser capture, systematically biased by paywalls and login walls, a caution for any researcher treating scraped HTML as ground truth. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] demonstrates an older but still relevant workaround — Facebook's ad platform itself as a recruitment instrument for hard-to-reach populations — illustrating that commercial platform infrastructure can be repurposed for research even absent formal data-sharing programs. [[Yang2025-iv]], [[Thiele2025-ol]], and [[Pante2025-pq]] address a more specific infrastructural asymmetry: ground-truth data for studying coordinated manipulation is heavily skewed toward Twitter's periodic releases, with Meta and Reddit providing far less, which shapes both what detection methods can be validated (Yang's speed/frequency-based Facebook coordination detector) and what can be attributed or debunked (Thiele's rational-choice attribution model; Pante's re-examination of inter-state coordination claims using modern indicators and control datasets). [[Bruns2025-fz]] contributes a general-purpose methodological advance — vector-embedding "practice mapping" — aimed at overcoming the "hairball" problem in multimodal network visualization regardless of data source.

## Downstream consequences: what constrained access does to substantive findings

Finally, several papers show how access limitations and dataset artifacts propagate into substantive conclusions about misinformation and public opinion. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] is foundational here: the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical monthly trajectories across 46 disparate countries, revealing that a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely Feed/MSI ranking changes) can masquerade as a substantive COVID-era social signal — a methodological warning taken up directly by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], which extends Guess et al.'s Facebook news-quality analysis to Germany, France, and Italy using the same dataset while explicitly flagging its US-skew and threshold artifacts as reasons to avoid naive US–EU comparison. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] uses billion-post Facebook diffusion trees to characterize misinformation's distinctive virality and reach during the 2020 US election, a scale of analysis only possible through privileged platform-mediated access of exactly the kind now foreclosed. [[McNally2025-dn]] shows CrowdTangle-era data could still support rigorous causal inference about algorithmic treatment of news — detecting lagged, differential effects on hard news via Granger causality — explicitly arguing this capacity for auditing "black box" feeds should be preserved and extended under DSA Article 40(4), a capacity now jeopardized by CrowdTangle's closure. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the source-trustworthiness infrastructure underlying much of this research, finding ratings have stabilized since 2022 but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings — a reminder that research infrastructure problems extend beyond platform APIs to the auxiliary databases used to interpret platform data. [[Scalco2026-bd]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] illustrate what is at stake substantively: the former operationalizes "information voids" using longitudinal Facebook/Twitter/Wikipedia/Google Trends data to link demand-supply imbalances to misinformation prevalence, while the latter warns that the production-consumption gap on social media distorts inferences about public opinion for researchers and citizens alike — both arguments that depend on exactly the kind of longitudinal, cross-platform access this whole literature shows is disappearing.
