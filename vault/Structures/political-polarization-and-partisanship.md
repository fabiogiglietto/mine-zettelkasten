# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The anchor case: affective dynamics inside a hyperpartisan network

The empirical center of gravity for this topic is [[Marino2026-slef]], which tracks emotional engagement inside a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network across the 2021–2023 crisis years. Its central finding disrupts the intuitive picture of hyperpartisan communities as stable, impermeable echo chambers: emotional and behavioral engagement patterns were highly volatile, particularly around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt, and were shaped jointly by *which* actor was mentioned and *when*, rather than by fixed ideological valence. This dovetails with [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which shows Italian populist-aligned news sources are more "insular" on Twitter and that insularity converts into different Facebook engagement signatures—amplification (sharing) for closed communities versus contestation (commenting) for cross-partisan sources—and that the Five Star Movement's supporters strategically deploy both. Together these two studies suggest that partisan communities are not simply bounded containers of belief but dynamic fields whose affective temperature and structure of engagement (love vs. anger, share vs. comment) shift with political shocks and with deliberate incursions from outside the community, a possibility Marino explicitly raises when noting apparent "brigading" by opposing partisans as a new form of politically motivated cross-cutting engagement.

The Brazilian context recurs as a natural laboratory for polarization dynamics beyond this Facebook case. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] extend the picture to the informational and attitudinal consequences of Bolsonaro-era misinformation, showing that belief in electoral fraud claims independently erodes both political tolerance and trust in election results, with WhatsApp functioning as a distinct pathway that either stabilizes or corrodes trust depending on network composition (strong ties vs. strangers). [[Rodarte2026-dk]] adds an elite-communication layer, showing how Bolsonaro-aligned and opposition parliamentarians pursued epistemic authority through opposing strategies—derivative reporting that borrows journalistic credibility versus direct claims to state authority that bypass the press entirely—during the Manaus health crisis. [[Ventura2026-yc]] closes this arc with the 2024 X ban, demonstrating that state intervention in a polarized platform ecosystem produced a durable "sorting ratchet": conservative users disproportionately evaded the ban while liberal users exited, permanently shifting the platform's political center of gravity rightward. Read together, these Brazilian studies chart a coherent story: affective volatility within partisan networks, weaponized epistemic claims by elites, misinformation belief eroding tolerance and trust, and platform governance interventions that—rather than neutrally moderating conflict—themselves become instruments of partisan sorting.

## Rethinking the echo chamber: from bubbles to feedback loops and curation

A parallel strand interrogates the master metaphor underlying most polarization research. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the foundational corrective, showing that Facebook friend networks are homophilous but not hermetically sealed, and that individual click behavior—not the News Feed algorithm—is the primary filter suppressing cross-cutting exposure. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies argues that much of the scholarly disagreement about whether echo chambers "exist" is an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than a real empirical contradiction. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] proposes replacing the bubble metaphor altogether with a feedback-loop framework, showing why reinforcing dynamics rarely run away to full radicalization (negative feedback from platform design and user correction typically bounds them), while still allowing for real, harmful self-reinforcing spirals in specific media ecosystems (as in Benkler et al.'s asymmetric left/right findings). [[Dehghan2026-sy]] pushes this further with a "sedimentation" account of polarization on political Reddit: cross-ideological overlap in users, sources, and even discursive resonance is minimal, undermining the idea of r/politics as a cross-cutting town square, and polarization is better understood as a precondition enacted through platform design, moderation, and discourse jointly rather than a discourse effect alone. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates measurement further by showing that domain-level partisan-audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity—moderate-scoring outlets like the Wall Street Journal contain individual stories with starkly divergent partisan audiences—so "curation bubbles" form at the level of the shared story, not the source. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] extend this skepticism cross-nationally: audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media in Europe is generally low but context-dependent, shaped by how institutionalized far-right access to mainstream politics is in a given media system, showing that "polarization" is not a uniform structural fact but a locally contingent outcome of media-system and platform architecture.

## Cross-partisan news interaction and the politics of measurement

Several papers turn explicitly to how cross-partisan news exposure and sharing should be measured, a methodological throughline running underneath the topic's substantive claims. [[Eady2025-vm]] introduces a joint ideal-point model estimating ideology for media, politicians, and ordinary users from shared URLs alone, finding that the most ideologically extreme legislators dominate the shared news environment and that electoral non-competitiveness predicts more polarized sharing—implicating gerrymandering as an amplifier of online polarization. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques prior work for treating partisan leaning as unidimensional and outlet-level only, proposing a multi-level "political cartography" spanning story, outlet, and content. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis of 504 operationalizations shows that measurement choices for both media slant and polarization dramatically affect coefficient magnitude (though rarely direction), with audience-based slant scores inflating apparent effects relative to content-based ones—a sobering meta-lesson for the entire literature. [[Mosleh2024-op]] extends the empirical base beyond the Twitter/Facebook duopoly, documenting genuinely divergent engagement patterns with partisan and low-quality news across seven platforms, reinforcing calls (echoed in [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]) for a less US-centric, less platform-narrow evidence base for polarization research generally.

## Algorithms, ad markets, and the architecture of amplification

A cluster of field experiments interrogates whether platform *design*—rather than user choice or content—causally drives polarization. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] is the pivotal contribution: switching users from a chronological to an algorithmic "For You" feed on X shifted political attitudes rightward and increased engagement, with the crucial mechanism being that algorithmic exposure changes *which accounts users follow*, producing effects that persist even after the algorithm is switched off—reconciling the null results of Meta's 2020 collaborative studies (echoed in [[Allcott2025-jb]], which found no detectable attitudinal effects from six weeks of political-ad removal) with the intuition that algorithms matter. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment complements this by showing engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized, emotional (IME) content and distort perceived social *norms* about political hostility—more than they distort attitudes directly—while a custom "diversified extremity" algorithm can dampen this without sacrificing platform enjoyment. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Smith2025-kc]] round out this cluster: ad-delivery algorithms shape political-ad reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigners, while the "attention broker" mechanism shows how amplification by high-degree accounts causally accelerates network closure around amplified content, offering a micro-causal account of how attention—rather than deliberate persuasion—restructures partisan network topology.

## Coordinated inauthentic behavior, bots, and manufactured consensus

Where the previous cluster treats amplification as an emergent property of platform architecture, another set of studies treats it as a *strategic* accomplishment by political actors and automated agents. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework to authoritarian "connective counteraction," showing pro-Navalny protest networks were centralized around key brokers while pro-government astroturf networks were more modular and preemptive. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a similarly decentralized, mimicry-based form of coordinated inauthentic behavior around Serbia's SNS party that evades detection precisely because it does not resemble centralized bot farms. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[Zhao2025-ny]] show bots operating as agenda-builders and hashtag hijackers respectively—less capable of setting the public or press agenda than of degrading campaign messaging through negative-sentiment injection or narrative capture. [[Kim2026-wg]] demonstrates the offline stakes of such targeting: geo-racially targeted 2016 voter-suppression ads (including Russian IRA content) produced measurable, heterogeneous turnout suppression concentrated exactly among the targeted subpopulations—a reminder that average-treatment-effect designs can obscure real democratic harm from microtargeted partisan manipulation.

## Elite strategy, party radicalization, and the disinformation order

A distinct but related line treats misinformation and polarization as elite political strategy rather than mass cognitive failure. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis of parliamentarians' tweets finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts sharing low-factuality content—only their conjunction, radical-right populism, robustly does, tying misinformation to a specific ideological formation rather than a generic social-media pathology. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces a parallel mechanism inside the US Republican Party: the sudden 2020–21 uptake of "Critical Race Theory" as a talking point reveals a "logic of connective faction," whereby ideologically extreme legislators build reciprocal, engagement-rewarded ties to hyperpartisan media that intensify intra-party radicalization. [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes this into a "communication-as-organization" framework, arguing that digital surrogate organizations (QAnon, Stop the Steal networks, Proud Boys-adjacent media) are pulling the Republican Party toward illiberalism in ways that purely individual-level disinformation research misses—a claim empirically grounded by [[Bailard2024-pj]]'s finding that Proud Boys' online diagnostic and motivational framing Granger-causes offline violence in a reciprocal online-offline cycle. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Balluff2026-bv]] (the latter documenting Austria's "Inseratenaffäre" media-capture scandal, in which government advertising secretly bought favorable coverage) show that institutional position and ideological orientation function as checks—or their absence, as levers—on elite misinformation and media capture even in established democracies. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review situates all of this within a broader claim that online and offline radicalization can no longer be meaningfully separated, while [[Marwick2025-vx]] offers a corrective to the "exposure equals belief" model implicit in much of this literature, showing that Far-Right "redpilling" is typically a gradual socialization process in which disinformation is marshaled as legitimizing "evidence" for a preexisting self-image of rational skepticism.

## Narrative, framing, and identity as the substrate of polarization

Several papers argue that polarization operates less through discrete false claims than through shared cultural narrative and identity work. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story*, arguing that formal narrative features (characters, catchphrases, moral stakes) rather than factual falsity explain spread and resistance to fact-checking. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop this insight computationally for US election-integrity rumors, showing that misleading rumors emerge from the interaction of largely accurate evidence with partisan interpretive frames, and that influential accounts often share bare evidence while partisan audiences supply the frame collaboratively—a "deep story" dynamic that persists and mutates from 2020 into 2022. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes narrative polarization computationally via Greimas' actantial model on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube content, finding that videos are sharply polarized while comments surprisingly converge on surface narrative even as deeper "motifs" preserve partisan difference—suggesting narrative and audience ideology can be partially orthogonal. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and reclaimed stigma ("we, the conspiracy theorists") rather than platform-specific content, uniting otherwise ideologically disparate groups into a diagonal coalition. [[Yoo2026-ev]] demonstrates that "fake news" itself has become a weaponized, near-empty rhetorical label used symmetrically by left- and right-hyperpartisan outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy and deviance. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows an analogous dynamic at the level of toxic engagement: influencer-sourced misinformation, especially on sociopolitical topics, generates more homogeneous, echo-chamber-like toxicity than identical content from ordinary users, because credibility and parasocial bonds legitimize hostility. [[Lieu2025-nl]] and [[Arora2025-tx]]/[[Sarmiento2025-as]] contribute complementary content- and method-level pieces—showing, respectively, that specific climate-misinformation content categories (attacks on climate solutions) are most polarizing regardless of underlying logical fallacy, and that multi-modal and unsupervised framing-detection methods can surface latent partisan framing structures that fixed-category coding misses.

## Party communication style, emotion, and cross-national comparison

Beyond Brazil and the US, a European comparative cluster examines how party communication strategies court affective, emotionally polarizing engagement. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] show that Scandinavian populist parties (Sweden Democrats, Norway's Progress Party) are especially effective at eliciting "Angry" Facebook reactions, which in turn predict higher sharing—while "Love" reactions predict less sharing, and negative sentiment has risen over the past decade, especially under confrontational leadership. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook Reactions research confirms this as a general pattern: political and sociopolitical content skews toward a broader, more negative emotional profile than lifestyle content, and anger reliably drives engagement for populist and far-right actors specifically. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extends this to visual, cross-platform strategy, showing the Sweden Democrats deployed argumentative posts on Facebook/X but memetic, affectively charged securitizing content on TikTok using decontextualized non-EU footage to manufacture crisis—illustrating how platform-specific affordances shape the *form* rather than merely the intensity of partisan emotional appeal. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] connects this comparative electoral picture to citizen perception, showing far-right electoral performance in the 2024 European Parliament elections shapes aggregate disinformation concern, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a "hostile misinformation effect" across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland: citizens systematically believe their own party is more targeted by misinformation than the opposing party, an effect amplified by political interest, identity strength, ideological extremity, and right-wing orientation. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] adds a further twist: media narratives about election interference themselves generate perceived and affective polarization—especially when the reported beneficiary is the reader's out-party—independent of whether the underlying interference was effective, implying alarmist coverage can itself be a polarization vector. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows a parallel mechanism operating over scientific expertise during COVID-19, with UK newspapers of different political leanings systematically citing scientists aligned with their own precautionary stance, producing polarized "scientist bubbles" that mirror partisan media bubbles more generally, and [[Balluff2026-ev]] shows that even where alternative and legacy media reference the same entities (as in German Nord Stream 2 coverage), they organize them into differently structured, more cohesive relational networks—polarization as network topology rather than content divergence.

## AI, persuasion, and the emerging frontier

A rapidly growing cluster asks how generative AI reshapes the persuasion and polarization landscape underlying all of the above. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] show frontier LLMs out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, driven substantially by higher factual throughput rather than rapport, and produce measurable ballot- and election-relevant attitude change including on out-partisans—while models advocating right-leaning candidates are systematically less accurate, echoing asymmetries already documented in the human-media ecosystem (Tornberg, Knupfer, Bennett). [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows the limits of this persuasive power are political: perceived out-party bias in an LLM substantially reduces its persuasive effect via increased argumentative pushback, a direct AI-era analogue of the hostile-media-effect literature ([[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]). [[Dubey2026-bl]] finds, counterintuitively, that users with higher conspiracy beliefs trust "balanced" chatbots more than low-conspiracy users, complicating assumptions that distrust is the primary barrier to depolarizing interventions. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] demonstrate LLMs' capacity to infer political alignment from even nonpolitical text and to scale ideological placement of political texts, respectively—capabilities that promise methodological advances for this entire research area while simultaneously raising the specter of AI-enabled microtargeting that could intensify exactly the dynamics ([[Kim2026-wg]], [[Ventura2026-yc]]) documented elsewhere in this collection.

## Moderation, intervention, and the normative horizon

Closing the arc, several papers turn from diagnosis to intervention and its limits. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows X's Community Notes bridging algorithm systematically undermoderates the most polarizing (and electorally consequential) content by design, since cross-partisan consensus is structurally hardest to achieve exactly where it is most needed. [[Esau2025-tf]] and the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] articulate normative responses—deliberative reciprocity, inclusive listening, proximate framing of policy—as antidotes to "destructive" (as opposed to healthy) polarization, while [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review delivers a sobering meta-critique: the overwhelming majority of depolarization recommendations in the literature are not actually derived from the evidence presented in the same studies, but instead smuggle in unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. Read against [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s call for a more integrated, globally equitable polarization science, this collection as a whole suggests the field has accumulated rich, often contradictory empirical detail—about Brazilian Facebook networks, Scandinavian populist emotion, algorithmic amplification, elite strategy, and narrative identity—without yet resolving what, precisely, should be done about any of it, or for whom.
