# Political Polarization and Partisanship

## The shape of the problem: from bubbles to feedback loops

Two decades into the "filter bubble" debate, the field has largely moved past the question of whether echo chambers exist toward asking what kind of theoretical object polarization even is. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] crystallizes this shift, arguing that the metaphor of bubbles should be replaced by a feedback-loop framework that can explain both the empirical fragility of pure echo-chamber effects and the very real, sometimes vicious, reinforcing dynamics documented elsewhere. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis from the opposite direction: disagreement in the literature is substantially an artifact of divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than a sign that scholars are studying different realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this empirically with a specification-curve analysis showing that the strength (though not direction) of the partisan-media/polarization relationship swings wildly depending on how "partisan slant" and "polarization" are each measured. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends the meta-critique to the normative register, showing that depolarization recommendations in the literature are usually disconnected from the very evidence scholars produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democratic ideals. Together these pieces suggest the topic has entered a reflexive, stock-taking phase, and [[Unknown2026-sl3d]]'s Nature editorial signals that this stock-taking is now spilling into calls for applied, interventionist science.

Foundational empirical anchors for this debate remain [[Bakshy2015-rn]], whose Facebook study located the main driver of ideological sorting in individual click choices rather than algorithmic ranking, and [[Green2025-ap]]'s "curation bubbles," which complicates that legacy by showing that source-level (domain) partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity — the real unit of partisan curation is the individual story, re-bundled through networked sharing, not the outlet. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] pushes the same critique further, arguing that news-circulation research has been trapped in unidimensional, outlet-level, single-country designs and proposing a multi-level "political cartography" as corrective. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s 16-year study of Reddit similarly undercuts an easy town-square/echo-chamber binary, finding that even discursively resonant subreddits share almost no users, sources, or cross-posts — polarization here is "sedimented" as a precondition of the platform's discursive-material assemblage rather than a measurable outcome of ideological distance.

## Algorithms, feeds, and causal effects

A cluster of recent field experiments tries to settle, causally, what algorithms actually do. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment finds that switching users from chronological to algorithmic feeds shifts attitudes rightward and is asymmetric — turning the algorithm off doesn't undo the shift, because algorithmic exposure changes who users choose to follow. This helps reconcile the null results of the earlier Meta collaboration studies, notably [[Allcott2025-jb]] (no detectable effects of removing political ads) and the broader 2020 Facebook/Instagram Election Study underlying [[Appel2026-qr]] (deceptive networks reached tens of millions, but most exposure came via reshares, and correlational effects on beliefs vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled). [[Brown2026-br]]'s YouTube audit similarly disaggregates supply from demand, finding strong "rabbit hole" effects but no algorithm-driven "echo chamber" or "radicalization pathway" — while still detecting a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations regardless of user identity. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky Registered Report moves from diagnosis to design, showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms (more than it directly changes attitudes), and that a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing platform enjoyment — reframing the mechanism of algorithmic harm as norm misperception rather than direct persuasion. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] complements this with an elite-centered account: blocking political-elite content (rather than politics generally) reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing without touching affective polarization, suggesting exposure architecture, not individual disposition, is the more tractable lever. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's 2024 X ban shows how state-level platform interventions can themselves become polarizing shocks, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the surviving public sphere — a useful bridge between algorithmic and regulatory levels of analysis.

## Elites, parties, and the "logic of connective faction"

A second major strand keeps the focus on political elites and party organizations as prime movers rather than incidental beneficiaries of platform dynamics. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" and [[Bennett2025-xs]]'s companion essay on "digital surrogate organizations" argue that the technocentric disinformation paradigm and the institutionalist party-politics paradigm need to be fused: hyper-partisan media and digitally networked factions don't just amplify existing party positions, they reorganize intra-party power and pull parties toward illiberalism, with the CRT case and the MAGA transformation of the GOP as exhibits. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country, 32-million-tweet analysis of parliamentarians gives this comparative teeth: misinformation sharing is not explained by populism or right-wing ideology alone, but by their interaction — radical-right populism specifically. [[Tai2026-qk]] adds a narrower but complementary claim about institutional and ideological "checks" on elite misinformation-sharing. [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s study of Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus COVID collapse shows the mechanics of elite epistemic-authority contests in real time — government critics doing "derivative reporting," Bolsonaro allies asserting authority directly, and local Amazonas deputies sidestepping polarization via brokerage narratives, a finding that nicely complements Yoo's and Mosca's work on "fake news" as elite rhetorical weapon ([[Yoo2026-ev]], [[Mosca2026-yh]]) rather than descriptor of falsity.

Adjacent to elite behavior sits a growing literature on how misinformation *labeling* itself becomes a partisan battlefield: [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] argues the bridging algorithm underlying Community Notes structurally undermoderates precisely the most polarizing (and electorally consequential) content — two papers that together suggest crowdsourced moderation doesn't escape the asymmetries it was designed to bypass. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s cross-national "hostile misinformation effect" study shows citizens perceive their own side as disproportionately targeted by misinformation, a partisan-perception mechanism that helps explain why moderation asymmetries are experienced as unfair regardless of their actual distribution.

## Narrative, framing, and the cultural turn

A parallel and increasingly influential thread reconceives disinformation and polarization as narrative and identity phenomena rather than discrete factual claims. [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s cross-platform analysis of "Lottery Manure," "Crisis Actor," and "Pee Tape" argues disinformation succeeds as cathartic, identity-affirming story, not falsifiable assertion — a claim elaborated methodologically by [[Elfes2026-jb]], whose LLM-driven Actantial Model analysis of Israeli-Palestinian YouTube content finds video-level narratives are sharply polarized while comment-level discourse converges on the surface even as deeper "narrative motifs" preserve partisan difference. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extend this narrative turn to election-integrity rumors specifically: misleading claims arise from the *interaction* of accurate evidence with partisan frames (an "evidence-frame" dynamic legible in quote-tweet structure), and collective "deep stories" persist across election cycles such that 2022 audiences interpreted ambiguous events through the interpretive scaffolding built in 2020. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]]'s six-platform study of "Great Reset" discourse similarly de-emphasizes platform affordances in favor of symbolic boundary work — reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as identity, uniting ideologically disparate groups around shared anti-institutionalism. These pieces collectively push back against information-centric fact-checking paradigms, echoing older insights about audience and self-presentation ([[Marwick2011-sl02]] on context collapse and imagined audiences) that anticipated how identity performance, not information transmission, structures online political speech.

## Affect, engagement, and platform-specific dynamics

Facebook's emotional reaction architecture provides a recurring empirical site for studying how affect drives virality across partisan and populist contexts. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]'s Scandinavian election study converge on a consistent finding — anger reactions predict sharing more than love reactions do, and populist actors are disproportionately effective at eliciting anger — which [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s decade-long study of Norway's Progress Party corroborates at the level of an individual party's evolving communication style under leadership change. [[Marino2026-slef]] brings this affective-engagement lens to the Bolsonaro case directly, showing that a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network's emotional and behavioral engagement patterns were highly unstable across 2021–2023, with a marked inflection around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt — evidence against static echo-chamber models and consonant with Trilling's feedback-loop reframing. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s earlier Italian study anticipates this dynamic-rather-than-static reading: M5S's insular news ecosystem uniquely combined echo-chamber amplification of positive coverage with active contestation of negative coverage, a strategic hybrid unavailable to mainstream parties. [[Smith2025-kc]]'s "attention broker" concept offers a structural mechanism partly underlying these affective dynamics — showing causally that amplification by high-degree accounts accelerates triad formation, a general network mechanism potentially at work beneath partisan clustering everywhere from Twitter to TikTok duets.

## Brazil and Latin America: the recurring empirical anchor

Brazil functions as this topic's most sustained comparative laboratory, with a cluster of papers tracing the same political sequence — Bolsonaro-era mobilization, the 2022 election, and its violent aftermath — through different data sources and methods. [[Kansaon2025-id]] documents coordinated WhatsApp networks translating online misinformation into real-world mobilization, including the January 8 attacks; [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use companion panel surveys to show that belief in electoral fraud claims both erodes political tolerance and depresses trust in election outcomes, with messaging apps operating as an indirect pathway and mainstream news as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]'s crowdsourced ad-auditing system extends the Cambridge-Analytica-era concern about targeted advertising into the Brazilian 2018 context. Read together with [[Marino2026-slef]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]], these studies constitute a coherent longitudinal case study of how platform-elite-audience dynamics scale from ordinary hyperpartisan engagement to constitutional crisis — a template the field increasingly applies elsewhere, as with [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]]'s extension of connective-action theory to *counter*-mobilization in the pro-Navalny/pro-Kremlin Russian Twitter case, and [[Gaw2025-ru]]'s brokerage account of influence operations in the 2022 Philippine elections.

## Beyond the US and Brazil: comparative Europe

A distinct but complementary cluster generalizes these questions to multiparty European contexts, often via the 2024 European Parliament elections. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents systematic radical-right overrepresentation in EU election news coverage across five countries, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats deploying explicitly securitizing, platform-tailored visual campaigns (memes on TikTok, argument on Facebook/X); Zhu2026-zf-adjacent work, [[Zhu2026-tn]], maps how alternative-mainstream media audience overlap depends on the institutional access afforded to populist parties, with Sweden's dense-yet-isolated alternative cluster as an instructive outlier. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] both interrogate the discourse *about* interference and disinformation itself as a polarizing force, independent of whether underlying campaigns are effective — a self-referential concern that echoes Bennett/Livingston's and Törnberg's arguments that disinformation is best studied as embedded political strategy rather than freestanding informational pathology.

## Methods as an emerging sub-thread

Finally, a fast-growing methodological seam runs beneath all of the above, driven by the arrival of LLMs as both analytic tools and objects of study. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text, raising the same micro-targeting concerns documented empirically for older technologies in [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Green2025-ap]]. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Lai2024-to]] extend ideological scaling to new units (arbitrary text, YouTube videos), while [[Paci2025-ag]] complicates optimism about LLM political competence by showing persistent failures at interpreting implicature and presupposition in Italian political discourse — a caution against overreliance on LLM annotation that [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] extend into the domain of AI persuasion itself, finding that perceived partisan bias in a chatbot measurably attenuates its persuasive power even as, elsewhere, AI dialogue proves more persuasive than traditional political advertising — with a worrying asymmetry in which right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less factually accurate. Methodological reviews like [[Mosleh2024-op]], [[Sarmiento2025-as]], and [[Arora2025-tx]] round out this seam, pushing toward multi-platform, unsupervised, and multi-modal approaches better suited to a media environment none of the classical single-platform, text-only tools were built to capture — a fitting bookend to a literature whose central lesson, across all these strands, is that polarization is not a fixed structural fact to be measured once, but a moving target constituted anew by every platform design choice, elite strategy, and narrative contest it studies.
