# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The Shifting Ground of "Polarization"

The papers filed here converge on an uncomfortable but productive observation: "polarization" is less a settled finding than a contested theoretical object whose meaning shifts with method, platform, and political context. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] opens with the blunt diagnosis that the field is disciplinarily fragmented and geographically skewed toward the US, a critique sharpened by [[Hartmann2025-px]], whose systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies shows that scholarly disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist stems largely from incompatible operationalizations rather than divergent realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this empirically: running 504 specification variants on the same panel data, methodological choices about media-slant measures and polarization operationalizations swing coefficient magnitudes nearly sevenfold, though never reverse their direction. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends the critique normatively, showing that depolarization recommendations in the literature are almost never grounded in the evidence those same studies produce, but instead smuggle in Habermasian deliberative ideals. Together these pieces suggest that "polarization research" is as much a site of conceptual and methodological contestation as a cumulative empirical program—a point [[Trilling2024-slc8]] presses furthest by arguing that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" should be retired altogether in favor of a feedback-loop vocabulary capable of explaining why reinforcing dynamics rarely run away to totalizing extremes. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] bookend this cluster with calls to action: polarization is a civic problem that science and deliberative theory both have obligations to address.

## The Empirical Echo-Chamber Debate: From Bakshy to Reddit

A long empirical lineage interrogates whether online environments genuinely sort people into ideologically closed enclaves. The foundational Facebook-based studies—[[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]]—largely temper the strong filter-bubble thesis: cross-cutting ties exist, fake-news exposure is small and concentrated among supersharers, and even a large field experiment reducing like-minded exposure by a third produced no measurable attitudinal change. [[Messing2014-jc]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] add that social endorsement cues and topic-dependence complicate any simple echo-chamber story—political topics polarize audiences while apolitical ones cross ideological lines freely. Yet [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] substantially revises this picture: using the full Facebook population rather than samples, it finds segregation far higher than prior web-browsing estimates, increasing along the funnel from potential to actual engagement, and sharply asymmetric—concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner that also houses most flagged misinformation. [[Green2025-ap]] reconciles these tensions methodologically, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" sources often serve starkly divergent partisan audiences once curation is unbundled to the individual story. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and [[Barbera2015-je]] provide the network-science and ideal-point-estimation infrastructure underlying much of this debate, while [[Dehghan2026-sy]] extends the empirical interrogation to Reddit, finding that even discursively similar subreddits show minimal cross-ideological overlap—polarization there appears "sedimented," a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than a simple discursive outcome. [[Schulz2026-ts]] reframes the entire debate again: much of what looks like opinion polarization online may be an artifact of self-selected posting behavior rather than genuine attitude divergence—"false polarization" rather than real division.

## Affective Polarization, Partisan Identity, and Emotion

A second major strand shifts from ideological to affective polarization—partisan dislike as social identity rather than policy disagreement. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] anchors this literature, showing affective polarization's growth is driven by out-party animus and spills into nonpolitical domains. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] extends this to misinformation sharing, showing fake-news dissemination is best explained not by ignorance but by partisan animus—essentially the same psychological logic governing real-news sharing. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] discovers a parallel "hostile misinformation effect," a partisan-identity-driven perception that one's in-group is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, amplified by political interest and ideological extremity. Emotion's structuring role recurs in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], both showing that anger-inducing content—especially from populist actors—drives engagement and sharing more than affectively positive content on Facebook. [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates static affective-polarization models by tracking a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network longitudinally, finding emotional engagement is highly unstable and event-contingent rather than a fixed echo-chamber property—an important corrective to treating hyperpartisan communities as impermeable.

## Narrative, Identity, and Meaning-Making Beyond Opinion Metrics

Several papers push polarization research away from attitude/exposure metrics toward narrative and identity construction. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation itself as cultural narrative—identity-affirming, cathartic story rather than discrete false claim—explaining why fact-checking fails to counter it. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes this narratological turn computationally, introducing "narrative polarisation" to show that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict diverge sharply by partisan actor-role assignment even when comment sections show surface convergence, revealing polarization's narrative depth beneath apparent dialogue. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] extends the interactionist, identity-centered approach to conspiracy cultures, arguing "Great Reset" communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity rather than ideological content or platform mechanics alone—explicitly pushing back against techno-centric echo-chamber explanations. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces a parallel politicization dynamic in the transformation of #StayWoke into "culture wars" discourse across platforms. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows how the very term "fake news" is weaponized symmetrically by left and right alternative outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy and deviance, a discursive mechanism of polarization distinct from informational content per se.

## Elites, Platforms, and the Political Economy of Partisan Media

A substantial cluster examines how political and media elites actively construct and exploit partisan ecosystems rather than merely reflecting mass-level sorting. [[Benkler2018-lw]] remains foundational here, arguing American epistemic crisis is asymmetric and institutional—a radicalized right-wing media ecosystem insulated from self-correction—rather than a symmetric, technology-driven phenomenon. [[Bennett2025-xs]] builds on this institutionalist turn, proposing that "digital surrogate organizations" pull parties toward illiberalism through organizationally-enabled connective action, a framework [[Knupfer2025-vt]] operationalizes empirically via the sudden elite adoption of "Critical Race Theory" as a radicalizing, engagement-rewarded talking point. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Rodarte2026-sl16]], both on Brazilian parliamentarians, show elites actively contesting journalistic gatekeeping and adapting communication style to platform incentives that reward antagonism over substantive legislative content—a "democratic cost" of virality-seeking. [[Eady2025-vm]] quantifies a structural driver of this dynamic: ideologically extreme politicians, especially those from electorally uncompetitive districts, share disproportionately polarized news, suggesting reduced electoral competition fuels ecosystem-level polarization. [[Darius2026-xl]] finds a parallel pattern across the 2024 European Parliament election: populist radical-right (and far-left) parties receive outsized audience support, especially on TikTok, through emotionally resonant, anti-elitist communicative style rather than sheer posting volume. [[Groebner2026-pc]] and [[Heft2021-ky]] extend this elite/infrastructure lens transnationally, showing far-right YouTube channels and hyperlink networks increasingly integrate across borders, with the US media ecosystem (especially Fox News and Breitbart) functioning as a central hub—evidence that polarized media ecosystems are becoming transnational phenomena rather than nationally contained ones.

## Platform Governance, Moderation, and Intervention

A policy-oriented cluster asks whether platform design and moderation can mitigate—or inadvertently deepen—polarization. [[Bail2018-fk]] delivers the field's most famous caution: paying partisans to follow opposing-view bots backfired, making Republicans more conservative. [[Brady2026-ln]] offers a more hopeful registered-report counterpoint, showing a "diversified extremity" algorithm can reduce amplification of intergroup, moralized, emotional content and improve norm perception without sacrificing platform enjoyment—reframing the mechanism as algorithmically distorted social-norm perception rather than direct persuasion. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] complicates optimism about crowd-sourced moderation, showing X's Community Notes bridging algorithm systematically undermoderates the most polarizing (and electorally risky) content by design. [[Ventura2026-yc]] examines a blunter intervention—Brazil's 2024 platform ban on X—finding it produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberals exited, a cautionary tale about state-imposed content governance deepening rather than resolving partisan fragmentation. [[Zhu2026-tn]] rounds out this cluster comparatively, showing audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media across six European countries depends heavily on media-system type and the institutional access afforded to populist actors—alternative media's marginality is contextual, not inherent. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] gesture toward adjacent methodological and platform-specific questions (alternative platforms' discursive effects; unsupervised framing detection) that remain underdeveloped relative to the empirical weight of the rest of the cluster.

## The Latin American Turn: Misinformation, Trust, and Democratic Erosion

A coherent sub-literature extends polarization research beyond the US/Europe axis into Brazil's polarized electoral context, often explicitly as a corrective to Western-centric bias. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a matched pair using the same three-wave Brazilian panel: the first shows electoral-fraud misinformation belief directly erodes political tolerance, amplified indirectly through messaging-app news use; the second shows mainstream news consumption stabilizes trust in election results even among Bolsonaro voters, while misinformation belief and messaging-app use erode it—jointly illustrating messaging platforms (WhatsApp) as a distinct vector of democratic harm beyond open social media. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] extends the insularity concept to Italy's 2018 election, showing populist-aligned news sources are more insular and that insularity predicts a Facebook sharing/commenting pattern in which supporters amplify favorable coverage and contest unfavorable coverage—a strategic attention-economy dynamic uniquely exploited by the Five Star Movement. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] pushes the connective-action framework into authoritarian terrain, showing pro-Navalny and pro-government Russian Twitter mobilization follow distinct centralization/modularity signatures, extending "connective action" to "connective counteraction." [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] generalizes the Global South argument structurally, arguing platform privatization of public discourse imposes disproportionate harms where regulatory infrastructure is weak, using Brazil's failed Fake News Bill as exhibit A of platform power overriding national democratic deliberation.

## Causal Tests, Boundary Conditions, and Open Questions

A final set of studies probes specific causal mechanisms and boundary conditions of polarization-relevant beliefs. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows unsubstantiated elite fraud claims erode election confidence selectively among sympathetic partisans, with fact-checks failing to repair the damage—a finding that resonates with [[Karlsson2026-hd]], which shows that merely narrating election-interference stories (regardless of their factual effectiveness) asymmetrically raises affective polarization depending on which party is perceived to benefit, implying alarmist discourse about interference can itself inflict the harm it describes. [[Lieu2025-nl]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] extend polarization dynamics into science communication, showing climate misinformation content-type (not fallacy type) drives ideological polarization, and that UK newspapers politically sort which COVID-19 scientists they cite despite those scientists being mainstream rather than fringe. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to disinformation concern at the voter level, while [[Wan2026-ai]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] probe more granular mechanisms—political efficacy's role in algorithmic news selection, and the evidence-frame dynamics by which true facts become misleading rumors through partisan framing in quote-tweet threads. [[Divon2026-wx]] closes the set with a comparative study of ostensibly apolitical influencers brokering state narratives during crises in Israel and China, showing nationalism operates as a situational visibility resource cutting across regime type—a fitting coda to the collection's overall argument that polarization is not a single measurable quantity but an entangled outcome of platform design, elite strategy, narrative construction, and identity performance, requiring continual re-specification across contexts rather than a single master explanation.
