# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The Arc of the Topic

This cluster of papers charts a single, tightly interconnected story: the rise and fall of a "Golden Age" of open platform data, the scramble to measure what was lost, and the uneven, contested attempt to rebuild research infrastructure through regulation — above all the EU's Digital Services Act. Read together, they move from historical diagnosis, to methodological critique of what remains, to institutional and political analysis of the DSA's implementation, to normative argument about what should come next.

## From APIcalypse to the Post-API Age

The foundational narrative is laid out by [[Bruns2019-nr]] and [[Freelon2018-ao]], who coin and periodize the "APIcalypse" following the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal — the moment platforms began treating independent researcher access as a reputational liability rather than a public good. [[Freelon2024-sc]] extends this into a full periodization across six platforms, showing that data access has become permanently contingent on scandal, leadership change, and commercial interest rather than settling into any stable regime. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and Bastos2025-ya retell this arc specifically for Twitter/X — from "Wild West" to "Golden Age" Academic API to Musk-era "Walled Garden" — while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the human cost: a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024, the first sustained contraction after two decades of growth. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review generalizes this: across social science disciplines, the empirical study of social media plateaued and declined in 2022–2024, precisely as API restrictions bit, with research oddly over-indexed on Twitter/X and Facebook relative to actual platform use (cf. YouTube, TikTok). [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] document the downstream consequences for specific subfields (election research; representative sampling), showing how "independence by permission," price, proficiency, and geographic privilege now gate who gets to study platforms at all — a structural inequity amplified for Global South and non-WEIRD researchers also stressed in [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]].

## Industry Capture of the Scientific Record

A second strand interrogates not just *whether* data is accessible but *who* gets to use it and *how that shapes knowledge*. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] provide the most systematic reckoning: roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring circle of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, yielding an estimated ~80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this through the lens of tobacco- and pharma-style industry influence, and [[Munger2025-cz]] offers an insider-adjacent critique of the flagship Meta2020 partnership, arguing that even the most ambitious platform-academic collaboration produced narrow, temporally fragile estimates — exposing a structural mismatch between the pace of platform change and the pace of social science. [[Cullen2026-cb]] develops the "Seeing like an API" framework to show how CrowdTangle's technical and governance design actively constrained what could be known, not merely accessed, while [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] offer granular methodological audits of the Social Science One URL Shares dataset, demonstrating that its 100-share censoring threshold — not differential privacy noise — systematically distorts fake-news prevalence estimates and induces spurious cross-country "trends" that are really platform-wide algorithmic artifacts. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] builds on this dataset cautiously for a European comparative study, underscoring how contested the instrument remains even among those who use it productively.

## Auditing the Instruments of Access

A parallel body of work turns critical attention to the *new* tools meant to replace CrowdTangle and open APIs, generally finding them inadequate. [[Rieder2025-ju]] audits YouTube's Data API and finds it "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and irreproducibility that undermine DSA-relevant systemic-risk research. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] reaches similar conclusions about TikTok's Research API, and [[Bruns2026-pn]] offers a sweeping critique of the "clean room" model (Meta Content Library, ProQuest TDM Studio), arguing that disconnected, code-only environments structurally privilege quantitative, well-resourced researchers over qualitative, comparative, or Majority-World scholarship. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] complements this with a methodological warning about web-scraping's own distortions (in-situ vs. ex-situ capture), and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the broader API/tool landscape in the DSA era. Together these pieces insist that "access" without usable, well-documented, representative data is a hollow transparency gesture — a theme crystallized in [[Rieder2020-sl35]]'s influential reframing of platform accountability from "transparency" (the black-box-opening metaphor) to "observability" (a sustained, resourced, institutional practice), which anticipates much of the DSA debate that follows.

## The DSA as Contested Infrastructure

The regulatory core of the topic is the EU Digital Services Act, especially Article 40's vetted-researcher access provision. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] offer complementary assessments of what Article 40 actually changes in practice — new APIs and portals, but also legal, technical, and organizational friction, platform resistance, and misaligned incentives between researchers, regulators, and VLOPs. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] frame the resulting turbulence not as regulatory failure but as evidence the DSA is finally constraining entrenched platform power — a "moving target, moving politics" situation requiring sustained implementation rather than retreat. [[Annabell2026-ev]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] dig into the conceptual substructure of this fight, showing how basic terms — "data," "data quality" — are "floating signifiers" that platforms (especially Meta, Amazon, Booking.com) strategically narrow during consultation processes to limit what counts as accessible or adequate. [[Lukito2026-nb]], [[Schulte2026-df]], and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] ground these abstract battles in concrete empirical casework — multi-platform election-discourse infrastructure problems, and DSA-mandated transparency data revealing stark linguistic disparities in human moderation workforces (Global South languages chronically under-resourced relative to English). [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends the audit logic to YouTube's election-period search and labeling practices, finding inconsistent application of authority signals and opaque removal communication — again calling for expanded researcher access via YouTube's Research Program. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out comparatively, treating DSA-style "flow optimisation" alongside US "free circulation" and German "patrolling" regimes as variants of a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation," while [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA era demands a conceptual shift from individual causal-effects research toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] shows how DSA provisions (Article 25, dark patterns) are also generating new HCI-legal hybrid methodologies for auditing design-level autonomy violations, as in the ongoing X blue-checkmark case. [[Park2026-tr]] supplies a sobering coda: researchers doing this work — on vulnerabilities, scraping, social computing — face escalating legal risk (CFAA, CMA, DMCA, ToS litigation) that chills exactly the public-interest scrutiny the DSA is meant to enable.

## Platform Self-Governance and Its Discontents

A final cluster examines what platforms do *within* this access and regulatory landscape — how they define, communicate, and quietly revise their own moderation commitments. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the conceptual root: the term "platform" itself is a rhetorical technology that lets intermediaries claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] extends this into the "platform society" thesis of privatized public value. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] identifies "reduction" (algorithmic demotion short of removal) as an underexamined, largely invisible form of moderation — a framework directly operationalized by [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], whose empirical analysis of Meta's political content reduction policy on Italian parliamentarians finds a 72% reach decline for mainstream politicians (and a transparency failure in Meta's own DSA disclosures) alongside a perverse resilience of extremist accounts that offset losses through increased posting volume. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's shifting terminology ("fake news" → "misinformation" → "borderline") as a depoliticizing strategy that obscures the persistently right-leaning skew of top-performing problematic content, while [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] analyze Meta's and other platforms' official communications as discursive "changecraft" that reframes ideological pivots as mere continuity and externalizes blame to "inauthentic" outside actors. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] documents the resulting policy arc across five platforms — protection, civic-integrity expansion, and recent backlash/rollback — and [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows how Meta's narrowing definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has let much manufactured attention (ad networks, hijacked groups, media coordination) persist unchallenged. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] extends the discursive-legitimation lens to climate misinformation, finding platforms substitute "soft" interventions and performative care narratives for enforceable rules. The retreat from institutionalized fact-checking is covered empirically by [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] (Meta's shift to "conflictual cooperation" post-Zuckerberg) and [[Farkas2026-lr]] (fact-checkers' rhetorical defense of platform funding), with [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] providing quantitative scrutiny of Community Notes and user-flagging as replacements — finding persistent partisan asymmetries and politically motivated (not strictly truth-tracking) reporting behavior, even as [[Miller2026-cp]] describes platform efforts to scale AI-assisted note-writing while preserving human arbitration. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] situate this retrenchment within a broader "trust and safety" collapse with implications for democratic backsliding, while case studies of TikTok's jihadist "everyday extremism" ([[Karo2026-dn]]) and YouTube's persistent "Tate-space" ([[Rieder2025-ju]]/[[Rieder2026-pp]]) demonstrate empirically that moderation — even where well-resourced — is structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation. Adjacent threads on recommender-system profiling ([[Bouchaud2026-lr]]), platform-independent experimentation under closed APIs ([[Allen2025-ot]]), algorithmic black-boxing of news ([[McNally2025-dn]]), and AI-driven scam ecosystems (Schiffrin2025-undated) all extend the same governance question — who sees, controls, and is accountable for platform-scale effects — into new technical and harm domains, while [[Suk2026-ai]] gestures toward how this entire infrastructure/trust problem may reconstitute itself around generative AI.

## The Throughline

Across all these registers — historical, methodological, legal, and discursive — the papers converge on a shared diagnosis: platform research is not a neutral empirical enterprise but one whose very possibility is continuously negotiated between corporate control of infrastructure, regulatory ambition, and researcher ingenuity. The DSA represents the first serious attempt to convert this negotiation from informal "data philanthropy" into enforceable right, but as [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Peters2026-mo]] all stress, the battle has simply relocated — from whether access exists to what counts as adequate data, quality, and observability. The unresolved question threading through this entire structure note is whether platform accountability research can ever escape dependence on the very entities it studies.
