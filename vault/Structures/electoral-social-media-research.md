# Electoral Social Media Research

## The Italian backbone: coordination as method and object

The longest continuous thread in this topic is the Italian line running from [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] through [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]. It begins by diagnosing the 2018 election's hybrid media ecology — populist parties (M5S, League) cultivating insular news sources whose Facebook engagement patterns diverge sharply from mainstream outlets — and then pivots methodologically: rather than chase content veracity or unmask "bad actors," [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] proposes detecting *coordinated link sharing behavior* as a signature of manipulation, a move consolidated into a live monitoring workflow in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] that surfaces not just ideological operations but click-economy and religious-proselytism networks hiding behind political content. This lineage increasingly turns reflexive about its own tools: [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Meta's URL Shares Dataset comparatively to Germany, France and Italy, finding untrustworthy-URL shares spiking in electoral years even as *views* of such content stay flat — a finding sharpened into an argument about active platform curation in [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which shows partisan reach penalties and journalistic-quality rewards fluctuating in lockstep with Meta's known "break the glass" interventions. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] closes the loop by using the DSA-mandated Content Library to show that Meta's 2021 political-content-reduction policy hit Italian MPs nearly a year before its announced rollout and disproportionately spared extremist accounts — evidence, the author argues, of both a transparency deficit and the research value of DSA-enabled infrastructure. Running alongside this empirical arc is a methodological subplot on LLM-assisted analysis of Italian political text: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] validates embedding models for clustering, and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] reflects on the harder problem of validating fully LLM-integrated pipelines — together modeling how the MINE programme's computational toolkit has evolved from CrowdTangle-era network detection to embedding- and prompt-based analysis.

## Germany: infrastructural strain and multimodal experimentation

The German 2021 and 2025 elections generate a smaller but pointed cluster preoccupied with *how* to study platforms rather than only what is found on them. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] opens the empirical file on political advertising and coordinated behavior before the 2021 federal election, while [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] pushes methodology forward by benchmarking multimodal LLMs against specialized computer-vision pipelines for visual political communication on Instagram, finding GPT-4o dramatically better at identifying front-runners and concentrated visibility strategies. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] widens the lens to five platforms simultaneously, showing that German far-right networks are not a single object but a set of platform-specific sociotechnical formations — Telegram's fringe inward orientation, Twitter's scalable outward broadcast, Facebook's hierarchical AfD-dominated replicability — that no single-platform study could reveal. [[Schulte2026-df]] then turns the 2025 election into an explicit case study of research *infrastructure*, arguing that the field's central bottleneck is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable, standardized multi-platform observability under DSA-era access constraints — a claim that dovetails with [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] on the wider data-access crisis (see below).

## Brazil: from Bolsonarist networks to the fragility of post-election legitimacy

The Brazilian cluster traces an arc from campaign-period coordination to post-election contestation of legitimacy itself. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] opens with a crowdsourced audit of irregular Facebook advertising in the 2018 race, prefiguring later concerns about platform opacity. The 2022 election and its January 2023 aftermath dominate: [[Kansaon2025-id]] documents coordinated WhatsApp networks whose misinformation and doxxing campaigns fed directly into real-world mobilization around the January 8 attacks, while [[Marino2026-slef]] shows that the pro-Bolsonaro Facebook hyperpartisan community was far from a stable echo chamber — its affective engagement swung sharply around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt, complicating static polarization models. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a matched pair using the same panel-survey infrastructure: one shows electoral-misinformation belief driving political intolerance over time, the other shows it eroding trust in election results specifically among Bolsonaro voters via messaging-app pathways, jointly arguing that Brazilian democratic backsliding is mediated less by generic platform use than by *which* informational pathway citizens rely on. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends this messaging-centric focus with a WhatsApp deactivation experiment, and [[Ventura2026-yc]] moves downstream to the 2024 X ban, showing that state-level platform bans can produce durable rightward "sorting ratchets" rather than symmetric demobilization. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] pull back to elite behavior: parliamentarians waging epistemic-authority contests during the Manaus crisis, and more broadly adapting communication style to platform virality incentives at a documented "democratic cost" to substantive accountability. [[Holt2026-zq]]'s analysis of user-flagged "false news," which uses 2018 Brazilian election data as one of its two critical cases, suggests users report content they *disagree with* rather than content they believe false — a cautionary note for Meta's post-fact-checking reliance on crowd flags.

## The US minimal-effects tradition and its contested afterlife

A long-running debate about whether social media "fake news" and foreign influence campaigns meaningfully swayed the 2016 and 2020 US elections structures much of the topic's theoretical stakes. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Guess2020-rr]] establish the canonical "minimal effects" position: fake news exposure and sharing were real but heavily concentrated among small, older, conservative subpopulations, dwarfed by mainstream news consumption. [[Eady2023-xg]] extends this logic to the Russian IRA campaign specifically, finding exposure concentrated among strong partisans least in need of persuasion and no measurable attitudinal effect. The 2020 Meta-academic collaboration continues the tradition at scale: [[Guess2023-ai]] and [[Guess2023-ur]] find that stripping reshares or reverting to chronological feeds sharply changes content exposure without moving polarization or knowledge, and [[Allcott2025-jb]] similarly finds no detectable effects from removing political ads. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] complicates the picture with diffusion-tree evidence that misinformation exhibits distinctively viral reach patterns even within this broadly null-effects environment. This consensus is directly challenged from several directions: [[Yang2023-cg]] argues that link-based misinformation measures have missed the dominant form — image posts — recovering rates of 20%+ misinformation with strong right-wing asymmetry; [[Kim2026-wg]] shows that average-treatment-effect designs obscure large, geographically and racially targeted turnout suppression from digital ads; and [[Appel2026-qr]] reframes "deceptive networks" to include financially motivated operations reaching tens of millions largely through unaffiliated reshares, while still finding attitudinal correlations vanish after controlling for prior characteristics. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X's algorithm partially reconciles these camps: switching the algorithm on shifts attitudes rightward through a persistent following-behavior mechanism, even though switching it off does not reverse the effect — explaining, the authors argue, why prior null findings for symmetric on/off comparisons may have missed asymmetric persistence.

## Coordination and deplatforming beyond the core cases

The coordinated-behavior paradigm pioneered for Italy travels widely. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] reframes coordination as a continuum rather than a binary for the 2019 UK election, showing coordination and automation are orthogonal and that the most tightly coordinated clusters are small activist groups, not mainstream parties. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework to authoritarian "connective counteraction," contrasting centralized pro-Navalny mobilization with modular, astroturf-like pro-government coordination on Russian Twitter. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] pushes further into digital authoritarianism, arguing Serbia's SNS mimics authentic grassroots support in ways that evade standard bot-farm detection entirely. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceives influence operations as political brokerage in the 2022 Philippine elections, foregrounding the political-economic infrastructure sustaining manipulation rather than isolated bad actors. On the intervention side, [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" that flipped authority structures rather than merely subtracting banned nodes — a rare demonstration of moderation efficacy that stands in tension with [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s finding that X's Community Notes system structurally *undermoderates* polarizing content by design, creating cross-national electoral risk.

## Platform governance, transparency, and the data-access crisis

A distinct meta-layer interrogates the infrastructural conditions of this entire research programme. [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] converge on the diagnosis that platform-permission-based access is inherently unstable ("independence by permission"), unevenly distributed globally, and skewed toward production over consumption data — pointing to the EU Digital Services Act as both cause of new friction and potential remedy. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] historicizes this by tracing three phases of platform election policy (protective, civic-integrity, rollback) largely reactive to US election cycles, while [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[McNally2025-dn]] operationalize DSA-era auditing directly — the former exposing inconsistent authoritative-source labeling on YouTube, the latter using time-series methods to show Facebook's News Feed is not an unknowable "black box" but a system whose strategic interventions leave detectable, lagged traces. Read together with [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], these papers make the case that platform governance itself — not just user behavior — is now a primary object of electoral social media research.

## Misinformation as elite strategy, frame, and narrative

Several papers relocate the locus of the misinformation problem from isolated false claims to elite strategy and collective narrative-work. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] shows across 26 countries that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts parliamentarians' misinformation sharing, but their conjunction — radical-right populism — does. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces a parallel mechanism inside the US Republican party, where a "logic of connective faction" links ideologically extreme legislators to hyper-partisan media ecosystems via triggering issues like Critical Race Theory. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorize the *rhetoric* of falsehood itself — fake-news accusations as delegitimization weapons, and "strategic lying" as an intensification of PR spin whose rebuttals paradoxically amplify the original claim. On the reception side, [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop complementary frameworks — evidence-frame analysis and "deep storytelling" — showing that 2020–2022 US election-integrity rumors are less about fabricated facts than about politically charged framing performed collaboratively by influencers and audiences across quote-tweet chains, with 2022 discourse increasingly relying on audiences to supply meaning via an already-established 2020 deep story. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] adds a further reflexive twist: media narratives *about* interference can themselves raise perceived and affective polarization, independent of whether the underlying interference was effective — a finding in productive tension with the "minimal effects" literature above.

## Visibility, emotion, and the shape of campaign visuals

A final set of papers examines the demand-side dynamics that make certain content, actors, or ideological families disproportionately visible. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents structurally disproportionate media visibility for the Radical Right across five countries in the 2024 European Parliament elections, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance directly to voters' disinformation concerns. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] turn to Scandinavian Facebook data to show that anger-inducing populist content is disproportionately shared while love-reactions suppress sharing, and that negative sentiment has intensified over a decade of Progress Party leadership change. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual discourse platform-by-platform, prioritizing TikTok meme culture over their larger Facebook and X followings to reach younger voters — a strategic logic echoed in [[Lukito2026-il]]'s account of US right-leaning outlets curating distinct content packages for Twitter versus Truth Social, and in [[Much2026-gu]]'s discovery of "gendered media spaces" in non-political podcasts that incidentally sorted young men toward conservative content in 2024. [[Lin2025-xp]] closes the topic with a forward-looking provocation: brief human–AI dialogues produce persuasion effects larger than traditional political ads across four elections, achieved mainly through relevant facts rather than manipulation — although right-leaning AI advocacy was systematically less accurate, suggesting the next frontier of electoral social media research may concern generative dialogue rather than static feeds. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] gestures toward the methodological corrective this whole literature increasingly needs: multi-level "political cartographies" of news circulation that move past unidimensional, outlet-only, single-country measures of partisan sharing.
