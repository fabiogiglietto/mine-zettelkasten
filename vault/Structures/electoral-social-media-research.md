# Electoral Social Media Research

## The empirical backbone: three national arenas, one research programme

This cluster of papers constitutes the country-by-country empirical core of the MINE programme: dense, data-intensive studies of how coordinated actors, hyperpartisan pages, and paid advertising shaped specific election campaigns in Italy, Germany, and Brazil. Read together, they trace an evolving methodological and theoretical arc — from early content- and link-based detection of coordination, through platform-specific and multimodal analyses, to a mature concern with how platform *governance itself* (moderation policy, ad delivery, data access) becomes an object of study rather than a background condition.

### Italy: from coordinated link sharing to LLM-assisted narrative mapping

The Italian case is the longest-running and most methodologically self-reflexive strand. It begins with [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframes disinformation research around *coordinated link sharing behavior* rather than content veracity, showing across the 2018 and 2019 campaigns that coordinated Facebook networks disproportionately amplify problematic domains. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] complements this with a partisanship lens, demonstrating that populist-aligned news sources (M5S above all) are more insular on Twitter and that insularity predicts distinctive Facebook engagement patterns — amplification via shares versus contestation via comments. The 2022 snap election becomes the testbed for a second generation of tools: [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] develops a workflow for continuously *updating* lists of coordinated accounts rather than relying on static blacklists, uncovering an M5S-aligned hyperpartisan network, a religiously-cloaked clickbait operation, and a proselytising bot network — evidence that coordination is actor-agnostic and spans political, commercial, and religious motives. Methodological innovation continues with [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], which together validate LLM-based embedding and classification pipelines for clustering Italian political news across both the 2018 and 2022 datasets, explicitly wrestling with how to validate fully LLM-integrated research infrastructure. This Italian thread closes the loop on platform governance itself in [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which uses the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72% well before its announced global rollout — while extremist accounts compensated by posting more, revealing an asymmetric, undisclosed governance effect that DSA transparency mechanisms failed to surface. [[Mosca2026-yh]] extends the Italian focus into rhetoric, proposing a "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model for how "fake news" accusations function as weapons of negative campaigning in the Italian press, while two earlier studies — [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] on the hybrid TV/Twitter ecology of the 2013 election and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] on Facebook-ad-based survey recruitment — establish the country's longer methodological pedigree in hybrid-media and platform-survey research that the later coordination studies build upon.

### Germany: platform-specific ecologies of the far right and the campaign

The German cluster is smaller but pointed. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] directly targets the 2021 federal election, examining political advertising and coordinated behaviour as intertwined features of the campaign's online environment. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] turns to the same election's visual dimension, validating multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) against specialised computer-vision tools for Instagram content and revealing distinct "concentrated visibility" strategies between candidate and party accounts, and between stories and posts. Looking beyond a single election cycle, [[Kakavand2026-kt]] maps far-right actor networks across five platforms (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, Telegram), arguing that affordances are relational rather than fixed — the AfD's networks look radically different depending on whether one studies Facebook's hierarchical, party-coordinated structure or Twitter's loosely-clustered international broadcast arena. [[Schulte2026-df]] closes this strand by turning the 2025 German election into a case study of research *infrastructure* itself, documenting how X and TikTok's divergent scraping regimes and algorithmic filtering bias what can even be observed about party-specific platform strategies (e.g., AfD's TikTok dominance versus BSW's X-centrism), making explicit a concern — data access as a constraint on empirical election research — that runs implicitly through the entire Italian and Brazilian literatures as well.

### Brazil: post-Bolsonaro fracture, coordination, and the cost of platform politics

The Brazilian cluster is the most populous and thematically richest, tracking the country's post-2018 hyperpartisan trajectory through crisis, election, insurrection, and platform bans. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] opens with the Manaus healthcare collapse, showing how parliamentarians contest journalistic gatekeeping through derivative reporting, direct authority claims, or proximity-based brokerage — a preview of the elite narrative warfare that intensifies through the 2022 election. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] anchors the advertising side earlier, in the 2018 campaign, with a volunteer browser-plugin audit of Facebook political ads that anticipates later platform-transparency debates. The coordination strand is carried by [[Kansaon2025-id]], which traces coordinated WhatsApp activity (13 million messages, 1,444 groups) from the 2022 election through the January 8 uprising, showing coordinated accounts driving both misinformation dissemination and real-world mobilization such as the doxxing of Supreme Court justices — a rare demonstration of digital coordination translating directly into offline political violence. [[Marino2026-slef]] and [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] extend the post-election analysis: the former shows that a pro-Bolsonaro hyperpartisan Facebook network's affective engagement is volatile rather than a stable echo chamber, pivoting sharply around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt; the latter demonstrates that parliamentarians' own beliefs about viral incentives — confirmed computationally — push them away from programmatic communication toward antagonism, a "democratic cost" of platform logics. Attitudinal consequences are picked up by [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], companion panel studies showing that belief in electoral fraud misinformation both erodes trust in election results and increases political intolerance, with messaging apps operating as an indirect pathway distinct from open social media. [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes this messaging-app focus into causal territory via a WhatsApp deactivation field experiment, while [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the arc past Bolsonaro into 2024, showing that Brazil's Supreme-Court-ordered X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban and liberal users exited — evidence that state-level platform intervention can itself become a partisan-polarizing event, echoing the governance-sensitivity theme found in the Italian Meta-policy studies.

### Convergences across the three settings

Despite their national specificity, these studies converge on several claims that anchor the MINE programme's broader argument. First, coordination is best understood behaviourally and infrastructurally rather than through fixed lists of bad actors — a lesson learned in Italy ([[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]) and confirmed in Brazil's WhatsApp networks ([[Kansaon2025-id]]). Second, platform governance interventions — Meta's content-reduction policy in Italy ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]), Brazil's judicial X ban ([[Ventura2026-yc]]) — are not neutral background conditions but active, asymmetrically distributed forces that reshape visibility and partisan composition, often in ways platforms fail to disclose. Third, affective and hyperpartisan dynamics are more unstable and event-driven than static echo-chamber models suggest, whether in Italian populist insularity ([[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]) or Brazilian pro-Bolsonaro engagement volatility ([[Marino2026-slef]]). Finally, methodological innovation — LLM-assisted classification and embedding in Italy ([[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]), multimodal vision models in Germany ([[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]) — is not incidental but constitutive of what these country cases can even claim to observe, a concern that generalizes into the broader MINE questions about research infrastructure and data access surveyed elsewhere in the programme.
