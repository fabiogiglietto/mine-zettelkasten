# Social Media and Electoral Campaigns

## The Shape of the Field: From Virality Panic to Infrastructure Critique

The papers filed here trace an arc that begins with alarm over "fake news" circa 2016 and ends, a decade later, with a far more disaggregated set of questions about algorithms, coordination, platform governance, and data access. Early foundational work — [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]] — converged on a counterintuitive consensus: misinformation exposure was real but heavily concentrated among small, predictable (older, more conservative) subpopulations, and its aggregate electoral impact was likely modest. [[Allcott2019-gn]] extended this into a platform-comparative trend analysis showing Facebook's fake-news problem declining relative to Twitter's after 2016. This "minimal effects, concentrated exposure" consensus becomes the empirical baseline against which nearly every later study in this collection positions itself — either confirming it in new contexts or complicating it with new methods.

## The Meta Collaborations and the Causal Turn

A cluster of large-scale Meta-partnered field experiments around the 2020 US election — [[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Eady2023-xg]] — pushed the field from correlational description toward causal inference at population scale. The headline finding, replicated across reshares, chronological feeds, like-minded exposure, and political ad removal, is a cascade of precisely-estimated nulls: manipulating core platform mechanisms changed what people saw but not what they believed, felt, or did politically. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicates this picture by showing ideological segregation increases sharply along the "funnel of engagement" and is strikingly asymmetric — a homogeneous conservative corner hosts most fact-checked misinformation — even as individual-level attitudinal effects remain elusive. [[Appel2026-qr]]'s audit of 49 deceptive networks reinforces the pattern: huge raw reach figures dissolve into near-zero causal effects once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled, and most exposure arrives indirectly via reshares by non-network accounts.

This consensus of null attitudinal effects is now being directly challenged. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X's "For You" algorithm finds a real, asymmetric, persistence-driven conservative shift in attitudes — explicitly reconciling itself with the Meta 2020 null results by locating the mechanism in algorithmically-induced *following* behavior that outlives the intervention. This suggests the earlier null results may be artifacts of short intervention windows and platform-specific algorithmic dynamics rather than a general law.

## Coordination, Inauthenticity, and the Shift to Behavioral Detection

A parallel lineage moves from content-based to behavior-based detection of manipulation. [[Keller2019-nk]]'s ground-truthed study of South Korean NIS astroturfing established that coordination traces, not bot-like features, are the reliable signature of orchestrated campaigns — and that even well-resourced operations have limited discourse-level impact. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] generalizes this into a continuous, community-specific measure of coordination (decoupled from automation) applied to the 2019 UK election. The Italian research program — [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] — builds a sustained methodological and empirical throughline across the 2018 and 2019 Italian elections, reframing disinformation research around "coordinated link sharing behavior" and iterative, self-updating detection workflows, with case studies ranging from M5S hyperpartisan networks to religious-proselytism clickbait operations. [[Oprea2025-lf]] extends this to Romanian hyperactive-user auditing in the 2024 EP elections, while [[Kansaon2025-id]] carries the coordination paradigm into the closed ecosystem of WhatsApp, linking coordinated Brazilian groups directly to real-world mobilization around the January 8 attacks. [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extend coordination/bot questions into hashtag hijacking and agenda-building theory, respectively, arguing that non-human actors now co-produce campaign and press agendas.

## Platform Governance, Algorithmic Visibility, and the Data-Access Crisis

A distinct and increasingly dominant strand treats the platform itself — not just user or elite behavior — as the object of study. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] demonstrate, via longitudinal time-series and breakpoint methods, that Facebook's News Feed is not an inert conduit but an actively, variably curated system whose governance interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures) leave detectable fingerprints in reach and amplification data. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] documents a concrete case — Meta's political content reduction policy — showing a 72% reach collapse for Italian MPs that both preceded Meta's announced rollout and asymmetrically spared extremist accounts, raising transparency concerns. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's shifting moderation vocabulary ("fake news" → "borderline") across three US election cycles, arguing depoliticized, content-agnostic framing obscures a persistently right-skewed problem. [[Jurg2025-ur]] performs a comparable audit of YouTube's "authoritative sources" and labeling regime around the 2024 EP elections, finding inconsistent and linguistically uneven transparency. [[Ventura2026-yc]] and [[Simeone2025-vo]] examine the downstream partisan consequences of platform-level interventions — Brazil's X ban and Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts — both finding durable, asymmetric restructuring of political discourse communities. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] extends governance critique to crowd-based moderation, arguing Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing (including electoral) content.

Undergirding all of this is a growing meta-discourse about research infrastructure itself. [[Lukito2026-nb]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[Schulte2026-df]] each catalogue how data-access regimes (CrowdTangle's shutdown, DSA-mandated APIs, the Meta Content Library) shape what can be known about platform effects at all, converging on calls for pluralistic, regulator-backed, multi-institutional access — with [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] providing the complementary policy history of how platforms' own election-integrity rules have cycled through phases of protection, expansion, and backlash.

## National and Regional Case Studies: Italy, Germany, Brazil

Within this methodological arc, country-specific campaigns anchor the empirical core of the topic. The Italian sequence ([[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]) moves from early hybrid-media/second-screen studies of the 2013 election through coordination detection in 2018/2019 to LLM-assisted pipelines and policy audits in 2022–2024, constituting a near-complete methodological genealogy within one national context. German election studies cluster more recently and more technically: [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] on 2021 ad/coordination patterns, [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] on multimodal LLM auditing of Instagram visibility, [[Schulte2026-df]] on cross-platform infrastructure, and [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]]'s sock-puppet audit finding a real (if supply/demand-driven rather than overtly biased) AfD advantage in TikTok's incidental-exposure feed.

Brazil forms the collection's most politically fraught case cluster, chronicling the aftermath of democratic stress under and after Bolsonaro: [[Rodarte2026-dk]] on parliamentary epistemic-authority contests during the Manaus crisis, [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] on how electoral misinformation and messaging-app use eroded both political tolerance and institutional trust across the contested 2022 election, [[Kansaon2025-id]] and [[Ventura2025-sw]] on WhatsApp's distinctive coordinative and informational role, [[Marino2026-slef]]'s longitudinal finding that even hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro communities show volatile rather than static affective engagement, and [[Ventura2026-yc]] on the 2024 X ban's lasting partisan sorting effects. Together these papers argue that Brazil's information ecology — dominated by closed messaging infrastructure rather than open feeds — requires analytic frameworks distinct from the Facebook/Twitter-centric US literature, a point also made comparatively by [[Gaw2025-ru]] on Philippine influence-operation brokerage.

## The Populist Radical Right as a Through-Line

Running beneath the national cases is a recurring finding: whatever metric is used — media visibility ([[Iris2026-pg]]), cross-platform audience support ([[Darius2026-xl]]), algorithmic feed exposure ([[Tjaden2026-sl8e]], [[Gauthier2026-iq]]), misinformation sharing propensity ([[Tornberg2025-ir]]), or securitizing visual strategy ([[Bouchafra2026-ts]]) — radical-right and populist actors consistently receive disproportionate traction relative to conventional political strength. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary analysis crystallizes the mechanism: not populism or right-wing ideology alone, but their conjunction, predicts elite misinformation sharing, tying the phenomenon to a broader "disinformation order" and attention-driven media ecosystem rather than to any platform-neutral technological effect. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] extends this into the generative-AI era, examining synthetic multimedia in far-right 2024 EP campaigning, while [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenyan deepfake experiment shows that even AI-fabricated content's persuasive power is socially negotiated through audience commentary rather than fixed at the moment of production — a reminder that platform-level findings from Europe and the US do not automatically generalize to the Global South's information ecologies.

## Measurement, Methods, and the Sensemaking Turn

A final cluster addresses how researchers should conceptualize misinformation and partisan dynamics at all. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] reframe election-rumor research around "evidence-frame" dynamics and "deep stories," arguing that misleadingness lives in the interaction between (often accurate) evidence and partisan framing rather than in false facts per se — a theoretical move that complicates the binary true/false operationalizations underlying [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]]. [[Eady2025-vm]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] push measurement itself forward, proposing joint ideology-estimation models and multi-level "cartographies" of news circulation that avoid the oversimplified left-right, outlet-only measures critiqued throughout the collection. Methodological papers on LLM-assisted content analysis ([[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Larsson2026-ro]], [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]) mark a recent pivot toward generative-AI-in-the-loop pipelines for coding and clustering political content at scale, while reflecting on the validation challenges this introduces.

Taken together, the collection narrates a field maturing from binary questions ("did fake news elect Trump?") toward a systems-level understanding in which platform governance, algorithmic curation, elite strategy, coordinated inauthentic behavior, and audience sensemaking interact dynamically and country-specifically — with the populist radical right's persistent digital advantage, and the growing opacity of platform data access, emerging as the two most urgent unresolved problems for the field going forward.
