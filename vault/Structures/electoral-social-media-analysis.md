# Elections & Social Media Political Communication

## Italy as the recurring laboratory

The single most sustained empirical thread in this collection is a decade-long observation of Italian elections, anchored by [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], with [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] providing a pre-history from the 2013 "hybrid" talk-show/Twitter campaign. The arc moves from characterizing partisan attention (2018: insularity of M5S- and League-favored sources, [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]) to detecting the coordinated infrastructures behind that attention (coordinated link-sharing networks across 2018–2019, [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], later made continuously updatable in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] for the 2022 snap election). A parallel methodological strand asks how far LLM-based tools can be trusted to do this labeling work at scale: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] jointly validate embedding-based clustering and LLMs-in-the-loop pipelines for classifying and narratively labeling political news shared on Facebook in 2018 and 2022, arguing that expert (not crowdsourced) validation is now required because LLMs increasingly outperform low-skilled human coders. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the Meta URL Shares Dataset comparison across Italy, Germany, and France, finding untrustworthy-URL shares spiking specifically in Italian and German election years — linking the Italian case to the German cluster below. Most recently, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] closes the loop on platform accountability by using the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently throttled Italian MPs' reach starting nearly a year before its announced global rollout, with extremist accounts adapting by simply posting more. [[Paci2025-ag]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] complicate this news-centric picture from the discourse side: Italian political speech is saturated with implicature and presupposition that current LLMs still fail to parse reliably, while "fake news" accusations themselves function in the Italian press as rhetorical weapons of delegitimization rather than simple misinformation corrections — a reminder that the Italian case is as much about strategic language as about platform mechanics.

## Germany: from second screen to multi-platform infrastructure problem

The German thread is thinner but pointed. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) against specialized computer-vision models for analyzing visual self-presentation on Instagram in the 2021 federal election, finding candidate accounts foreground front-runners far more than party accounts do. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] examines political advertising and coordinated behavior around the same election. [[Schulte2026-df]] then reframes the entire enterprise for the 2025 election as an infrastructure problem rather than a substantive one: parties strategically differentiate by platform (AfD on TikTok, BSW on X), and TikTok's "politainment" style decouples textual, vocal, and facial signals — but the paper's real argument is that platform access constraints, not missing findings, are now the binding constraint on German election research, echoing the governance concerns raised elsewhere in this set.

## Brazil: crisis, coordination, and the erosion of trust

The Brazilian cluster forms the topic's most coherent narrative arc around democratic backsliding. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] opens with the Manaus healthcare collapse, showing how parliamentarians contest epistemic authority through derivative reporting, direct state-aligned claims, and local brokerage — establishing that Brazilian political elites function as media producers, not just sources. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] extend this into the 2022 election proper via a three-wave panel: belief in electoral misinformation directly fuels intolerance ([[Rossini2026-jn]]) and directly erodes trust in the results, with mainstream news use as a stabilizing counterweight and WhatsApp as an accelerant among Bolsonaro voters specifically ([[Rossini2026-mj]]). [[Kansaon2025-id]] supplies the coordination mechanics behind that WhatsApp effect, tracing 13 million messages to show how coordinated accounts moved from disseminating misinformation to mobilizing real-world protest, including the January 8 attacks. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a WhatsApp deactivation field experiment, while [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the story past Bolsonaro into the 2024 X ban, showing that platform bans in polarized democracies produce durable rightward "sorting ratchets" rather than symmetric demobilization. [[Marino2026-slef]] closes the loop by showing that even hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro Facebook communities are not stable echo chambers but volatile affective formations that reorganize sharply around the 2023 inauguration and coup attempt — a finding that pushes back against static polarization models used elsewhere in the corpus.

## The US 2020 benchmark: exposure, advertising, and null effects

A distinct cluster uses the 2020 US election as a methodological benchmark for what rigorous, platform-collaborative research can and cannot show. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] report the same large-scale audit of 49 deceptive networks reaching tens of millions of users, emphasizing that reshares by unaffiliated accounts — not direct network posting — drove most exposure, and that associations with downstream attitudes evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Allcott2025-jb]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] extend this null/limited-effects sensibility: a large ad-removal field experiment found no detectable attitudinal effects, while diffusion-tree analysis of a billion reshared Facebook posts characterizes how misinformation's virality is concentrated rather than uniform. [[Kim2026-wg]] is the sharpest counterpoint, showing that average-treatment-effect designs mask severe heterogeneous suppression effects among geo-racially targeted minority voters exposed to 2016 voter-suppression ads — a methodological caution that null-finding papers in this set should be read alongside. [[Green2025-ap]] provides a structural diagnosis underlying much of this debate: source-level partisanship scores conflate genuine moderation with story-level heterogeneity, meaning "echo chamber" findings built on domain-level data may misstate how curation actually bubbles content.

## The 2024 European Parliament elections: a cross-national stress test

A cluster converges on the 2024 EU elections as a natural comparative setting. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for the Radical Right across five countries, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks regardless of outlet orientation. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric platform-by-platform, prioritizing TikTok memes for younger audiences. [[Oprea2025-lf]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] turn to platform integrity mechanics: hyperactive/inauthentic Facebook accounts amplified Romanian party pages, while a YouTube audit finds legacy and public-service media systematically favored in search yet inconsistently labeled and opaquely moderated across EU languages. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links these visibility dynamics to public opinion, arguing far-right electoral performance itself shapes citizens' disinformation concerns — suggesting a feedback loop between the visibility findings of [[Iris2026-pg]] and mass perception.

## Platform governance, data access, and the infrastructure of knowing

A meta-layer of papers addresses the conditions of possibility for all the above research. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] map how DSA-mandated APIs and the broader "price, proficiency, permission" landscape unevenly enable election research, with [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] historicizing platform election policy itself through three phases — protection, civic-integrity expansion, and post-2022 rollback — that contextualizes the throttling documented in [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and the access frictions in [[Schulte2026-df]]. Moderation mechanisms come under direct empirical scrutiny in [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]], both examining X's Community Notes: the former shows Republicans are flagged asymmetrically more than Democrats even under a bridging algorithm, while the latter argues the same bridging design structurally undermoderates polarizing (and electorally risky) content by construction. [[Simeone2025-vo]] demonstrates the flip side — that targeted deplatforming of a handful of accounts produced network-wide "ripple" collapse of the Arizona election-audit community's authority structure, an instructive contrast to Community Notes' softer, consensus-based approach.

## Narrative, rumor, and elite strategy in contested elections

A final cluster treats election misinformation less as discrete false claims and more as narrative and strategic practice. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] develop evidence-frame and "deep story" frameworks showing how factually accurate evidence gets reframed into fraud narratives through quote-tweet conversational structure, with 2022 audiences relying on the pre-established 2020 "deep story" to interpret ambiguous new events. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] tests a related second-order effect experimentally: merely reading about interference narratives — regardless of real efficacy — asymmetrically raises affective polarization depending on which party is framed as beneficiary. At the elite level, [[Tornberg2025-ir]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] shift attention to who spreads misinformation: across 26 countries, it is specifically radical-right populism (the interaction of populism and right ideology), not either alone, that predicts elite sharing of low-factuality sources, while institutional role and ideology similarly condition US officials' misinformation-sharing. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[Gaw2025-ru]] extend the elite-strategy lens to non-elite amplifiers — social bots as agenda-builders in Ohio's 2022 midterms, and covert influence operations reconceived as political brokerage in the Philippines' 2022 election — situating the topic's Western cases within a broader global infrastructure of manipulation. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] closes this arc with a methodological corrective, arguing that outlet-level partisanship measures used throughout much of this literature oversimplify what "news sharing" actually captures, echoing and reinforcing the structural critique already raised by [[Green2025-ap]].
