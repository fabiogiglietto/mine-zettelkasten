# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## From Networked Publics to Parasocial and Post-Social Media

The anchor for this collection is a set of anniversary-style reflections asking whether "social media" still names what it once did. boyd's own foundational account of [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] networked publics—persistent, replicable, scalable, searchable—and the companion volume on [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] the networked self set the terms that almost everything downstream argues with or against. Two decades later, [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has displaced posting and that influencer content has displaced peer exchange. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] makes a structurally parallel but more architectural claim: TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media organized around algorithmically clustered publics rather than networked ones, a shift that [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes into a full "post-social media" thesis in which algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated chat together dissolve the user-generated-content-plus-social-graph paradigm. [[Helmond2026-ll]] supplies the infrastructural correlate, tracing how Meta's retirement of the Like button literalizes the abandonment of the social-graph model in favor of AI-driven inference, and warning that "platformization" itself needs re-theorizing as this AI-centered phase begins. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] write the elegiac bookend to this arc from opposite temporal vantage points: Marwick looks backward to LiveJournal's granular privacy and reciprocal intimacy as evidence that today's alienation was a political-economic choice, not a technical inevitability, while Bruns narrates Twitter's own death as the erosion—through mismanagement, commercialization, and weaponized disinformation—of a flat, open structure that had briefly supported prosocial public debate. [[Swartz2026-zb]] extends this diagnosis of decline into economic terrain, arguing that scams are not peripheral to but constitutive of the platform economy's current "scam age." Read together, these pieces trace a shared narrative arc—from reciprocal networked sociality, through algorithmic/parasocial reconfiguration, toward a fragmented, AI-saturated, economically precarious "post-social" condition—even as they disagree about causes (business model vs. interface design vs. generative AI) and about whether anything of the original promise is recoverable.

## Intimacy, Context, and the Self Across Platform Eras

A second cluster interrogates how platforms shape self-presentation and relational intimacy, forming a conceptual throughline from Goffmanian audience management to algorithmic identity flattening. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] establishes "context collapse" and the "imagined audience" as the foundational vocabulary for understanding how Twitter users negotiate many-to-many visibility; [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] shows the same dynamic operating as collaborative, public "networked intimacy" on Italian Facebook, where tagging, liking, and implicit sharing substitute for private disclosure. [[Chancellor2026-sld9]] revisits this inheritance critically, arguing that context collapse has become conceptually overstretched for algorithm-driven, consumption-oriented platforms, and proposes a structural "grammar" of user, platform, and data dimensions that yields new collapses (algorithmic, situational, identity) alongside the classical ones. [[Tiggemann2020-sl44]] offers an experimental counterpoint at the level of embodied self-presentation, showing that "Instagram vs reality" posts can neutralize the harms of appearance comparison—evidence that platform vernaculars of authenticity can be turned reflexively against the idealized imagery platforms otherwise reward.

## Affordances, Algorithms, and the Visibility Game

A large group of papers treats platform affordances not as fixed features but as sites of ongoing negotiation between users, algorithms, and owners. [[Bucher2017-qh]] inaugurates this line with the "algorithmic imaginary," showing that Facebook users' affective "algorithm stories" of profiling, surprise, and betrayal are themselves generative of algorithmic behavior. [[Cotter2019-js]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] develop complementary accounts of the resulting power struggle: Cotter reframes "gaming the system" as "playing the visibility game," emphasizing influencers' culturally inflected interpretations of opaque ranking rules, while Petre names the platforms' own moralized, asymmetric rule-making "platform paternalism." [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys the resulting literature on "algorithm literacy," finding the construct itself fragmented across the social sciences. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates empirically that affordances are relational rather than fixed: German far-right networks take radically different shapes—broadcast-like on Twitter, fringe-clustered on Telegram, hierarchical on Facebook—depending on platform-specific connectors. [[De2026-ld]] examines the discursive labor platforms perform to manage their own constant feature and policy churn ("changecraft"), while [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supply the enduring critical vocabulary—the rhetorical ambiguity of "platform," the contest between public and corporate values—against which all these affordance studies implicitly argue. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extend the affordance lens to TikTok specifically, showing how its mimetic, humor-driven format is deployed symmetrically across ideological divides on immigration in Canada and how it amplifies "legal-but-harmful" incivility among Gen Z Filipino influencers. [[Graham2026-fb]] rounds out this cluster by dissolving the human/bot binary itself, showing Reddit's "fungi" phenomenon to be a culturally negotiated performance of authenticity rather than a technical fact.

## Labour, Care, and the Influencer Economy

Running parallel to the affordance literature is a labour-centered strand examining how creators survive, instrumentalize, and are instrumentalized by platform demands. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers deploying second-hand fashion content as a survival strategy that fuses genuine care with reputational labour under algorithmic pressure. [[Divon2026-wx]] extends this logic into political brokerage, showing lifestyle influencers in Israel and China opportunistically mobilizing state-aligned narratives during crises to sustain visibility. [[Tang2026-gu]] pushes the labour question into generative AI, documenting Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable, industrialized output. Together with Cotter's and Petre's accounts of the visibility game, these studies suggest an arc in which creative labour becomes increasingly systematized, whether through human ingenuity, platform-mediated reputational capital, or algorithmic mass production.

## Communities of Suspicion: Conspiracy, Extremism, and the Far Right

The largest thematic cluster concerns how platforms host, shape, and sometimes manufacture extremist and conspiratorial publics. [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most theoretically ambitious account, arguing that the "Great Reset" conspiracy forms through platform-rewarded repetition and adjacency ("the Memeplex") rather than coherent narrative—alignment precedes story. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this with an interactionist account of the same phenomenon, showing that "Great Reset" believers across six platforms are bound less by ideology than by symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity, reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor. [[Marwick2025-ov]] disrupts the demographic stereotype of the conspiracist altogether, finding ConspiracyTok dominated by young, non-white, and female creators who build a "generous epistemology" blending standpoint knowledge with disinformation. [[Gardam2025-er]] contributes a methodological intervention tailored to this same terrain, arguing that Instagram's climate-denial discourse demands visual-first rather than text-first analysis. On the organized far right, [[Askanius2026-de]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] both trace professionalization and transnationalization: Askanius shows Swedish pandemic-era YouTubers fusing influencer branding with political propaganda and using YouTube as a gateway to alt-tech platforms, while Groebner documents a genuinely transnational Anglophone far-right audience anchored by Fox News. [[Nangle2026-yo]] identifies the affective mechanism underlying mainstreaming on Instagram specifically—humor and positive affect that "soften" extremist content—a finding that resonates with Hollingshead's and Cabbuag's observations about humor as a shared, ideologically agnostic vernacular. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces a related politicization arc from #StayWoke to "culture wars" discourse operating both within and across Twitter and YouTube. These studies converge on a shared methodological lesson, also voiced by [[Kakavand2026-kt]], that single-platform analysis cannot capture how extremist publics are actually constituted.

## Fragmentation, Polarization, and the Limits of Cross-Platform Debate

A closely related but more explicitly political-communication cluster asks whether platforms can sustain cross-ideological publics at all. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] delivers perhaps the most pointed empirical rebuttal to the "town square" ideal, showing that even discursively resonant political subreddits exhibit minimal user, source, and discourse overlap—polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition rather than an emergent outcome. [[Zhu2026-tn]] extends this relational logic across national media systems, finding that alternative-mainstream audience overlap depends less on inherent marginality than on how institutionalized populist actors are within a given media system. [[Lukito2026-il]] documents a parallel dynamic of deliberate non-overlap at the level of content strategy, showing right-leaning U.S. outlets curating distinct Twitter and Truth Social presences rather than duplicating posts. [[Wang2026-ub]] provides a cautionary case study of platform exit rather than fragmentation within a platform: even highly motivated academic Twitter users largely failed to sustain migration to Mastodon, with retention hinging on niche server communities and cross-server engagement diversity. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] frame the normative stakes of this fragmentation literature, the former asking directly whether alternative platforms drive polarization and the latter proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as theoretical antidotes to its destructive forms. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] looks forward rather than backward, surveying how large language models might be harnessed for bridging systems, collective dialogue, and community moderation to counteract exactly the fragmentation these empirical studies document. Older case studies of hashtag-based contestation—[[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] on #JeNeSuisPasCharlie as a counter-hashtag dissenting from #JeSuisCharlie, and [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] on a pro-Vietnam Facebook firestorm reframed as political protest rather than reputational crisis—anticipate this literature's central insight that online collective expression is rarely as unified as its platform-level aggregation suggests. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] adds an individual-differences perspective to the toxicity question, finding that dark personality traits predict self-reported incivility but not observable linguistic toxicity, complicating assumptions that online hostility straightforwardly reveals underlying psychology.

## Hybrid Media, Audiences, and the Shifting Meaning of News

A smaller but coherent group examines how audiences and legacy media institutions interact with platforms, often via older "hybrid media system" logics. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Giglietto2015-320951c4]] both analyze Italian second-screen Twitter practice during television broadcasts, finding narrow, TV-agenda-driven participation that nonetheless varies meaningfully by genre (political talk shows eliciting more indirect, hedged speech than entertainment). [[Hartley2006-oi]] supplies deeper historical and theoretical ballast for this hybrid-media sensibility, proposing a "cultural public sphere" to explain how private leisure identities (the Australian beach) erupt into mainstream political visibility. On the audience-reception side, [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] both reject institutional definitions of "news" in favor of audience-centered ones: Aharoni's cross-national study finds users operating with both a "core" journalistic definition and an "expanded" platform-native one, while Wedel's study of young German adults proposes "informativeness" as a more portable concept spanning influencers, friends, and journalists alike. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends this audience/institution tension into the AI era, showing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers discursively contest the meaning of AI Overviews as a redefinition of search authority itself—a concern echoed, at the level of ordinary platform users, by [[Li2026-zv]], whose Reddit-based study of folk conceptualizations of "AI slop" finds users actively theorizing the defects, algorithmic overreach, and perverse pleasures of generative content that platforms' own moderation regimes fail to capture. [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] offers a complementary theoretical reframing, arguing LLMs are better understood as "communication partners" situated in a long history of information-management tools than as autonomous AI agents—a claim that dovetails with Tornberg's post-social diagnosis of "platforms without users."

## Methods for a Fragmenting, Multimodal, Multiplatform Ecology

Finally, several papers are primarily methodological interventions responding to the empirical challenges this topic raises. [[Fan2026-af]] proposes treating digital trace data as time-evolving "user-sequences" across Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube, arguing existing cross-sectional methods lose crucial temporal and cross-platform information. [[Bruns2025-fz]] tackles a parallel representational problem with "practice mapping," using vector embeddings to escape the "hairball" limits of conventional network visualization for multimodal interactions. [[Lompe2026-vm]] makes a case for reviving URL-link analysis as a cross-platform controversy-mapping tool, showing that the same links (e.g., to mainstream news) carry different evidentiary and mobilizing functions on Twitter versus Facebook. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] is an earlier, more infrastructural proposal in this methodological vein, arguing that Internet architecture itself should be redesigned around social-capital metrics (trust, reach, influence) to accommodate the rise of the "prosumer"—a prescient anticipation of the platform-society debates that would follow. Taken as a whole, this methodological cluster reflects the volume's broader argument: as platforms fragment into algorithmic, semi-private, and AI-mediated formations, the tools long used to study "social media" as a unified object—network graphs, cross-sectional surveys, text-only discourse analysis—must themselves be retheorized, mirroring the conceptual retheorizations of context collapse, platformization, and the "social" itself that anchor this collection.
