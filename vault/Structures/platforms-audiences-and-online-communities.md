# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## Foundations: Networked Publics, Platformization, and the Hybrid Media System

The topic's intellectual spine runs back to danah boyd's foundational account of social network sites as "networked publics" [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and the companion volume theorizing the "networked self" as a performed, relational identity rather than a fixed attribute [[Slack1783508042-sl69]]. Marwick and boyd's study of "context collapse" on Twitter [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalized this theory, showing how users manage an "imagined audience" through strategies of self-censorship, audience-targeting, and micro-celebrity branding—establishing authenticity itself as a strategic performance, a theme that recurs throughout the later corpus. Farci's study of Italian Facebook users extends this lineage by showing that intimacy, too, is not private disclosure but a *networked*, collaboratively managed achievement—tagging, implicit sharing, and liking are read as low-cost relational signals that blur public/private boundaries while reinforcing community [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]. Chadwick's "hybrid media system" [[Chadwick2015-ii]] complicates any simple narrative of displacement, arguing that older and newer media logics coevolve and that power remains unevenly distributed even as participation expands—a caution echoed decades later by papers skeptical of platform-driven democratization. Van Dijck's "platform society" [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supplies the macro-frame that several contemporary papers presuppose: platforms are not neutral infrastructures but value-laden architectures contesting public goods against private interest, a thesis that anticipates Helmond's later diagnosis of "platformization" as an ongoing, AI-inflected process of making domains "platform-ready" [[Helmond2026-ll]].

## The Unraveling of "Social" Media: A Field in Retrospect

A striking cluster—largely drawn from a twentieth-anniversary reflection in *Social Media + Society*—revisits these foundational claims and finds the object of study dissolving. danah boyd argues that platforms once built on reciprocal sociality now reward scrolling over posting and parasocial attachment to influencers over mutual friendship, proposing "parasocial media" as the more accurate label [[Boyd2026-op]]. Törnberg and colleagues push this further, arguing that the entire "social media" paradigm is unraveling into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—driven by the shift from social-graph to interest-based recommendation and the rise of generative AI [[Tornberg2026-lc]]. Gerbaudo supplies the clearest mechanism for this shift, theorizing TikTok's "For You" logic as the emergence of "clustered publics"—statistically inferred neighborhoods of shared interest that replace the explicit, people-centric ties of "networked publics," with consequences of depersonalization, opacity, and fragmentation [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]. Helmond's own retrospective on platformization corroborates this empirically: Meta's retirement of the Like button signals its decoupling from the open-web social graph in favor of in-app behavioral inference and AI-driven personalization [[Helmond2026-ll]].

Marwick's autoethnographic return to LiveJournal makes the human stakes of this shift vivid, contrasting the intimacy and reciprocal writing she experienced there with the alienation and surveillance of contemporary platforms, while insisting the trajectory was contingent rather than inevitable [[Marwick2026-ss]]. Baym's revisitation of her own 2015 diagnosis of social media's four threats—inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—concludes that all four have intensified, now compounded by generative AI's appropriation of user content and the political ascent of a tech "broligarchy" [[Baym2026-tr]]. Bruns narrates the "death of Twitter" as a case study in this broader decline, arguing that Twitter's flat, open structure once enabled prosocial crisis communication and hashtag activism, but that commercial mismanagement and "participatory disinformation" eroded it, leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to replicate its form without solving its governance failures [[Bruns2026-yv]]. Swartz's essay extends the diagnosis into political economy, arguing that scams have become a central—not peripheral—organizing logic of platform capitalism, with anti-fraud infrastructures themselves becoming governance mechanisms that normalize surveillance [[Swartz2026-zb]]. Together these essays form a coherent, if polemical, argument: the "social" of social media is a historically specific and now-receding condition, not an essential property of the technology.

## Testing the Thesis: Migration, Attention Asymmetries, and False Appearances

Several empirical papers interrogate pieces of this decline thesis directly. Wang's survival analysis of academic Twitter users who attempted to migrate to Mastodon after Musk's acquisition finds that even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort largely failed to sustain the move, with field-specific servers and cross-server engagement diversity the strongest predictors of retention—evidence that decentralized alternatives struggle to replicate the social capital of legacy platforms [[Wang2026-ub]], a finding that dovetails with Bruns's skepticism about Twitter's heirs [[Bruns2026-yv]]. Oswald's account of the "production-consumption gap" complicates any reading of visible platform discourse as representative: a small minority of highly active users generates most political content, systematically distorting both citizens' and researchers' perceptions of public opinion [[Oswald2025-km]]. Schulz and colleagues sharpen this into a causal claim, showing across observational data, experiments, and a forty-country survey that ideologically stronger users self-select into posting, producing "false polarization" that overstates societal division relative to underlying attitudes [[Schulz2026-ts]]—a result that should give pause to any account (including some discussed below) that reads visible online conflict as a direct index of social reality.

## From Networks to Clusters: Algorithmic Publics in Practice

Gerbaudo's theoretical distinction between networked and clustered publics [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] finds empirical traction across several TikTok- and platform-comparative studies. Kakavand's multi-platform network analysis of the German far right demonstrates that the same actors produce structurally distinct networks on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, arguing that affordances like connectivity and scalability are relational, sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed platform properties [[Kakavand2026-kt]]—a methodological rebuke to single-platform generalization that resonates with Hollingshead's finding that TikTok's Canadian immigration discourse looks markedly different (more pro-immigration) from comparable X/Twitter studies, even as users across ideological camps deploy the platform's affordances symmetrically [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]. De and colleagues add a reflexive layer, showing how platforms themselves narrate their own architectural changes through "changecraft"—discursive strategies of visibility, continuity-framing, and incremental "patchworking" that manage perceptions of transformation while consolidating platform power [[De2026-ld]], which can be read as the rhetorical counterpart to the infrastructural shifts Helmond documents [[Helmond2026-ll]].

## TikTok as Genre: Conspiracy, Incivility, Labor, and the State

A dense cluster treats TikTok less as infrastructure than as a cultural form with recurring genres. Marwick's study of U.S. "ConspiracyTok" finds that its creators—disproportionately young, non-white, and/or female, confounding stereotypes of the conspiracy theorist—practice a "populist knowledge production" and "generous epistemology" that can simultaneously empower marginalized standpoints and circulate disinformation [[Marwick2025-ov]]. Cabbuag's account of Filipino "dogshows" documents how legal-but-harmful humor is normalized among Gen Z influencers and audiences, platform affordances actively amplifying incivility as entertainment [[Cabbuag2024-me]]. Divon's comparative study of TikTok (Israel) and Douyin (China) shows how even ostensibly apolitical lifestyle influencers become situational political brokers during crises, translating state-aligned narratives into platform-native vernaculars under both democratic and authoritarian governance regimes [[Divon2026-wx]]. Tang's study of Chinese "AI slop" creators extends the labor analysis into generative AI, showing virality itself industrialized through benchmarking, format-testing, and matrix-account systems—unsettling received notions of the "creator" [[Tang2026-gu]]. Volpe's ethnography of Italian second-hand-fashion micro-influencers rounds out this picture of platform-conditioned labor, arguing that care and ethical branding are intertwined survival strategies under dual precarity (unstable labor markets, demanding algorithmic infrastructures) rather than opposed poles of authenticity and commerce [[Volpe2026-um]]. Read together, these studies substantiate boyd's and Baym's claims about precarious, algorithmically disciplined labor [[Boyd2026-op]] [[Baym2026-tr]] with granular ethnographic and content-analytic evidence.

## Far-Right and Extremist Ecologies Across the Multi-Platform Web

A parallel body of work maps how extremist and hyperpartisan actors exploit platform-specific and cross-platform affordances. Askanius traces a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube during the pandemic, showing how guest appearances, hyperlinking, and in-video mentions weave influencers, organized groups, and alternative media into a cohesive, commercially hybridized ecosystem that uses YouTube as a "sanitized" gateway to more extreme alt-tech platforms [[Askanius2026-de]]. Groebner extends this logic transnationally, finding substantial and sustained commenter overlap among English-language hyperpartisan channels (Fox News, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, GB News), with the U.S. occupying a central hub position [[Groebner2026-pc]]—a finding anticipated by Heft's earlier hyperlink-network study showing that right-wing news sites build interlinked national and transnational ecologies, with U.S. sites as central hubs and, counterintuitively, legacy mainstream media as the most common shared transnational reference points [[Heft2021-ky]]. Balluff's entity-network analysis of German coverage of Nord Stream 2 reinforces this counterintuitive finding at a different scale: alternative media reference largely the same named entities as legacy outlets, differing instead in network structure (more cohesive, less modular) and in how often they cite other media [[Balluff2026-ev]]. Nangle's multimodal analysis of far-right Instagram Reels identifies a complementary micro-mechanism—positive affect, humor, and memetic "softening" that normalizes extremist content through algorithmic proximity to mainstream material [[Nangle2026-yo]]. Kuznetsova's network analysis of pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus shows an inverse dynamic: state-aligned coordination rather than grassroots alternative-media formation, illustrating how the same forwarding affordances that enable counter-public alliance-building elsewhere can also be harnessed for top-down regime amplification [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]. Kristensen's shorter piece on "platform polarization" asks whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization, situating these empirical ecology studies within the broader normative question of platform choice and fragmentation [[Kristensen2025-ni]].

## Conspiracy, Polarization, and the Work of Boundary-Making

A complementary strand treats polarization and conspiracy as achieved through meaning-making rather than simply technical artifacts. Grusauskaite's cross-platform study of "Great Reset" discourse argues that conspiracy culture is sustained through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, constructing a collective "we" of "critical thinkers," and binding ideologically disparate groups through affective, "deep story" narratives of shared grievance against a "mainstream other" [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], a sociological counterpoint to purely algorithmic explanations like Gerbaudo's clustering thesis [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]. Dehghan's sixteen-year study of political subreddits similarly challenges platform-deterministic accounts, finding minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar communities and theorizing polarization as "sedimented"—an entanglement of platform design, moderation, discourse, and user agency rather than a simple output of algorithmic sorting [[Dehghan2026-sy]]. Gardam's visual-first methodology for climate-denial discourse on Instagram responds to a related gap, arguing that image-centric conspiracy narratives demand analytic frameworks that foreground visual rhetoric rather than treating images as secondary to text [[Gardam2025-er]]. Zhu's comparative audience-overlap-network study across six European countries nuances the picture further: alternative-mainstream audience overlap is generally low, but context-dependent—Sweden's strong professional journalism combined with the political marginalization of populist actors produces an isolated alternative "enclave," while countries where far-right parties enjoy institutionalized political access show more blurred boundaries [[Zhu2026-tn]]. Giglietto's earlier case study of the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag offers a precedent for this boundary-work literature, documenting how dissenting voices articulate disagreement without endorsing violence within a moment of mass platformed solidarity [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], while Holland-Levin's study of #StayWoke's drift into "culture wars" framing traces a parallel trajectory of politicization operating both within and synergistically across Twitter and YouTube [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]]. Slack's case study of a pro-Vietnam Facebook "firestorm" against China's digital embassy in Italy warns against treating such events as a uniform genre, showing that superficially similar online collective outbursts can stem from entirely different sociological logics—commercial reputational crisis versus coordinated political protest [[Slack1783507463-sl24]].

## Authenticity, Toxicity, and the Limits of Automated Inference

Running beneath these studies is a persistent question about what, exactly, online behavior reveals about the people (or non-people) producing it. Graham's study of Reddit "botting" via the viral "Anus_Fungi" mushroom-emoji phenomenon argues that the human/bot binary is analytically unstable: what looked like automated spam was largely human imitation embedded in participatory culture, with authenticity emerging as a community-negotiated performance rather than a technical property [[Graham2026-fb]]—a finding that complicates any assumption that visible platform activity straightforwardly indexes either human intention or bot coordination (compare Kuznetsova's coordinated-channel detection [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]). Cerulli's linkage of validated dark-personality questionnaires to real Reddit activity finds a similarly humbling gap: dark traits predict self-reported engagement in incivility but not computationally detected toxicity or linguistic markers, suggesting current text-based toxicity proxies capture something more superficial than underlying personality [[Cerulli2026-sl75]]. Arminio's methodological contribution—using vision-language models to generate connotative textual descriptions of Instagram images for clustering—directly addresses this inferential gap for visual content, substantially improving semantic cluster quality and interpretability over CNN-based pipelines, with clear relevance to studying memetic and ideologically coded imagery like that analyzed by Gardam and Nangle [[Arminio2025-tw]] [[Gardam2025-er]] [[Nangle2026-yo]]. Gagrcin's integrative review of "algorithm literacy" scholarship surveys the fragmented conceptual landscape researchers have used to understand how users comprehend the very algorithmic systems (à la Gerbaudo's clustered publics) that shape their platform experience [[Gagrcin2024-dl]].

## News, Audiences, and the Boundaries of the Informative

A distinct but related strand asks how ordinary users, rather than professionals, define news and information on audience-driven platforms. Aharoni's four-country study finds that users hold both a stable "core" definition of news (political, negative, journalistically formatted, timely) and an "expanded" digital definition admitting entertaining, personally relevant, non-journalistic content—revealing a growing gap between public and professional conceptions of news [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]]. Wedel's parallel study of young German adults proposes "informativeness" as a more audience-centered alternative concept, structured along five dimensions including platform-specific cues and perceived expertise rather than institutional credentialing [[Wedel2026-ix]]. Both studies extend the audience-reception tradition into the platformized, algorithmically curated feeds theorized by Gerbaudo and Törnberg, showing empirically how "news-ness" and "informativeness" are renegotiated once content circulates outside journalistic institutions [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] [[Tornberg2026-lc]].

## AI as the Next Platform Frontier

The corpus closes by turning toward generative AI as both an emerging medium and a structural force reshaping platform publics, picking up threads from Helmond's AI-centered platformization and Törnberg's "post-social" AI-mediated communication [[Helmond2026-ll]] [[Tornberg2026-lc]]. Manovich analyzes generative AI as a creative medium in its own right—"a medium that thinks"—with built-in media cognition, a structural trade-off between variability and precise control, and an aesthetically conservative, "make it old" logic that privileges statistically dominant training-data conventions over avant-garde novelty [[Manovich2026-ih]]. Ng and colleagues argue, from a more technical register, that agentic AI systems operating in social environments require social theory as a "structural prior," formalizing multi-agent social systems and empirically validating structural dynamics (heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) using data from an AI-only social network [[Ng2026-og]]—a speculative extension of platform-and-community theory to a near-future in which the "audience" may itself be synthetic. Goldberg's position paper, emerging from a civil-society convening, surveys how large language models might reshape "digital public squares" through collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community-driven moderation, and proof-of-humanity mechanisms, explicitly weighing the promise of more deliberative, decentralized platforms against the risk of deepening societal division [[Goldberg2026-eb]]. Read against the collapse-of-the-social narratives of boyd, Törnberg, and Baym, these AI-oriented papers suggest the field's next task: determining whether AI-mediated infrastructures will further hollow out reciprocal sociality or offer genuine architectural alternatives to the engagement-optimized, parasocial platforms the rest of this literature diagnoses as already in decline.
