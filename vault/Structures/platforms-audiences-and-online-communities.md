# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Arc of Platform Transformation

A strong throughline in this collection is a historical narrative about what "social" media used to mean and what it has become. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] supply the foundational vocabulary—boyd's "networked publics," organized around persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability, and the "networked self" as a performative, relational achievement of platform affordances. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] extends this into the mechanics of self-presentation under "context collapse," showing how Twitter users conjure an imagined audience and manage authenticity as a strategic performance rather than an intrinsic quality. These early-2010s accounts describe a web still organized around explicit interpersonal connection.

The anniversary essays in this set—written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth year—stage a collective reckoning with how far that world has receded. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that "social media" has become a misnomer: scrolling has displaced posting, algorithmically curated and professionalized content has displaced peer exchange, and one-sided parasocial attachment has displaced mutual friendship, warranting the term "parasocial media." [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis from a more intimate angle, contrasting LiveJournal's reciprocal, vulnerable, granularly-filtered sociality with the alienation and surveillance of contemporary platforms, insisting the current condition was a political-economic choice, not an inevitability. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this shift a structural name: the move from "networked publics" (people-centric, explicit, visible) to "clustered publics" (item-centric, algorithmically inferred, opaque), with TikTok as the paradigm case and "TikTokification" as its diffusion mechanism across the rest of the ecosystem. [[Helmond2026-ll]] historicizes the same arc at the level of infrastructure, revisiting her own coinage of "platformization" to argue that Meta's retirement of the Like button marks the end of the open-web social-graph paradigm in favor of AI-driven inference—positioning the current moment as the opening of an "AI-centered" phase of platformization reaching toward space-based compute. Read together, these five pieces move from Web 2.0's connective sociality, through algorithmic clustering, to a parasocial, AI-infrastructural present—an arc this whole topic area implicitly narrates.

## Loss, Nostalgia, and the Political Economy of Decline

Several papers extend this narrative of decline into specific platform biographies. [[Bruns2026-yv]] traces Twitter's rise as a uniquely open, flat, prosocial space for crisis reporting and hashtag activism, and its "enshittification" under commercial pressure and Musk's acquisition, arguing that Threads, Bluesky, and Mastodon largely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures—a diagnosis empirically corroborated by [[Wang2026-ub]], whose survival analysis of academic early adopters finds that even a highly motivated, well-networked migrating cohort mostly drifted back to Twitter/X, with retention hinging on niche server identity and cross-server diversity rather than internal cohesion. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] poses the adjacent question of whether alternative platforms actually reduce or merely relocate discursive polarization. Gillespie's foundational analysis of platform rhetoric, [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], underwrites much of this skepticism: platforms deploy the ambiguity of the very word "platform" to claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions, a discursive sleight-of-hand that [[De2026-ld]] shows persisting into the present under the label "changecraft"—Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok narrating infrastructural and ideological changes as continuity, creator benevolence, or democratization, thereby masking consolidation and deepening dependency. Castells' macro-theoretical account of the "network society" ([[Slack1783507648-sl94]]) supplies the deep structural backdrop against which all of these platform-specific stories of consolidation, financialization, and inequality play out.

## Algorithmic Mainstreaming of Extremism and Misinformation

A substantial cluster examines how platform affordances enable the normalization and circulation of extremist or misleading content, largely through TikTok and Instagram's clustered-public logics. [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right Instagram Reels using positive affect and humor to soften extremist worldviews within an "affective-discursive ecology." [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist supporters exploiting TikTok's participatory vernacular—audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes—to embed "everyday extremism" beneath moderation thresholds, explicitly noting these tactics recur across ideologically disparate communities. [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a parallel dynamic on YouTube, where Swedish far-right influencers fuse microcelebrity branding and monetization with propaganda, using the platform as a "sanitised" gateway funneling audiences toward alt-tech spaces. [[Bosch2024-hj]] adds a distinctly platform-specific mechanism—sound—as an under-examined vector of Ukraine-war disinformation on TikTok, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats deliberately tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric to platform-specific audiences, concentrating memetic content on TikTok to court younger voters using footage decontextualized from outside Sweden and the EU. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates the demographic assumptions behind this literature, showing that ConspiracyTok's creators are disproportionately young, non-white, and female, deploying a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering (standpoint knowledge) and vector for disinformation. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes back against purely platform-affordance explanations altogether, arguing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through algorithmic sorting than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as identity, converting personal hardship into shared narrative—consistent across six otherwise very different platforms. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extends the incivility strand to a non-Western case, showing how Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" normalize legal-but-harmful humor as collaborative platform practice. Together these papers suggest that TikTok-style clustering (per [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) does not merely surface extremist content passively but is actively gamed, softened, and vernacularized by strategic actors—while [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] reminds us that identity work, not just algorithms, does much of the binding.

Misinformation and toxicity research in this set moves beyond content-centric framings toward audience- and source-centered accounts. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] finds that Australians' everyday misinformation encounters are dominated by mainstream/alternative news headlines rather than fringe sources, redirecting attention toward "clickbait concern" and news-ecology decline rather than platform manipulation alone. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that influencer-sourced brand misinformation generates more, and qualitatively different, toxicity than identical content from ordinary users—toxicity as a source-amplified rather than purely content-driven phenomenon, converting parasocial credibility into collective antagonism. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates assumptions about conspiracy believers' distrust, finding they rate "balanced news chatbots" more favorably than low-conspiracy users. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] round out this cluster methodologically: the former stressing that single-platform (Twitter/Facebook) studies of partisan news engagement understate ecosystem heterogeneity, the latter synthesizing the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy itself.

## Identity, Intimacy, and Community Formation

Running counter to the extremism and misinformation literature is a smaller but important strand asking how platforms sustain ordinary intimacy and belonging. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] shows Italian Facebook users achieving "networked intimacy" through public, collaborative disclosure practices (showing rather than telling, tagging, liking) that depend on friends' cooperation—intimacy as community-sustaining rather than narcissistic performance, a claim that resonates with, yet historically precedes, [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s elegy for LiveJournal-era reciprocity. [[Volpe2026-um]] examines a more precarious, professionalized register of platform sociality: Italian micro-influencers whose second-hand-fashion "care" narratives are simultaneously genuine community orientation and reputational survival strategy under algorithmic production demands—illustrating how platform culture (per [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Boyd2026-op]]) reshapes even ostensibly ethical creator labor into parasocial content performance. [[Wedel2026-ix]] investigates how young adults decode "informativeness" across YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok, finding that journalistic norms are extended to influencers judged by expertise and relatability rather than institutional credentials—evidence that platform-specific selection logics (self-selected, actor-mediated, algorithmic) shape not just what circulates but what audiences consider worth trusting.

## Formal and Structural Dynamics of Platformed Interaction

A final cluster turns from culture to network structure and method. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds minimal cross-ideological overlap across political subreddits, arguing polarization on Reddit is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, design, and discourse rather than derivable from discourse alone—directly complicating any simple "algorithm did it" story. [[Graham2026-fb]] destabilizes the human/bot binary itself through Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated, community-evaluated performance rather than a technical property—a methodological cousin to [[Cerulli2026-sl75]], which finds that dark personality traits correlate with self-reported incivility but not with computationally detected toxicity, exposing a gap between trait and textual signal. [[Smith2025-kc]] and [[Bruns2025-fz]] contribute to network-structural theorizing and methodological innovation respectively: the former identifying "attention brokerage" as a causal mechanism behind triad transitivity via amplification, the latter proposing "practice mapping" via vector embeddings to escape the interpretive limits of hairball-style network visualizations. [[Fan2026-af]] pushes methodological innovation further, arguing communication research has neglected the temporal, sequential texture of digital trace data and demonstrating six computational approaches (sequence analysis, HMMs, process mining, embeddings) on cross-platform donated trace data. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] serve as earlier historical markers in this methods-and-structure lineage—the former anticipating "prosumer" network architecture debates, the latter an early case study of hashtag counter-discourse (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) that prefigures the boundary-work and dissent dynamics later elaborated by [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Dehghan2026-sy]].

Across all these strands, the collection traces a single overarching movement: from platforms imagined and analyzed as networked publics built on explicit ties and reciprocal intimacy, toward a present of clustered, parasocial, algorithmically-managed publics whose cultures of care, extremism, misinformation, and community are all, in different ways, adaptations to that underlying structural shift.
