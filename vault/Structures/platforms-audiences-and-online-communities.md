# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Unraveling of "Social": Platform Transformation and Its Discontents

A striking number of papers filed here converge on a single diagnostic claim: the thing scholars have called "social media" for two decades is dissolving, and the concept needs replacement or serious qualification. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes "parasocial media" to capture the shift from reciprocal peer exchange to one-sided consumption of influencer content; [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes a shift from "networked publics" to "clustered publics" organized around algorithmically inferred interest rather than explicit social ties; and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] synthesizes these threads into a call for "post-social media studies," identifying three emergent formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—that no longer fit the old paradigm. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own foundational concept of "platformization" a decade on, arguing it has been diluted by overuse just as platforms pivot from social-graph logics toward AI-centered infrastructure (illustrated by Meta's retirement of the Like button). These pieces share a historical self-consciousness: they are written as retrospective, often elegiac, interventions marking an inflection point, and several appeared together in anniversary reflections for *Social Media + Society*, alongside [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s autoethnographic account of LiveJournal-era intimacy and [[Swartz2026-zb]]'s claim that "scam" has become a central organizing logic of platform economies. Read together, these essays stage a generational argument: the first-generation "networked publics" framework associated with [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] described a world of explicit friending and reciprocal visibility; that world, these authors argue, is gone or going.

## Affordances, Algorithms, and the Architecture of Attention

A second cluster treats platform affordances not as background conditions but as objects of direct empirical analysis. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] remains a touchstone, showing how the very term "platform" performs discursive work that obscures editorial power—a lineage extended by [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft," which shows how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate their own policy and feature changes to mask power consolidation. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supplies the broader theoretical scaffolding, arguing that platforms do not merely reflect society but constitute it, embedding contestable values into infrastructure. Several papers operationalize affordance theory empirically: [[Kakavand2026-kt]] shows that far-right networks in Germany take structurally different shapes on Facebook, Twitter, Telegram, YouTube, and Instagram depending on each platform's connectivity and scalability affordances, arguing affordances are relational rather than fixed; [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds that pro- and anti-immigration TikTok users deploy the platform's mimetic and interactive features in strikingly similar ways, challenging assumptions that affordances inherently favor one ideological valence. [[Fan2026-af]] pushes methodology itself to catch up with this granularity, proposing computational techniques for analyzing timestamped "user-sequences" across Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube rather than flattening behavior into cross-sectional snapshots.

Running beneath these affordance studies is a persistent question about algorithmic experience and literacy. [[Bucher2017-qh]] established that users encounter algorithms affectively, through "algorithmic imaginaries" built from moments of surprise and breakdown, a framework [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] formalizes into the broader construct of "algorithm literacy." [[Cotter2019-js]] and [[Petre2019-zh]] extend this into the labor of visibility itself: Cotter reframes "gaming the system" as "playing the visibility game," showing influencers tactically interpreting opaque rules, while Petre names the platforms' moralized punishment of those same tactics "platform paternalism." [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Divon2026-wx]] carry this logic into contemporary creator economies, showing how micro-influencers absorb algorithmic demands into performances of ethical care (second-hand fashion) or political brokerage (state-aligned content during crises on TikTok and Douyin)—demonstrating that visibility labor is now inseparable from ideological and reputational positioning.

## Echo Chambers, Polarization, and the Limits of Algorithmic Determinism

A substantial empirical literature here interrogates—and often complicates—claims that platforms straightforwardly produce polarization or radicalization. The foundational pieces, [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Messing2014-jc]], established early that Facebook's algorithm plays a smaller role in limiting cross-cutting exposure than users' own click behavior, and that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues. This skepticism toward strong algorithmic-determinist accounts recurs decades later: [[Brown2026-br]] finds no evidence that YouTube's recommender independently drives radicalization pathways, only mild, user-driven echo chamber effects and genuine "content rabbit holes"; [[Schulz2026-ts]] argues that apparent online polarization is largely an artifact of self-selection into posting by already-committed partisans rather than a true attitudinal divide. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates the picture further at the community level, showing that political subreddits exhibit minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar communities—polarization here is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, platform design, and discourse rather than reducible to any single cause. Meanwhile [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] insist on cross-platform and cross-national comparison as a corrective to Twitter/Facebook-centric assumptions, with Zhu finding that alternative-mainstream media overlap depends heavily on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] offers a more unsettling counterpoint via sock-puppet audit: non-partisan German TikTok users are disproportionately exposed to pro-AfD content, though the authors attribute this to supply-side volume and virality rather than deliberate algorithmic favoritism—illustrating how "no algorithmic bias" findings can still coexist with real asymmetries in exposure.

## Platform Vernaculars of Extremism, Conspiracy, and Incivility

A dense set of papers trace how far-right, conspiratorial, and uncivil discourse is produced, normalized, and circulated through platform-specific vernaculars rather than simply "spreading." [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows Instagram far-right content relying on humor and positive affect for normalization; [[Rieder2026-pp]] documents how Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persists on YouTube through decentralized remix and interview ecosystems that outpace moderation; [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a Swedish "alternative influence network" fusing micro-celebrity branding with pandemic-era conspiracy and anti-vaccine content. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates stereotypes of who produces conspiratorial content, finding ConspiracyTok dominated by young, non-white, and female creators engaged in "populist knowledge production." [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes back against purely platform-deterministic accounts of conspiracy culture by foregrounding symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity in "Great Reset" discourse across six platforms—a reminder that identity-construction, not just algorithmic sorting, sustains these communities. Adjacent studies examine incivility and toxicity as emergent cultural practices: [[Cabbuag2024-me]] on Filipino TikTok "dogshows," [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] on how influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct toxic echo chambers, and [[Graham2026-fb]] on Reddit "botting" as a participatory, culturally negotiated practice that destabilizes clean human/bot binaries. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] extend this terrain into authoritarian and alternative-platform contexts, examining coordinated Telegram amplification and discursive polarization on alternative platforms respectively.

## Migration, Fragmentation, and the Search for Alternatives

Several papers track what happens when users attempt to leave dominant platforms or when platform ecosystems fragment. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's broader "death" as a space for public debate, arguing successor platforms like Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky largely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] substantiates this skepticism empirically: even a highly motivated cohort of academic early-adopters largely failed to sustain migration to Mastodon, with retention driven by discipline-specific servers and cross-server engagement rather than internal network strength. [[Lukito2026-il]] examines a different kind of fragmentation—right-leaning outlets' selective "cross-platformization" strategies across Twitter and Truth Social—while [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces how social justice discourse becomes politicized both within and across Twitter and YouTube. Together these studies suggest that platform-switching and multi-platform strategy are not simply technical migrations but reconstitutions of community, audience, and discursive practice that rarely replicate what was left behind.

## Audience Epistemologies: Defining News, Information, and Legitimacy

A smaller but coherent set of papers shifts attention to how ordinary users, rather than institutions, define legitimate content. [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] finds users across four countries operating with both a "core" journalistic definition of news and an "expanded" digital definition incorporating entertainment, personal relevance, and non-journalistic sources. [[Wedel2026-ix]] develops a parallel audience-centered concept, "informativeness," to describe how young adults evaluate YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok content as useful independent of journalistic certification. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends this epistemic contestation to the infrastructural level, showing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers discursively battle over the meaning and legitimacy of AI Overviews as a redefinition of search itself. [[Wan2026-ai]] connects audience agency to psychological disposition, examining how political efficacy conditions selection behavior within algorithmic feeds. These papers collectively insist that platform studies cannot rest on institutional or technical definitions of information quality without attending to how audiences themselves negotiate categories like news, expertise, and authenticity.

## Intimacy, Identity, and Self-Presentation: The Older Lineage

Underpinning much of the above is a foundational lineage concerned with self-presentation and relational intimacy under conditions of networked visibility. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] established the concept of "context collapse" and the "imagined audience" as central mechanisms by which users navigate Twitter's many-to-many structure, a framework indebted to [[Slack1783508184-sld3]]'s theorization of networked publics' affordances (persistence, searchability, scalability) and extended in the edited volume [[Slack1783508042-sl69]]. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers a complementary account of "networked intimacy" on Italian Facebook, showing how practices like tagging, implicit sharing, and liking constitute collaborative, public-facing intimacy work rather than narcissistic self-display. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] extend this early-2010s moment into hybrid media and hashtag-based dissent, respectively, documenting how Twitter's open architecture structured—and limited—forms of political and civic participation that later papers in this collection suggest have since receded. [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] rounds out this thread by showing that even superficially similar collective phenomena like "firestorms" can mask entirely different sociological logics (reputational crisis versus coordinated political protest), a caution against treating platform-native phenomena as uniform categories.

## Coda: AI as the Next Platform Horizon

Finally, several papers gesture toward where platform studies is heading next, as generative AI reshapes both content production and the public sphere itself. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys LLM-enabled possibilities for more deliberative digital public squares (collective dialogue, bridging systems, AI-assisted moderation) while cataloguing their risks. [[Tang2026-gu]] documents the industrialization of "AI slop" production in China, where virality itself becomes an engineered, systematically managed output. [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] offers a media-theoretical reframing of LLMs as "communication partners" rather than intelligent agents, situating them within older histories of information infrastructure. Read against [[Tornberg2026-lc]] and [[Helmond2026-ll]], these pieces suggest that the questions this topic has long asked of platforms, audiences, and communities—who is visible, to whom, and by what logic—are now being reposed for an infrastructure in which human audiences may no longer be the primary counterparties at all.
