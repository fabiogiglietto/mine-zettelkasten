# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Unraveling of "Social Media" as a Concept

A striking feature of this year's crop of papers is how many are not empirical studies of platforms but interventions in the vocabulary used to describe them. Three anniversary essays for *Social Media + Society* converge on a shared diagnosis: the term "social media" itself has become inadequate. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues platforms have become "parasocial media," dominated by one-sided attention to influencers rather than reciprocal peer exchange; [[Marwick2026-ss]] contrasts the intimate, reciprocal culture of early LiveJournal with the alienating extractive present, insisting the current configuration was never inevitable but the product of political-economic choices; and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] goes furthest, proposing that "social media" as a unifying paradigm has actually dissolved into three "post-social" formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. These essays share evidence: declining posting rates, rising scroll-only consumption, and the primacy of recommendation over the social graph. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the structural theory underlying this shift, distinguishing first-generation "networked publics" (organized by explicit interpersonal connection) from TikTok-style "clustered publics" (organized by algorithmically inferred interest similarity)—a distinction that [[Helmond2026-ll]] extends by reading Meta's 2026 retirement of the Like button as the symbolic end of the social-graph paradigm and the start of an AI-centered phase of "platformization." Together these pieces suggest a field-wide reckoning: the tools built to study "networked publics" and "participatory culture" (foundational statements like [[Slack1783508184-sld3]], [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], [[Marwick2011-sl02]], and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] all cited as ancestral) may no longer fit what platforms have become. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the same unsettling further, arguing that scams—not sociality—are becoming social media's central organizing logic, with anti-fraud infrastructure now a key mechanism of platform governance.

## Algorithms, Feeds, and the Contested Politics of Amplification

A second cluster interrogates, empirically, what recommender systems actually do to political discourse—often reaching genuinely discrepant conclusions. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed causally shifts attitudes rightward and, crucially, that this effect *persists* even after the algorithm is switched off, because users have already followed new conservative accounts—a mechanism that helps reconcile the earlier, more null-leaning [[Nyhan2023-gb]] Facebook study, where reducing like-minded exposure produced no measurable attitudinal change. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates the amplification story further: right-leaning visibility gains on pre-X Twitter largely wash out once one controls for "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk himself, suggesting behavioral dynamics rather than partisan bias per se. [[Brown2026-br]]'s YouTube audit similarly disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects, finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization effect—though a platform-wide conservative tilt persists regardless of user identity. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment reframes the causal pathway yet again: engagement-based feeds amplify moralized, emotional content and distort *perceived social norms* around political dialogue (rather than directly polarizing attitudes), and a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without denting platform enjoyment. Underlying all of this is a methodological throughline traceable to [[Barbera2015-fw]], whose foundational finding that online echo chambers are topic- and time-contingent rather than fixed is echoed, decades later, in [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s finding that Reddit political polarization is not simply discursive but "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and user practice, and in [[Schulz2026-ts]]'s argument that much apparent polarization is an artifact of self-selected posting rather than genuine attitudinal division. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] extend this conversation normatively, the latter proposing "deliberative reciprocity" as an antidote to what it calls destructive polarization, while [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys a next-generation toolkit—LLM-based bridging systems, collective dialogue platforms, community moderation—aimed at engineering better public squares.

## Platform-Specific Cultures: TikTok, Facebook, and the Textures of Affect

Running alongside the macro-diagnoses is a body of work attentive to how specific platforms cultivate distinctive affective and discursive registers. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] both foreground TikTok's affordances of mimesis, sound, and interest-clustering, with the latter finding that immigration discourse on Canadian TikTok skews pro-immigration and that ideologically opposed users deploy the platform's affordances in symmetrical ways—a "shared vernacular" also visible in [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of "dogshow" incivility among Filipino Gen Z influencers and in [[Bosch2024-hj]]'s attention to sound as a distinctively TikTok-native propaganda technique around the Ukraine invasion. Facebook, by contrast, remains the terrain of "Reactions" research: [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both show that "Angry" reactions predict sharing while "Love" dampens it, with populist actors especially adept at eliciting anger—a pattern that [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates by showing that even within a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro network, affective engagement is temporally volatile rather than stable, spiking around discrete political shocks rather than reflecting fixed ideological echo-chambering. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s earlier ethnography of Italian Facebook users offers a different register entirely, theorizing "networked intimacy" as a collaborative, audience-dependent practice of disclosure (tagging, implicit sharing, liking) that reframes Facebook sociality as community-sustaining rather than narcissistic—an argument that resonates uneasily with [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s elegy for a lost intimacy culture and with [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s classic account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience, which already anticipated how platform architecture forces strategic, branded self-presentation.

## Influencers, Micro-Celebrity, and Platformed Labour

A related thread examines how ordinary users and micro-influencers labour within platform demands. [[Volpe2026-um]]'s study of Italian second-hand-fashion micro-influencers shows how care, ethics, and self-branding become inseparable survival strategies under algorithmic pressure—a dynamic that generalizes the "aspirational labour" literature invoked since [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. [[Tang2026-gu]]'s account of Chinese "AI slop" creators pushes this further, describing virality itself as an engineered, benchmarked production process via matrix-account systems, destabilizing what counts as a "creator" at all—a question also raised more speculatively by [[Manovich2026-ih]], who treats generative AI as a medium with its own cognitive logic rather than a social threat. [[Askanius2026-de]]'s mapping of the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube shows the same fusion of microcelebrity branding, monetisation, and political propaganda, while [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s study of "ConspiracyTok" demonstrates how platform affordances (greenscreen, curated evidence) let young, disproportionately non-White creators construct a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and vector for disinformation. [[Wedel2026-ix]] takes the audience side of this same phenomenon, showing that young adults extend journalistic norms of "informativeness" to precisely these non-journalistic actors, judging expertise and usefulness over institutional credentialing—a finding that dovetails with [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]]'s cross-national study of how ordinary users, not journalists, police the boundary of what counts as "news" at all.

## Coordinated and Extremist Communities Across the Multi-Platform Ecosystem

Several papers turn from single-platform snapshots toward multi-platform ecologies of coordinated far-right and conspiratorial communities. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] finds that German far-right actors do not simply replicate their networks across platforms but adapt distinctively to each platform's affordances—Twitter as scalable broadcast, Facebook as replicable hierarchy, Instagram as reciprocal echo chamber. [[Groebner2026-pc]] extends this transnationally, showing sustained commenter overlap between US, Australian, Canadian, and UK hyperpartisan YouTube channels, with Fox News as an outsized hub. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions of alternative-media divergence by showing German right-wing outlets reference largely the same named entities as legacy media, differing instead in relational network structure. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] both push past purely structural accounts toward affect and identity: the former shows how far-right Instagram content uses humor and "affective-discursive ecologies" to normalize extremism, while the latter demonstrates that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through platform-specific mechanics than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming stigma, sharing "deep stories" of grievance—remarkably consistent across six different platforms. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] round out this cluster with cases of explicit coordination (pro-government Telegram amplification) and counter-hashtag dissent (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie), while [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that misinformation's toxic uptake depends heavily on *source* (influencer vs. regular user) rather than content alone.

## Migration, Decline, and the Search for Alternatives

Finally, a set of papers directly addresses platform exit and succession. [[Wang2026-ub]] documents the largely failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon, finding that internal network cohesion could not substitute for discoverability and diverse cross-server engagement. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's broader "death" as a space for public debate, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) risk merely cloning Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. This concern for what comes *after* is where the volume's reflective and empirical strands meet most explicitly: [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] gesture toward small-scale, care-oriented, non-scaled infrastructures as the genuine alternative to platform decay, while [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s foundational framing of the "platform society" reminds us that these are never merely technical questions of feed design but contests over public versus private value that long predate the current wave of anniversary reckonings. Methodologically, this cluster is served by new analytic toolkits—[[Fan2026-af]]'s temporal "user-sequence" mining across multiple platforms, [[Bruns2025-fz]]'s "practice mapping" for multimodal interaction—that aim to keep pace with a media environment whose basic unit of analysis, the stable "platform" itself, is everywhere in question.
