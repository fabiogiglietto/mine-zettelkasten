# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Line of Argument: From Networked Publics to Post-Social Media

This body of work traces an arc that begins with foundational accounts of social network sites as spaces of identity, intimacy, and community, and ends in a moment of self-conscious crisis about whether "social media" remains a coherent object of study at all. Read together, the papers filed here narrate the rise, transformation, and partial unraveling of a two-decade paradigm.

### Foundations: Networked Publics and the Performed Self

The theoretical bedrock is laid by [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], which establish social network sites as "networked publics"—structurally distinct from face-to-face publics through affordances like persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability, and as sites where identity is performed rather than fixed. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] extends this into the problem of "context collapse," showing how Twitter users manage an imagined, unknowable audience through strategic self-presentation and micro-celebrity practices—already anticipating the tension between authenticity and self-branding that recurs throughout later work (e.g., [[Volpe2026-um]]). [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the crucial critical vocabulary for this era, dissecting how the term "platform" itself performs ideological work, allowing intermediaries to claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions—an argument whose descendants appear in [[De2026-ld]] and [[Helmond2026-ll]]. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] round out this early stratum with empirical studies of Facebook intimacy and Twitter-as-second-screen, documenting the collaborative, community-sustaining possibilities of first-generation platforms.

### The Elegy: What Was Lost

A striking cluster of anniversary essays—commissioned to mark *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth year—looks backward with something between nostalgia and alarm. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] both revisit their own histories (LiveJournal fieldwork; a 2015 manifesto) to argue that the intimacy, reciprocity, and prosocial potential of the "old internet" have been supplanted by extraction, surveillance, and concentrated wealth ("broligarchy"). [[Boyd2026-op]] delivers the most pointed terminological intervention, proposing "parasocial media" to name a shift from peer reciprocity to one-sided consumption of professionalized content. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates this same decline through the specific case of Twitter/X, arguing that mismanagement, weaponized disinformation, and Musk's acquisition destroyed a uniquely valuable open network structure, while successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) merely clone its form without repairing its governance failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] provides granular empirical confirmation of this failure-to-replace thesis: even a highly motivated cohort of academic early adopters largely could not sustain migration to Mastodon, undermining hopes that decentralized alternatives can simply inherit displaced communities.

### The Algorithmic Turn: From Graphs to Clusters

A second major thread documents the mechanism behind this transformation: the shift from social-graph-based circulation to algorithmic, interest-based recommendation. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] offers the clearest theorization, contrasting first-generation "networked publics" with TikTok's "clustered publics"—item-centric, opaque, behaviorally-inferred groupings that produce depersonalization and "silosociality." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] escalates this into a full paradigm challenge, arguing that "social media" itself is dissolving into three post-social formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, AI-mediated communication), citing Meta's own data that only 7–17% of feed time involves followed content. [[Helmond2026-ll]] tracks the same shift from the industry side, showing how Facebook's retirement of the Like button signals decoupling from social-graph signals in favor of AI-driven inference, while platformization itself migrates toward "Big AI" infrastructure. [[De2026-ld]] examines how platforms discursively manage these disruptive pivots—"changecraft"—framing ideological rupture as continuity and infrastructural change as aesthetic progress. Empirically, Brown2026-af provides a methodologically rigorous corrective to popular radicalization narratives, finding strong "rabbit hole" effects on YouTube but little evidence that algorithms independently drive echo chambers or radicalization beyond user choice—a finding that resonates with [[Schulz2026-ts]]'s argument that much apparent polarization online is an artifact of self-selected posting rather than algorithmic sorting or genuine attitudinal divergence.

### Platform Vernaculars and Creator Labor

A substantial cluster examines how specific platform affordances shape cultural production and creator practice. [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] both interrogate the blurred line between authentic practice and strategic production under algorithmic demand—Italian micro-influencers performing care through second-hand fashion, Chinese "AI slop" creators engineering virality as a managed industrial process. [[Graham2026-fb]] complicates the human/bot binary through Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property, a theme also touched by [[Cerulli2026-sl75]]'s finding that dark personality traits predict self-reported toxic behavior but not machine-detectable textual toxicity. [[Wedel2026-ix]] shifts focus to audience reception, proposing "informativeness" as a concept for how young adults evaluate content across YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok, decoupled from institutional journalism norms—while [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] finds Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting differentiated trust regimes for AI depending on its institutional framing. [[Fan2026-af]] offers a methodological bridge across this cluster, proposing computational techniques for capturing the temporal, cross-platform texture of everyday platform-switching behavior.

### Platform-Specific Extremism, Polarization, and Disinformation

The largest empirical cluster examines how platform architectures shape political extremism, polarization, and disinformation, often through explicit cross-platform comparison. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] most directly operationalizes the affordance framework, showing that German far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks—broadcast-style on Twitter, cohesive party hierarchies on Facebook, fragmented echo chambers on Instagram—demonstrating that affordances are relational outcomes rather than fixed technical properties. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] extend this comparative logic to TikTok specifically, finding that authoritarian populist and immigration discourse take memetic, affectively-charged forms distinct from text-heavy Facebook/X content. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] examine mainstreaming mechanisms—Instagram's "affective-discursive ecologies" of humor and softening, YouTube's hybridized "alternative influence network" fusing activism with influencer commercialism—while [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows how deplatforming (Andrew Tate) fails to contain "ambient ideology" because recommendation-driven circulation outpaces moderation. [[Groebner2026-pc]] documents the transnationalization of this ecosystem via YouTube commenter overlap across four countries' hyperpartisan channels, with the US anchoring a shared far-right identity. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates demographic assumptions by showing ConspiracyTok's creators are disproportionately young, non-white, and female, deploying "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinforming. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes back against platform-affordance determinism altogether, arguing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity consistent across six platforms, regardless of technical features—a claim in productive tension with [[Kakavand2026-kt]] and [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]].

Several papers interrogate the structural conditions of polarization more directly. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds minimal cross-ideological interaction across political subreddits, challenging "town square" assumptions about even ostensibly neutral spaces like r/politics, while [[Green2025-ap]] shows that domain-level measures of media partisanship obscure substantial story-level heterogeneity, producing "curation bubbles" through networked re-sharing. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] extend this into comparative alternative-media research, finding that audience overlap and entity networks between alternative and mainstream outlets depend on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access rather than fixed alternative/mainstream boundaries. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] pose related questions about whether alternative platforms and cross-platform circulation drive discursive polarization and the politicization of social justice discourse respectively.

### Toxicity, Misinformation, and Governance

A final cluster addresses the darker affordances of contemporary platforms as sites of harm and contested governance. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that misinformation's source (influencer vs. regular user) shapes toxic response more than content itself, converting parasocial credibility into collective antagonism. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents culturally specific normalization of incivility through Filipino TikTok "dogshows," while [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesizes how Facebook's Reactions feature has been mobilized to study negative-affect-driven political engagement. [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] both push against Twitter/Facebook-centric methodological defaults—the former highlighting TikTok's underexamined sonic propaganda techniques around the Ukraine invasion, the latter documenting genuinely divergent engagement patterns with partisan/low-quality news across seven platforms. [[Swartz2026-zb]] broadens the frame furthest, arguing that scams are not peripheral but constitutive of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself functioning as a governance and surveillance mechanism. Looking toward remediation, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] represent the field's forward-facing, reformist wing: proposing LLM-enabled collective dialogue and bridging systems to counteract engagement-optimized polarization, and mapping the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy as a precondition for meaningful platform governance.

### The Arc

Taken as a whole, this collection moves from confident theorization of networked publics (boyd, Marwick, Gillespie) through detailed empirical mapping of platform-specific cultures and harms, toward a moment of paradigmatic doubt. The anniversary essays ([[Marwick2026-ss]], [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Baym2026-tr]]) and structural theorizations ([[Tornberg2026-lc]], [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], [[Helmond2026-ll]]) converge on a shared diagnosis: the reciprocal, community-building "social" of early social media has given way to algorithmic, parasocial, and increasingly AI-mediated configurations. Yet the empirical papers complicate any single grand narrative—showing platform effects to be uneven, contested, sometimes overstated (as in Brown2026-af and [[Schulz2026-ts]]), and always entangled with user agency, national context, and cultural practice, as [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Dehghan2026-sy]] insist. The unresolved question this structure note surfaces is precisely the one [[Tornberg2026-lc]] poses explicitly: whether the field needs new concepts for a genuinely post-social landscape, or whether—as [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Kakavand2026-kt]] suggest—the older toolkit of affordances and networked publics can still be extended, platform by platform, to capture what comes next.
