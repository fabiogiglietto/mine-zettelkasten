# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## Twentieth-anniversary reckonings: is social media still social?

A striking cluster of essays written for *Social Media + Society*'s anniversary issue converges on a single diagnosis: the thing scholars have called "social media" for two decades is dissolving, and the field's vocabulary has not caught up. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that platforms once built for reciprocal peer exchange now reward scrolling over posting and parasocial attachment to influencers over mutual friendship, proposing "parasocial media" as the more honest label. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this shift a structural name, distinguishing TikTok-style "clustered publics"—statistically assembled audiences built from implicit behavioral signals—from the "networked publics" of first-generation platforms built on explicit following and friending. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes the claim furthest, arguing that algorithmic recommendation, generative AI, and users' retreat into semi-private spaces are splitting "social media" into three distinct post-social formations that each require different analytic tools. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own coinage of "platformization" to show that even that concept is being stretched toward a new, AI-centered phase—exemplified by Meta's retirement of the Like button and Big Tech's pivot toward "Big AI" infrastructure. [[Swartz2026-zb]] adds a darker inflection, proposing that scams, not sociality, are now the organizing logic of platform economies. Against this chorus of structural diagnosis, [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies an affective counterpoint: a reflective, autoethnographic account of LiveJournal-era intimacy that makes vivid exactly what has been lost—reciprocal, vulnerable, slow writing sustained by granular privacy tools absent from contemporary platforms. Read together, these six pieces frame the entire topic's central question: not whether platforms have changed, but whether "social media" remains a coherent object of study at all.

## Theorizing platforms, publics, and the networked self

Underneath these millennial reckonings sits an older theoretical lineage that the anniversary essays explicitly build on and revise. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] establish the foundational "networked publics" and "networked self" paradigms—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability, and collapsed contexts—that [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes empirically through Twitter users' management of an "imagined audience," and that [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] extends into "networked intimacy," showing how Italian Facebook users achieve closeness through public, collaborative practices (tagging, implicit sharing, liking) rather than private disclosure. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the companion critique of platform rhetoric itself, showing how the word "platform" does discursive work—claiming neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions—a move that [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scales up into a full political economy of the "platform society," where private platform logics collide with public values across sectors. [[De2026-ld]] brings this theoretical lineage into the present, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage their own transformations ("changecraft") to appear responsive while consolidating power—a direct descendant of Gillespie's insight updated for an era of constant platform flux.

## Algorithmic curation, visibility, and the polarization debate

A large empirical cluster investigates whether algorithmic curation produces ideological segregation, and the answers are persistently more modest than popular fears suggest. The foundational Facebook studies—[[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Messing2014-jc]]—find that individual choice, not algorithmic ranking, drives most selective exposure, and that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues entirely. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] complicates the picture further: social media and search are associated with *both* higher segregation and greater exposure to opposing views, while the bulk of consumption remains direct visits to mainstream outlets. This "user choice over algorithm" finding recurs in [[Brown2026-br]]'s YouTube audit, which finds strong "rabbit hole" effects but no independent algorithmic radicalization pathway, and in [[Efstratiou2025-gs]]'s analysis of pre-X Twitter, where apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification dissolves once posting behavior and proximity to Elon Musk are controlled for. [[Schulz2026-ts]] reframes the entire debate as one of perception: much observed "polarization" is an artifact of who self-selects into posting, not of divergent underlying attitudes. [[Green2025-ap]] pushes methodology in a different direction, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity—"curation bubbles" are built story-by-story through networked sharing, not source-by-source. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] takes this entanglement of platform, moderation, and discourse to Reddit, finding that political subreddits are "sedimented" into mutual opposition regardless of actual discursive similarity, while [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] extend the inquiry across the broader, fragmented seven-platform and alternative-platform landscape beyond Twitter and Facebook. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] and [[Wan2026-ai]] bring this debate to newer algorithmic terrain: sock-puppet audits of TikTok find that non-partisan German users are disproportionately exposed to AfD content (driven by supply-side virality rather than platform favoritism), while political efficacy is shown to condition how individuals select political content from Google's algorithmic feed. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] and [[Fan2026-af]] step back methodologically, synthesizing the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy and proposing new computational techniques (sequence analysis, HMMs, embeddings) for capturing the hyper-longitudinal, cross-platform texture of algorithmic media use that cross-sectional designs miss. [[Esau2025-tf]] closes this cluster normatively, proposing deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as theoretical antidotes to the "destructive polarization" these empirical studies document.

## Curating across platforms: news, politics, and hybrid media logics

A parallel body of work examines how political actors and news systems operate across, rather than within, single platforms. [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supplies the master framework—the "hybrid media system," in which older and newer media logics interact rather than one displacing the other—which [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] applies concretely to Italian talk-show/Twitter dual-screening, finding narrow, elite-insulated participation. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] turn to the affective register of this hybridity, showing that Facebook's emotional "Reactions" feature systematically rewards anger-inducing populist content with greater shareability—a finding Anwar's review situates as a broader pattern across Facebook research since the feature's 2016 launch. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extends the gatekeeping question to health news, showing that Google News Italia's aggregation choices interact with Facebook engagement to amplify emotionally charged "popular" health topics over technical ones. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] examine deliberate cross-platform strategy by partisan actors, showing right-leaning outlets selectively differentiate content between Twitter and Truth Social, and that "culture wars" framings of social justice discourse emerge both within and synergistically across Twitter and YouTube. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] bring a more theoretical lens to misinformation itself, the latter arguing (via second-order cybernetics) that "fake news" typologies should shift attention from creators' intent to the chain of propagator judgments that drive circulation—a reframing that resonates with [[Slack1783507463-sl24]]'s argument that superficially similar collective phenomena (online firestorms) can mask entirely different underlying sociological and political dynamics. Rounding out this cluster, [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] return to the audience itself, showing across four countries and multiple platforms that ordinary users operate with "expanded" definitions of news and "informativeness" that diverge sharply from professional journalistic norms—evidence that platform logics are reshaping not just distribution but the very category of news.

## Creator labor, platform vernaculars, and everyday culture

A smaller but tightly linked set of papers examines how ordinary and semi-professional users inhabit platform demands at the level of daily practice. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers deploying second-hand fashion content as a survival strategy against dual precarity (unstable labor markets, relentless algorithmic production demands), blending genuine care with reputational self-branding. [[Tang2026-gu]] documents an even more industrialized version of this logic in China, where "AI slop" creators treat virality itself as an object of systematic, benchmarked production via generative tools and matrix accounts. [[Cabbuag2024-me]], [[Graham2026-fb]], [[Bosch2024-hj]], and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] turn to platform-specific vernaculars of humor, incivility, and audiovisual technique: Filipino "dogshow" humor normalizes legal-but-harmful incivility on TikTok; Reddit's "botting" phenomenon reveals authenticity to be a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property; TikTok's distinctive sound design becomes a vector for Ukraine-war disinformation; and Canadian TikTok immigration discourse shows ideologically opposed users deploying the platform's affordances in strikingly symmetrical ways. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] complicates assumptions about toxic online behavior by linking validated dark-personality measures to actual Reddit text, finding that self-reported toxicity aligns with observable language far more robustly than stable personality traits do—a caution against over-individualizing platform incivility.

## The far right and conspiracy across a fragmented platform ecology

The largest thematic cluster in this collection tracks how extremist and conspiratorial movements exploit multi-platform ecologies. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] provides the methodological anchor, showing that German far-right networks take structurally different shapes on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram—evidence that affordances are relational, sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed platform properties. This multi-platform logic plays out concretely in case studies: [[Askanius2026-de]] traces how Swedish far-right YouTubers fused influencer self-branding with pandemic-era political propaganda; [[Groebner2026-pc]] documents a genuinely transnational Anglophone far-right audience across Fox News, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, and GB News on YouTube, with the US functioning as a central hub; [[Heft2021-ky]] finds that right-wing alternative news sites hyperlink mainly domestically but, when they do cross borders, surprisingly reference legacy mainstream media as shared anchor points; and [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows the anti-gender organization CitizenGO centrally coordinating a multilingual Facebook-centered network that mimics progressive advocacy platforms like Change.org for legitimacy. [[Balluff2026-ev]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] push back gently on assumptions of total separation, finding that alternative media's distinctiveness lies more in relational network structure (or institutional context) than in which entities or sources are referenced. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] examine the softer, affective end of this ecology—Instagram's humor-driven "affective-discursive ecology" that normalizes extremism, and coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus. The conspiracy-specific strand is especially rich: [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows ConspiracyTok dominated by young, non-white, and female creators practicing a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone; [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] finds that "Great Reset" believers across six platforms are held together less by shared ideology than by symbolic boundary work and reclaimed stigma ("we, the conspiracy theorists"); and [[Tuters2026-vo]] offers the most ambitious theoretical synthesis, arguing that conspiratorial alignment around the Great Reset emerges from platform-driven patterned repetition and modular reuse—a "high-tech paranoid style"—that precedes, rather than follows from, narrative coherence.

## Migration, decline, and the search for alternatives

Two papers directly interrogate whether users can or will leave dominant platforms. [[Wang2026-ub]] tracks academic Twitter's attempted migration to Mastodon after Musk's acquisition, finding that even a highly motivated, well-connected cohort largely failed to sustain the move—retention depended on discipline-specific servers and cross-server engagement diversity rather than internal network strength. [[Bruns2026-yv]] extends this into a broader obituary for Twitter as a space for prosocial public debate, arguing that its "enshittification" under Musk accelerated a decline rooted in years of prior mismanagement, and that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) have largely cloned Twitter's form without solving its underlying moderation and governance failures—a conclusion that echoes [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s elegy for LiveJournal and [[Boyd2026-op]]'s "parasocial media" diagnosis: nostalgia for an earlier, more reciprocal social web runs through this entire topic.

## AI and the next infrastructural turn

Finally, a forward-looking cluster asks what comes after platformized social media as generative AI becomes infrastructural. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google's AI Overviews being discursively contested by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers as search shifts from indexical gatekeeping to generative epistemic authority. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] takes a more prescriptive, civil-society view, surveying how large language models could enable more decentralized and deliberative "digital public squares" through collective dialogue, bridging systems, and AI-assisted moderation—while warning against synthetic participation. [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] reframes LLMs themselves as communication partners rather than intelligent agents, situating them within a longer history of information-management infrastructures. Together with [[Tornberg2026-lc]]'s and [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s AI-centered reformulations of platformization, these pieces suggest that the questions animating this entire topic—how affordances shape sociality, identity, and community—are now being reopened for a landscape in which the "user" may increasingly be a spectator of, or interlocutor with, synthetic content rather than a participant in a network of peers.
