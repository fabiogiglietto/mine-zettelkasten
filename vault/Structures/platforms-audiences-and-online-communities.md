# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The "social media" concept under strain

A striking number of papers filed here converge on a single provocation: that "social media," as a genre and as a scholarly category, is dissolving. Writing for *Social Media + Society*'s anniversary issue, [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that platforms once built for reciprocal exchange now reward scrolling over posting and one-sided attention to influencers over mutual friendship, proposing "parasocial media" as the more honest label. [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred diagnosis from the opposite direction, using her own LiveJournal years as a benchmark of intimacy, reciprocity, and vulnerability against which contemporary platforms look extractive and alienating — a loss she insists was politically produced, not technologically inevitable. [[Helmond2026-ll]] historicizes this shift institutionally: platformization, the infrastructural logic she named a decade ago, is entering an AI-centered phase in which behavioral inference and generative content replace the open-web social graph (symbolized by Facebook's retirement of the Like button). [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes furthest, arguing that recommendation-driven feeds, generative AI, and retreat into semi-private spaces are splitting "social media" into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, bounded micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — each demanding distinct methods. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the structural mechanism underlying all of this: TikTok's shift from *networked* to *clustered* publics, in which item-centric, watch-time-driven recommendation replaces follower graphs as the organizing principle of visibility, a "TikTokification" now spreading across the ecosystem. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the same arc as institutional history rather than theory, tracing Twitter's decline from prosocial, flat-network debate space to Musk-era "enshittification," and warning that Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky risk cloning Twitter's form without repairing its governance failures — a warning empirically borne out by [[Wang2026-ub]], whose survival analysis of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migrants shows that even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort mostly reverted to Twitter, with retention hinging on niche servers and cross-server engagement rather than replicated social capital.

## What "networked publics" used to mean

These reflective essays gain their force by arguing against a prior consensus, one articulated in the foundational texts also filed here: [[Slack1783508184-sld3]]'s theorization of SNSs as networked publics defined by persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability; the companion volume [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] on the "networked self"; and [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s account of context collapse and the imagined audience on early Twitter. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] extends this vocabulary into Facebook-era intimacy, showing how Italian users collaboratively negotiate disclosure through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking — an interactionist, community-preserving reading of "networked intimacy" that stands as a positive counterpart to the loss narrated by Marwick and boyd. Read together, this cluster of papers frames the entire topic's central tension: platforms once organized visibility around *people*; now they organize it around *content and interest*. The rest of the corpus can be read as empirical testimony to what happens to community, identity, and culture on either side of that line.

## Community and identity formation under platform-specific vernaculars

A second cluster asks how identity and belonging are actually produced within particular platform cultures, largely vindicating the "clustered/vernacular" side of the ledger while resisting purely techno-deterministic readings. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers turning second-hand fashion into a low-cost, algorithmically legible niche where care rhetoric and self-branding are inseparable survival strategies under precarity. [[Marwick2025-ov]] finds that U.S. "ConspiracyTok" is populated disproportionately by young, non-white, and female creators who build intimacy and evidentiary authority through platform-native aesthetics, exemplifying a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through platform affordances than through symbolic boundary work — reclaiming stigma, sharing "deep stories" of hardship — a finding that pointedly resists techno-centric echo-chamber explanations and sits close to [[Graham2026-fb]]'s argument that Reddit's "botting" phenomenon is best understood as participatory culture and negotiated authenticity rather than a human/bot binary. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and [[Nangle2026-yo]] both document how humor and positive affect function as normalization mechanisms for harmful content — Filipino "dogshows" and far-right Instagram Reels alike — while [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows how Andrew Tate's ambient ideology persists on YouTube through diffuse remix ecologies that outpace moderation. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates any assumption that platform affordances straightforwardly favor hostility, finding pro-immigration content dominant on Canadian TikTok even as ideologically opposed users deploy the same affordances symmetrically. [[Tang2026-gu]] extends the "creator" concept into generative-AI production in China, where virality itself becomes an engineered, benchmarked output rather than an emergent social outcome — an eerie echo of Tornberg's "platforms without users."

## Platform infrastructure, governance, and discursive self-management

Where the previous cluster examines audiences and creators, another set of papers examines platforms as institutional actors managing their own transformation. [[De2026-ld]] coins "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate policy and feature changes as continuity, creator benevolence, or democratization, masking power consolidation — a discursive companion to Helmond's structural account of platformization. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] operationalizes affordance theory empirically, showing that identical far-right actors produce structurally distinct networks (broadcast-like on Twitter, cohesive on Telegram, fragmented on Instagram, hierarchical on Facebook), demonstrating that affordances are relational outcomes of platform-actor interaction rather than fixed technical properties. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] gestures toward a related question — whether alternative platforms themselves drive polarization — that the corpus otherwise investigates through content and network overlap rather than affordance alone.

## Audiences, trust, and the shifting boundary of "information"

A substantial thread concerns how audiences evaluate truth, informativeness, and misinformation within these transformed environments — often finding that everyday practice diverges from scholarly and institutional assumptions. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] shows Australians flag misinformation mostly in mundane domains like business/economics, attributed to mainstream clickbait rather than fringe sources — undercutting topic-narrow misinformation research. [[Wedel2026-ix]] proposes "informativeness" as an audience-centered alternative to institutional "news," showing young Germans extend journalistic norms to influencers based on perceived expertise rather than credentialing. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] finds Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, signaling automated search's growing intermediary role. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] both interrogate the alternative/mainstream media boundary structurally — the former via cross-national audience-overlap networks (finding a Swedish "alternative enclave" but blurred boundaries where populists have institutional access), the latter via named-entity co-occurrence networks around Nord Stream 2 (finding alternative German outlets share entities with, but organize them differently from, legacy media, while referencing legacy media more heavily). [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] complicates source-credibility assumptions by showing influencer-sourced misinformation generates more, and more homogeneous, audience toxicity than identical content from ordinary users. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies TikTok's audio layer as an underexamined propaganda vector in Ukraine-war disinformation, while [[Ventura2025-sw]] moves the misinformation conversation off public feeds entirely, using a WhatsApp deactivation experiment in Brazil to show private messaging's distinct causal role. [[Mosleh2024-op]] generalizes the point: engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges markedly across seven platforms, warning against Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations that much of this very topic collection otherwise risks.

## Polarization, affect, and the mechanics of visibility

A final cluster interrogates the specific mechanisms — emotional, algorithmic, curatorial — by which platforms structure political audiences and publics. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the touchstone empirical intervention against strong filter-bubble claims, showing individual click choices suppress cross-cutting exposure on Facebook more than the News Feed algorithm does. [[Green2025-ap]] extends this with "curation bubbles," showing story-level partisan heterogeneity is systematically masked by domain-level measures, especially for nominally moderate sources. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers a unifying theoretical corrective, replacing "echo chamber"/"filter bubble" metaphors with a feedback-loop framework that explains why reinforcing dynamics rarely run to catastrophic extremes. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] applies a related "entanglement" logic to Reddit, finding political subreddits sedimented into non-overlapping camps regardless of discursive similarity, implicating platform moderation as much as ideology. Emotional affordances receive dedicated attention in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] (Scandinavian elections, where "Angry" reactions predict sharing and populists dominate anger-engagement) and the systematic review [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], which synthesizes how Facebook Reactions function as low-effort paralinguistic signals amplifying negative and populist content. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents cross-platform tailoring directly, showing the Sweden Democrats deploy argumentative posts on Facebook/X but memetic securitizing content on TikTok aimed at younger voters. Earlier moments in this lineage—[[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s study of the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s account of Twitter-as-second-screen during Italian televised politics—show that hybrid, audience-driven contestation of dominant narratives long predates the TikTok-era clustering Gerbaudo describes, situating the newer platform dynamics within a longer history of networked political audiencehood.

## Methodological undercurrents

Running beneath these substantive threads is a methodological argument that the tools of platform/audience research must keep pace with the shifts they document. [[Fan2026-af]] reframes digital trace data as temporal "user-sequences" across Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube, arguing aggregate cross-sectional measures miss path-dependent, platform-specific rhythms of use. [[Arminio2025-tw]] and [[Smith2025-kc]] push computational methods toward meaning and mechanism respectively — VLLM-based image clustering to recover connotative, culturally embedded meaning from Instagram climate imagery, and a causal "attention broker" model explaining how high-degree amplifiers accelerate triad formation on Twitter/X. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys the fragmented state of "algorithm literacy" research, diagnosing the same conceptual disunity that Helmond and Törnberg identify at the level of platform theory itself. Taken together, these methodological interventions suggest that the crisis of the "social media" concept documented by the anniversary essays is not merely rhetorical: it is forcing genuine reinvention of how audiences, communities, and platform cultures can be empirically apprehended.
