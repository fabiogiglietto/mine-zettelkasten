# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Churn Beneath the Surface: Platforms, Audiences, and the Unsettling of "Social" Media

A cluster of papers filed this year reads almost as a collective obituary for the networked-publics paradigm that has organized two decades of platform scholarship. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling now dominates posting and influencer content has displaced reciprocal peer exchange. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] goes further, arguing the social-media paradigm itself is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—citing Meta's own disclosure that only 7–17% of feed time involves followed accounts. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the structural mechanism: a shift from "networked publics" organized around explicit ties to "clustered publics" assembled statistically from implicit behavioral signals like watch time, with TikTok as the template other platforms are now imitating. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own coinage of "platformization" through the symbolically loaded retirement of Facebook's Like button, reading it as the social graph's obsolescence in favor of AI inference—extending [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s earlier argument that platforms are not neutral intermediaries but infrastructures embedding contested values into economy, polity, and culture. Taken together, these pieces (fittingly clustered in an anniversary issue) suggest the field's foundational vocabulary—boyd's own networked-publics framework in [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and the identity-and-community lens of [[Slack1783508042-sl69]]—is being actively renegotiated rather than simply applied.

This diagnosis is counterpointed by explicitly elegiac fieldwork. [[Marwick2026-ss]] mourns the intimacy and reciprocity of LiveJournal-era blogging against the alienation of contemporary platforms, insisting the current trajectory was contingent, not inevitable—a claim that resonates with [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s much earlier observation that the rhetoric of "platform" itself performs ideological work, obscuring the editorial and commercial interventions platforms always already make. Read alongside each other, these two papers bookend the arc: Gillespie shows how the discursive sleight-of-hand began, Marwick shows what it cost. [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" picks up this thread empirically, showing how Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok narrate their own transformations—through visible aesthetics, continuity-framing, and incremental "patchworking"—to naturalize power consolidation, a discursive analogue to the platformization Helmond tracks infrastructurally.

### Audience Labor, Intimacy, and the Vernaculars of Platform Culture

A second strand examines how ordinary and semi-professional users actually inhabit these architectures. [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s foundational account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience on Twitter finds its contemporary echo in [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s study of "networked intimacy" on Italian Facebook, where disclosure becomes a collaborative, public performance requiring the cooperation of one's network—both papers treat authenticity and intimacy as managed performances rather than natural states. [[Volpe2026-um]] extends this logic into the creator economy, showing how Italian micro-influencers' ethical/sustainability framing of second-hand fashion is simultaneously genuine care and reputational labor exacted by algorithmic demands for constant, niche-consistent output—a finding that dovetails with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of how deplatformed figures like Andrew Tate persist through diffuse networks of "ambient ideology" that outpace moderation, and with [[Askanius2026-de]]'s mapping of Swedish far-right YouTubers who fuse microcelebrity branding with political propaganda. [[Graham2026-fb]] complicates the human/platform boundary from another angle, showing that Reddit's "botting" phenomenon blurs the human–automated distinction entirely, treating authenticity as a culturally negotiated performance evaluated by community rather than a technical property—a conceptual cousin to [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of Filipino Gen Z "dogshows," where legal-but-harmful humor is normalized through platform-specific vernacular. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] both show how audiences actively construct meaning within TikTok's affordances: the former finding that ideologically opposed users deploy TikTok's features symmetrically when discussing immigration, the latter identifying how young audiences decode "informativeness" through platform-specific logics that extend journalistic norms well beyond professional journalism—a move paralleled by [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]]'s cross-national finding that ordinary users maintain both a "core" and an "expanded" definition of news that increasingly diverges from professional boundaries.

### Community, Polarization, and the Limits of Platform-Centric Explanation

A cluster of papers pushes back against purely platform-architectural explanations of polarization and conspiracy, insisting on discourse, identity, and meaning-making as co-constitutive forces. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s longitudinal study of political subreddits finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar communities, arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and user agency rather than reducible to any single cause. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] makes a kindred argument about "Great Reset" conspiracy discourse across six platforms, showing that symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor—sustain community cohesion largely independent of platform affordances, a claim about identity-over-infrastructure that resonates with [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s account of ConspiracyTok's "generous epistemology," where young, disproportionately non-White and female creators construct populist knowledge production through documentary aesthetics and personal testimony. [[Marwick2026-qd]] extends this narrative turn to disinformation generally, reconceiving it as identity-affirming, cathartic cross-platform storytelling rather than discrete false claims, while [[Prochaska2025-ef]] shows empirically how election "deep stories" evolve collaboratively between influencers and audiences across the 2020–2022 cycle. Against this, [[Esau2025-tf]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] offer more normatively oriented and architecturally focused takes on polarization, the former proposing "deliberative reciprocity" as an antidote, the latter asking whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive fragmentation—a question [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] answer empirically and skeptically, finding generally low audience and entity overlap between alternative and mainstream media but context-dependent exceptions (Sweden's alternative enclave; Nord Stream 2 coverage) shaped by media systems and populist parties' institutional access.

### Multi-Platform Ecosystems and the Transnational Far Right

Several papers insist that single-platform analysis is methodologically inadequate for understanding contemporary sociality and extremism. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that German far-right networks take structurally distinct shapes across Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, arguing affordances are relational sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed properties—a thesis empirically reinforced by [[Groebner2026-pc]]'s finding of a genuinely transnational far-right audience across Anglophone hyperpartisan YouTube channels, with the U.S. (especially Fox News) as a central hub. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] extend this cross-platform sensibility to social-justice discourse and right-leaning media curation (Twitter/Truth Social) respectively, while [[Nangle2026-yo]] examines how Instagram's affective-discursive ecology specifically enables far-right normalization through humor and memetic softening. [[Wang2026-ub]]'s study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration shows the stakes of fragmentation empirically: even highly motivated early adopters largely failed to sustain migration, with retention hinging on niche server communities and cross-server engagement rather than network size—a sobering data point for anyone hoping decentralized alternatives can simply replace legacy platforms, echoing Marwick's skepticism in [[Marwick2026-ss]] about Bluesky merely reproducing Twitter's logics.

### Methods, Infrastructure, and Reflexive Tools

Underwriting much of this empirical work is a growing methodological self-consciousness about how platform data itself is produced, sampled, and interpreted. [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats tools address the "denominator problem" of popularity-biased sampling in the post-API age; [[Fan2026-af]] proposes sequence-based temporal methods to capture cross-platform user trajectories rather than static snapshots; [[Bruns2025-fz]] offers "practice mapping" via vector embeddings to escape the "hairball" problem of conventional network visualization; and [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows vision-language models can recover connotative, not just denotative, meaning from platform imagery. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s review of algorithm literacy and [[Cerulli2026-sl75]]'s linkage of dark personality traits to Reddit text (finding weak correlation with observable toxicity) both signal a maturing, if still fragmented, toolkit for studying audience cognition and behavior empirically rather than inferentially. Historically, [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]]'s call for socially-driven internet architecture and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s and [[Chadwick2013-ns]]'s hybrid-media-system analyses of Italian and British/American political communication remind us that the tension between infrastructure and sociality long predates the platform era proper, while [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] show how older frameworks of hashtag dissent and online firestorms still require disaggregation into distinct sociological logics—a reminder, consonant with [[Swartz2026-zb]]'s argument that scams are now constitutive rather than peripheral to platform economies, and [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s speculative agenda for LLM-mediated public squares, that the future of platformed sociality remains an open, contested design question rather than a technological given.
