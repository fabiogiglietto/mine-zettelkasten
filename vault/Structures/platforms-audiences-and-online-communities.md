# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The arc: from networked publics to parasocial, algorithmic, and post-social media

This body of work traces a field in the midst of redefining its own object. For roughly two decades, "social media" meant something fairly stable: reciprocal, interest- or network-based sociality organized around friending, following, and peer-to-peer content circulation, theorized through boyd's foundational account of [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] networked publics and extended in the edited collection [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] on the networked self. A striking cluster of anniversary essays for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth year revisits that paradigm and declares it largely exhausted. danah boyd ([[Boyd2026-op]]) argues the "social" in social media has curdled into the "parasocial"—scrolling over posting, influencer content over friend updates. Nancy Baym ([[Baym2026-tr]]) finds her 2015 diagnosis of inequality, opacity, precarity, and extraction not just confirmed but intensified by the "broligarchy" and generative AI. Alice Marwick ([[Marwick2026-ss]]) looks back to LiveJournal's intimacy and reciprocity as a road not taken, framing today's alienated, surveilled platforms as a contingent political-economic outcome rather than a technological destiny. Anne Helmond ([[Helmond2026-ll]]) revisits "platformization" itself, arguing the concept is entering an AI-centered phase as the open-web infrastructural logic she first described gives way to in-app inference and "Big AI." Taken together with Gerbaudo's ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) theorization of TikTok's shift from "networked" to "clustered" publics—item-centric, opaque, algorithmically inferred—and Törnberg's ([[Tornberg2026-lc]]) call for a "post-social media studies" organized around algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, this group constitutes something like a field-wide stocktaking: the vocabulary of "networked publics" and "participatory culture" no longer maps cleanly onto platforms optimized for watch-time and recommendation rather than social graphs. Swartz's ([[Swartz2026-zb]]) essay on the "scam age" extends this reflective mode into political economy, reading platform governance and anti-fraud infrastructure as sites where legitimate and illegitimate economic activity are jointly remade.

## Algorithms as lived experience and contested terrain

Beneath these macro-diagnoses sits a longer tradition of studying how algorithms are *experienced* and *gamed* by ordinary users and professionalized creators alike. Bucher's foundational concept of the "algorithmic imaginary" ([[Bucher2017-qh]]) shows that Facebook users encounter algorithms affectively, through moods of surprise, surveillance, and misrecognition, well before (or instead of) any technical understanding—and that these imaginaries feed back into the system itself. Cotter ([[Cotter2019-js]]) and Petre ([[Petre2019-zh]]) extend this into the influencer economy, showing how Instagram creators "play the visibility game" within rules that platforms themselves define, police, and arbitrarily revise, with Petre's "platform paternalism" exposing the moralized, self-interested rhetoric platforms deploy to punish "gaming" while rewarding identical behavior performed by platforms themselves. De's analysis of "changecraft" ([[De2026-ld]]) picks up this thread at the level of platform discourse writ large, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate their own continuous policy and feature changes through tropes of creator benevolence and democratization that mask power consolidation. Gagrcin's review of algorithm literacy ([[Gagrcin2024-dl]]) surveys the broader attempt to systematize how users come to understand (or fail to understand) these opaque systems. Volpe's ethnography of Italian micro-influencers ([[Volpe2026-um]]) and Tang's study of Chinese "AI slop" creators ([[Tang2026-gu]]) both show creative labor increasingly organized around reverse-engineering virality and algorithmic demand—whether through curated second-hand fashion content performing care-as-branding, or through matrix-account systems that convert virality from accident into managed production. Di-Domenico's study of influencer-driven misinformation and toxicity ([[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]) adds a darker register: influencer credibility itself becomes infrastructural, legitimizing misinformation and channeling parasocial bonds into collective antagonism.

## Platform-specific affordances and the TikTok/Instagram problem space

A dense cluster of papers treats TikTok and Instagram as sites where distinctive affordances generate both novel forms of community and novel vectors of harm. Gerbaudo's clustered-publics thesis ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) provides the master framework here, and several empirical studies populate it. Karo ([[Karo2026-dn]]) documents how jihadist supporters exploit TikTok's recommendation system and vernacular culture—audio camouflage, meme infiltration, emoji codes—to sustain "everyday extremism" beneath moderation thresholds, a dynamic echoed in Rieder's account of the diffuse "Tate-space" on YouTube ([[Rieder2026-pp]]), where ambient ideology persists through recommendation-driven circulation that outpaces takedown. Nangle's analysis of far-right Instagram Reels ([[Nangle2026-yo]]) similarly shows extremism softened through humor and positive affect, exploiting algorithmic proximity to mainstream content. Marwick's study of U.S. "ConspiracyTok" ([[Marwick2025-ov]]) complicates the demographic stereotype of conspiracy believers, showing young, non-white, and female creators building "populist knowledge production" through a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinforming—a theme that resonates with Grusauskaite's cross-platform study of "Great Reset" communities ([[Grusauskaite2026-po]]), which locates conspiracy culture's durability in symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity rather than platform mechanics alone, and with Tuters's infrastructural account of the same phenomenon ([[Tuters2026-vo]]), which argues conspiratorial alignment emerges from platform grammars of repetition and adjacency before any coherent narrative crystallizes. Cabbuag's study of Filipino "dogshows" ([[Cabbuag2024-me]]) and Bosch's analysis of sound-based disinformation about Ukraine ([[Bosch2024-hj]]) both center TikTok-specific affordances—legal-but-harmful humor, sonic propaganda—as culturally particular amplifiers of incivility and misinformation. Hollingshead's content analysis of Canadian immigration discourse ([[Hollingshead2026-vx]]) offers a partial counterpoint, finding pro-immigration content dominant and ideologically opposed users deploying the platform's affordances in strikingly similar ways—evidence that TikTok's "double-edged" architecture can cut toward inclusion as readily as toward xenophobia. Tjaden's sock-puppet audit of the German TikTok feed ([[Tjaden2026-sl8e]]) grounds these qualitative observations quantitatively, finding non-partisan users disproportionately exposed to pro-AfD content—driven, the authors argue, by supply-side virality rather than overt algorithmic favoritism. Divon's comparative study of TikTok and Douyin influencers ([[Divon2026-wx]]) shows how even ostensibly apolitical lifestyle creators broker state-aligned narratives during crises, with nationalism operating as a situational visibility resource across both democratic and authoritarian platform regimes.

## Polarization, fragmentation, and the multi-platform turn

A second major vein interrogates whether social media causes polarization or merely stages its appearance, increasingly through comparative, multi-platform designs that resist the field's historical over-reliance on Twitter. Dehghan's sixteen-year study of political Reddit (Dehghan2026-fb) finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar subreddits, proposing a "sedimentation of polarisation" arising from the entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than any single cause. Schulz ([[Schulz2026-ts]]) reframes the phenomenon itself as partly illusory: much apparent polarization is "false," generated by self-selection in who chooses to post, a finding that resonates with Messing and Westwood's earlier, now-classic demonstration ([[Messing2014-jc]]) that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues in news selection, complicating fragmentation narratives from the platform's early days. Kakavand's comparative network analysis of the German far right across five platforms ([[Kakavand2026-kt]]) operationalizes affordance theory directly, showing the same actors producing structurally distinct networks—broadcast-like on Twitter, cohesive and party-hierarchical on Facebook, echo-chambered on Instagram—because affordances are relational outcomes of design and practice rather than fixed properties. Askanius's mapping of the Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube ([[Askanius2026-de]]) shows similarly how guest appearances, hyperlinks, and in-video mentions weave a hybridized ecosystem fusing influencer commercialism with propaganda. Balluff's entity-network study of Nord Stream 2 coverage ([[Balluff2026-ev]]) and Lukito's analysis of cross-platformization among right-leaning U.S. outlets on Twitter and Truth Social ([[Lukito2026-il]]) extend this comparative, infrastructural sensibility to media organizations themselves, while Mosleh's seven-platform study of partisan and low-quality news engagement ([[Mosleh2024-op]]) and Kristensen's work on alternative platforms and discursive polarization ([[Kristensen2025-ni]]) make the broader methodological case that single-platform findings cannot be generalized across an increasingly fragmented ecosystem. Giglietto's early case study of the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag ([[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]) and the Facebook firestorm study ([[Slack1783507463-sl24]]) remind us that dissent, protest, and collective action online have always resisted easy categorization as either marketing crises or coherent political movements.

## Migration, decline, and the search for alternatives

Running parallel to these diagnoses of platform pathology is a strand asking whether users can or will move elsewhere. Wang's year-long study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration ([[Wang2026-ub]]) finds that even a highly motivated, well-connected cohort largely failed to sustain the move, with retention depending on niche server communities and diverse cross-server engagement rather than strong internal networks. Bruns's elegiac account of "the death of Twitter" ([[Bruns2026-yv]]) extends the diagnosis, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely clone Twitter's form without resolving its governance failures, and that effective moderation must be a core design feature rather than an afterthought—a claim that dovetails with Marwick's and boyd's nostalgia for earlier, more intimate platform cultures and with Goldberg et al.'s forward-looking position paper on AI-enabled "digital public squares" ([[Goldberg2026-eb]]), which treats LLM-based bridging, collective dialogue, and community moderation systems as a tentative architecture for what more deliberative platforms might look like, while cautioning against synthetic participation substituting for human deliberation.

## Identity, intimacy, and audience imagination

A final strand returns to the durable question of self-presentation under conditions of audience uncertainty. Marwick and boyd's foundational account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience on Twitter ([[Marwick2011-sl02]]) remains a touchstone for understanding how users manage multiplicity through micro-celebrity and strategically performed authenticity—a problem Farci's study of "networked intimacy" among Italian Facebook users ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]) reframes as a collaborative, community-sustaining practice rather than narcissistic exposure, achieved through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking. Aharoni's cross-national study of lay definitions of "news" ([[Aharoni2026-sl6f]]) and Wedel's study of youth "informativeness" judgments ([[Wedel2026-ix]]) both extend this audience-centered turn into the domain of information and journalism, showing that ordinary users construct their own criteria—relevance, format, perceived expertise—that diverge substantially from professional or institutional definitions. Graham's study of Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon ([[Graham2026-fb]]) pushes the question of authenticity to its logical extreme, showing that even the human/bot distinction is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical fact, evaluated by communities on grounds of cultural relevance rather than ontological status. Gillespie's foundational analysis of platform rhetoric ([[Gillespie2010-sla2]]) underlies much of this later work: the very word "platform" was always already doing ideological labor, obscuring editorial intervention behind a language of neutrality and openness that the essays in this collection, two decades on, are still working to dismantle.

## Infrastructure and method: studying platforms as they change

Finally, a methodological seam runs beneath the substantive debates, reflecting the field's own anxiety about keeping pace with its object. Zheng's TubeStats/TokStats tools ([[Zheng2026-bi]]) address the "post-API age" by enabling genuinely random sampling of YouTube and TikTok, countering the popularity bias of opportunistic datasets. Fan's framework for mining "user-sequences" from digital trace data ([[Fan2026-af]]) and Bruns's "practice mapping" approach to multimodal network interactions ([[Bruns2025-fz]]) both seek methodological innovations adequate to platforms' temporal and multimodal complexity. Arminio's VLLM-based visual clustering pipeline ([[Arminio2025-tw]]) tackles the interpretability problem in computational image analysis, while Iannelli's Facebook ad-based recruitment study ([[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]) demonstrates both the promise and the limits of platform-mediated survey sampling. Manovich's media-theoretical account of generative AI ([[Manovich2026-ih]]) and Esposito's reframing of LLMs as "communication partners" ([[Slack1783073271-slbb]]) gesture toward where this whole topic is heading next: as platforms absorb generative AI and user-generated content gives way to AI-mediated and AI-produced circulation, the very infrastructures and methods this literature has built to study "platforms, audiences, and online communities" may themselves require the kind of paradigmatic reorientation that Törnberg, Helmond, boyd, and Baym are already, urgently, calling for.
