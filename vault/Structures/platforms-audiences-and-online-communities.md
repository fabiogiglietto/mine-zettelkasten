# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The arc of inquiry

This structure note gathers a body of work that, taken together, tells the story of "social media" as a historically bounded phenomenon rather than a stable object — a genre that emerged in the mid-2000s, matured into a set of shared theoretical vocabularies, and is now, according to several of its most prominent chroniclers, dissolving into something else. The papers filed here range from foundational theorizations of networked publics and identity performance to granular ethnographies of platform vernaculars, algorithmic audits, and reflective essays marking the twentieth anniversary of *Social Media + Society*. Read as a whole, they trace a movement from *networks* to *algorithms* to *ambient, parasocial, and post-social* configurations of online life.

## Foundations: publics, audiences, and the networked self

The theoretical bedrock is laid by [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], which establish social network sites as "networked publics" structured by affordances — persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability — that produce invisible audiences and collapsed contexts. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this insight empirically, showing how Twitter users manage "context collapse" by conjuring an imagined, networked audience and treating authenticity itself as a strategic performance. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] extends this lineage into the register of intimacy, showing how Italian Facebook users achieve "networked intimacy" through collaborative, public disclosure practices (tagging, implicit sharing, liking) that depend on the cooperation of one's network rather than private confession. Together these pieces establish the terms — audience, affordance, context collapse, performance — that later papers in this collection either extend or explicitly retire.

## The reckoning: is "social media" still a useful category?

A striking cluster of anniversary and reflective essays argues that the platforms once captured by these founding concepts have mutated beyond recognition. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes renaming the field's object "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting and one-sided attention to influencers has replaced reciprocal friendship. [[Marwick2026-ss]] mourns the intimacy and reciprocity of LiveJournal-era platforms against the alienation and surveillance of contemporary ones, insisting this trajectory was politically contingent rather than technologically inevitable. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] formalizes this sense of rupture into a "post-social media" thesis, identifying three emergent formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication — that no longer fit the user-generated-content-circulating-through-networks paradigm. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own coinage of "platformization" a decade on, using Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's space-based AI infrastructure to argue that platformization has entered an AI-centered phase requiring conceptual retooling. [[Swartz2026-zb]] pushes the reckoning into political economy, arguing that scams are not peripheral but constitutive of platform capitalism, blurring legitimate and illegitimate economic activity. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the clearest mechanism for this shift, theorizing TikTok's move from "networked publics" (organized by explicit interpersonal connection) to "clustered publics" (statistically inferred interest neighborhoods), a transformation that other platforms are now imitating. [[Bruns2026-yv]] closes this arc with a eulogy for Twitter specifically, tracing its fall from a uniquely prosocial, flat public sphere to a weaponized, mismanaged platform, and arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) risk repeating its governance failures rather than its virtues. [[Wang2026-ub]] provides an empirical postscript to this eulogy, showing that even a highly motivated cohort of academic early adopters largely failed to sustain migration to Mastodon, undermining hopes that decentralized alternatives could simply inherit Twitter's community.

## Platform vernaculars, identity, and community formation

A second cluster moves from the macro-diagnosis of platform change to close ethnographic and discourse-analytic study of how communities and identities actually cohere within specific platform cultures. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities across six platforms are held together less by algorithmic sorting than by symbolic boundary work — reclaiming the stigmatized "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal grievance into collective "deep stories" — arguing for an interactionist rather than techno-centric account of conspiracy culture. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complements this with a study of U.S. "ConspiracyTok," finding that its creators are disproportionately young, non-white, and female, and that they construct legitimacy through a "generous epistemology" that simultaneously empowers marginalized standpoints and launders disinformation. [[Graham2026-fb]] extends the theme of vernacular authenticity to Reddit's "botting" culture, arguing that the human/bot binary is analytically unstable and that authenticity is a community-negotiated performance rather than a technical property. [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] both examine the labor and infrastructural conditions of visibility: Volpe shows Italian micro-influencers deploying care/sustainability discourse as survival strategy under algorithmic production pressure, while Rieder traces how Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" persists on YouTube through a decentralized ecology of remix and reinterpretation that outpaces moderation. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] rounds out this section with a account of Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" on TikTok, showing how legal-but-harmful humor is culturally normalized as a shared practice between influencers and audiences.

## Algorithmic architectures: visibility, clustering, and polarization

A distinct empirical strand interrogates how recommendation systems reshape visibility and political discourse. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] audits Twitter's "For You" feed in the immediate post-acquisition period, finding that apparent right-leaning amplification is better explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk than by ideology per se — a finding that resonates with [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]'s broader claim about item-centric, behaviorally-inferred publics. [[Smith2025-kc]] proposes the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) as a causal mechanism by which high-degree amplifiers reshape network structure through attributed resharing. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates simple polarization narratives by showing minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar political subreddits, arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than reducible to any one factor. [[Green2025-ap]] challenges source-level (domain) measures of partisanship, showing that "curation bubbles" emerge from story-level unbundling and rebundling of content by networked curators — a methodological corrective echoed by [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s call for multi-level "political cartography" of news sharing, and by [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]], which extend partisan-audience analysis across seven platforms and six countries respectively, cautioning against single-platform generalization. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the touchstone against which this literature argues, having shown that individual clicking behavior, more than Facebook's algorithm, suppresses cross-cutting exposure. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers the clearest theoretical reconciliation of this evidence, proposing a "feedback loop" framework to replace the empirically shaky echo-chamber/filter-bubble metaphors, explaining why self-reinforcing dynamics rarely produce runaway radicalization. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] extend the normative stakes of this discussion, the former asking whether alternative platforms independently drive polarization, the latter proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to its most destructive forms.

## Extremism, moderation, and the politics of platform design

Several papers examine how extremist and populist actors exploit specific platform affordances, and how moderation regimes fail to contain them. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist supporters' evasion tactics on TikTok (audio camouflage, emoji codes, bait-and-switch), framing this as "everyday extremism" embedded in ordinary platform vernacular rather than spectacle. [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right Instagram content using positive affect and humor as "multimodal softening" that normalizes extremism through algorithmic proximity to mainstream content — a dynamic paralleled in [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s account of Tate's "ambient ideology." [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound itself as an under-examined vector of computational propaganda around the Ukraine invasion, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual discourse platform-by-platform, concentrating memetic content on TikTok to target younger voters despite a smaller following there. At a more macro level, [[Bennett2025-xs]] argues that platform-enabled "digital surrogate organizations" — not just individual-level disinformation effects — explain how scattered extremist factions become electorally consequential, integrating institutionalist and technocentric accounts of democratic backsliding. [[De2026-ld]] supplies the discursive infrastructure underlying all these dynamics, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X strategically narrate their own policy and feature changes ("changecraft") to mask power consolidation as responsiveness and innovation.

## Misinformation, toxicity, and trust in an increasingly synthetic environment

A further cluster examines audience-side reception of (mis)information and toxicity. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] uses diary methods to show that everyday misinformation perceptions extend well beyond politics into business/economics and are attributed mainly to mainstream, clickbait-driven news rather than fringe sources. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] finds that influencer-sourced misinformation produces more frequent and qualitatively distinct toxicity than identical content from ordinary users, implicating parasocial bonds and credibility as mechanisms of legitimation. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] complicates computational toxicity research by linking validated dark-personality measures to real Reddit text, finding that self-reported toxic engagement — but not personality traits per se — correlates robustly with observable linguistic markers. [[Gardam2025-er]] proposes a visual-first methodology for studying Instagram climate denial, arguing image-driven platforms demand analytic tools beyond text-centric discourse analysis. [[Dubey2026-bl]] and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] both interrogate emerging trust dynamics with AI-mediated information: balanced-news chatbots are, counterintuitively, trusted more by high-conspiracy-belief users, while audiences rate AI-generated and human-written news as equivalent in quality, with disclosure producing only a short-lived curiosity bump rather than durable acceptance. [[Wedel2026-ix]] reframes the underlying audience concept itself, proposing "informativeness" to capture how young adults evaluate YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok content against journalistic norms they apply well beyond professional journalism.

## Political affect, hybrid media, and platform-specific engagement

A smaller but coherent set of papers examines the affective and cross-media dynamics of political communication. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both interrogate Facebook's Reactions feature, finding that anger reliably predicts sharing and mobilization while love does not, with populist actors particularly adept at eliciting angry engagement. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] examines the counter-hashtag #JeNeSuisPasCharlie as a site of dissenting discursive strategy within a moment of mass online solidarity. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] documents an earlier hybrid-media moment — Twitter as second screen during Italian televised talk shows — showing that participation remained narrow and dominated by opinion/comment rather than substantive agenda-shaping, with TV logic still setting the terms of engagement. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] returns to TikTok specifically, finding that Canadian immigration discourse skews more positive than prior X/Twitter-centered research would predict, and that ideologically opposed users deploy the platform's affordances in strikingly symmetrical ways.

## Methods and infrastructure for studying platforms after the API

Finally, a set of methodological interventions responds to the changed conditions of platform research itself. [[Zheng2026-bi]] builds open tools (TubeStats, TokStats) for genuinely random sampling of YouTube and TikTok in the "post-API age," correcting for popularity-biased opportunistic samples. [[Fan2026-af]] proposes mining fine-grained "user-sequences" from cross-platform digital trace data to recover the temporal dynamics that cross-sectional methods erase. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] synthesizes 169 studies of algorithm literacy into an integrative framework, addressing the field's conceptual fragmentation. Read alongside the anniversary essays that open this note, these methodological contributions suggest that the crisis of "social media" as a category is also a crisis of method: as platforms fragment into clustered, parasocial, and AI-mediated formations, the tools built for the networked-publics era — APIs, network graphs, domain-level partisanship scores — increasingly strain to capture what audiences and communities are actually doing.
