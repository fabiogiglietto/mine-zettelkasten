# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Sociality Question: Has "Social Media" Stopped Being Social?

A striking convergence runs through this year's anniversary-themed reflections: several senior scholars independently conclude that the term "social media" no longer describes what it claims to. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes "parasocial media" to capture the shift from reciprocal peer interaction to one-sided consumption of professionalized creator content; [[Tornberg2026-lc]] goes further, arguing the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—each requiring distinct analytic tools. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the affective counterpoint, using her own LiveJournal history to show what has been lost: granular privacy filtering, reciprocal long-form writing, and durable friendships forged in a smaller, self-selected internet. These essays share a historicizing move—treating today's platform condition not as technological destiny but as the contingent outcome of political-economic choices (advertising, consolidation, algorithmic optimization). [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] gives this shift its sharpest structural formulation: a move from "networked publics" organized around explicit interpersonal ties (the boyd-derived paradigm formalized in [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]]) to "clustered publics" built from implicit behavioral signals like watch time, exemplified by TikTok's For You feed and its industry-wide imitators. [[Helmond2026-ll]] anchors this in infrastructural detail, reading Meta's 2026 retirement of the Like button as the literal death of the social-graph paradigm and the opening of an "AI infrastructure" phase of platformization. Together these pieces reframe the topic's baseline assumption—that platforms are essentially technologies of connection—as historically specific to a "first generation" now passing.

## Affordances, Genres, and the Texture of Platform Vernaculars

A second cluster treats platform affordances not as abstract structuring forces but as materials creators and audiences actively work with. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] remains foundational here: its account of "context collapse" and the "imagined audience" on early Twitter set the template for understanding how users perform authenticity under conditions of unknowable audience composition—a problem later papers extend into influencer economies. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers converting Instagram/TikTok's demand for constant, niche-legible output into performances of ethical care, blurring the line between branding and community service as a survival strategy under precarity. [[Tang2026-gu]] pushes this further into the algorithmic register: Chinese "AI slop" creators treat virality itself as an engineerable output, industrializing content production via benchmarking and matrix-account systems. [[Wedel2026-ix]] asks what audiences themselves do with this content, proposing "informativeness" as an audience-centered replacement for institutional news categories, showing that platform-specific selection logics (self-selected YouTube, actor-mediated Instagram, algorithmic TikTok) shape what young adults treat as useful knowledge. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] offers an earlier but resonant account of collaborative, platform-specific intimacy work on Facebook, in which "networked intimacy" is achieved through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking rather than private disclosure—an affordance-level precursor to the influencer-era self-branding studied by Volpe and Marwick.

Several papers extend affordance analysis to genre and comedic/tonal registers. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents how TikTok's "dogshow" phenomenon normalizes legal-but-harmful humor among Filipino Gen Z, while [[Graham2026-fb]] radically destabilizes the human/bot binary through Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" meme, arguing that authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property—a claim that resonates with Marwick's imagined-audience framework but pushes it toward non-human participants. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] shows how TikTok's shared vernacular (audio, humor, community Toks) is deployed nearly symmetrically by pro- and anti-immigration creators alike, complicating assumptions that affordances inherently favor one political valence.

## Platform Power, Discourse, and Self-Legitimation

A distinct strand examines how platforms narrate and justify their own power. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] is the field's touchstone here, showing how the very word "platform" performs discursive work—signaling neutrality to regulators while enabling editorial control. [[De2026-ld]] extends this into the present with "changecraft," documenting how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X stage infrastructural and ideological shifts as continuity, creator benevolence, or democratization. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] applies a similar lens to Google's AI Overviews, tracing contested framings of AI-mediated search across corporate, journalistic, and SEO discourse. [[Swartz2026-zb]] widens this to political economy, arguing that scam logics—not scams as deviance but as organizing infrastructure—now structure platform governance and "economic citizenship," while [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows the limits of platform power in practice: YouTube's moderation apparatus cannot outpace the recommendation-driven circulation sustaining Andrew Tate's "ambient ideology" even after formal deplatforming.

## Community, Identity, and the Far Right Across Platforms

A large subset examines how community and identity form around ideological content, especially far-right and conspiratorial discourse, revealing platform-specific but strikingly convergent dynamics. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] finds that across six platforms, "Great Reset" believers construct nearly identical symbolic boundary work—reclaiming stigma, building affective solidarity from personal hardship—suggesting identity performance, not platform architecture, drives conspiracy culture. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates the demographic stereotype of conspiracy theorists by showing ConspiracyTok's young, non-white, female-skewing creators build "populist knowledge production" via a "generous epistemology." [[Nangle2026-yo]] documents how far-right Instagram content uses positive affect and humor rather than hostility to normalize extremism, an "affective-discursive ecology" that parallels Cabbuag's account of humor-as-incivility vector. Network-level studies add structural texture: [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a Swedish far-right "alternative influence network" on YouTube fusing microcelebrity branding with propaganda; [[Kakavand2026-kt]] shows German far-right actors adapting distinctly to each platform's affordances rather than reproducing uniform networks across Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram; and [[Groebner2026-pc]] traces a genuinely transnational far-right audience across Anglophone hyperpartisan YouTube channels, with the US as central hub. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] extend the multi-platform lens to right-leaning media curation strategies and TikTok's sonic propaganda techniques respectively, while [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions of alternative-media insularity, finding substantial entity overlap with legacy outlets despite distinct network topologies—an echo of [[Heft2021-ky]]'s earlier finding that transnational right-wing hyperlink networks paradoxically rely most heavily on mainstream media as shared reference points.

## Polarization, Echo Chambers, and the Limits of Platform Effects

A long-running empirical debate about whether platforms cause polarization runs through this collection, and the papers filed here largely counsel skepticism toward strong platform-effects narratives. Foundational studies—[[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], [[Messing2014-jc]]—established that algorithmic curation and social endorsement modestly shape but do not dominate ideological exposure; individual choice, not algorithmic ranking, does most of the sorting work. Recent papers sharpen and extend this: [[Nyhan2023-gb]]'s large-scale Facebook field experiment finds that reducing like-minded exposure changes feed composition without moving attitudes at all, and [[Brown2026-br]] uses an audit design to show YouTube's algorithm produces content "rabbit holes" but not user-specific echo chambers or radicalization pathways—differences in Democrat/Republican recommendations stem from user choice, not algorithmic bias. [[Schulz2026-ts]] offers a unifying mechanism: much apparent polarization is "false polarization" produced by self-selection, since ideologically stronger users are simply more likely to post. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates measurement itself, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" sources can harbor sharply polarized individual stories. Against this broadly skeptical current, [[Dehghan2026-sy]] offers a structurally grounded counter-case: political subreddits show minimal cross-ideological overlap even where discursively similar, suggesting polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition of Reddit's platform architecture and moderation practices rather than reducible to selective exposure alone. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] gesture toward related dynamics—alternative platforms' role in discursive polarization and the "networked exposure" through which ordinary content becomes politically weaponized via circulation—while [[Oswald2025-km]] reminds us that all such discourse studies capture only the vocal minority, the "tip of the iceberg" of a mostly silent public.

## Coordinated Behavior, Toxicity, and Emotional Dynamics

A methodologically adjacent cluster examines how coordination, incivility, and emotion circulate at scale. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Luceri2025-tr]] both tackle coordinated inauthentic behavior—the former mapping pro-government Telegram amplification in Russia/Belarus, the latter adapting detection methods to TikTok's video-first structure, implicitly arguing (alongside [[Bruns2025-fz]]'s "practice mapping" methodology) that text-centric analytic tools imported wholesale from Twitter research misfit newer platforms. On toxicity specifically, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] finds that influencer-sourced misinformation generates more toxic backlash than identical content from regular users, mediated by parasocial legitimation, while [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] finds only a partial link between validated dark personality traits and observable Reddit toxicity—self-reported victimization predicts linguistic markers far more reliably than personality alone. Emotional affordances receive sustained attention in the Facebook Reactions literature: [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesizes findings that anger reliably drives engagement and sharing, especially for populist actors, a pattern empirically confirmed across Scandinavian elections in [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], where "Angry" reactions predict shares while "Love" reactions suppress them.

## Cross-Platform Comparison, Migration, and Method

Finally, a reflexive strand interrogates the field's own methodological habits, pushing back against Twitter/Facebook-centrism. [[Mosleh2024-op]] documents divergent partisan-news engagement across seven platforms, and [[Zhu2026-tn]] shows that alternative-mainstream audience overlap is deeply conditioned by national media systems and populist parties' institutional access—Sweden's isolated alternative enclave versus Austria's blurred boundaries. [[Wang2026-ub]] chronicles the largely failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon, finding that internal network cohesion could not substitute for discoverability and field-specific server communities—a cautionary case for [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s broader elegy for Twitter as a space of "prosocial" public debate, now diffused across incomplete successors (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) that replicate Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Chadwick2013-ns]] provide earlier hybrid-media grounding for this cross-platform sensibility, showing television and social media, or old and new media generally, as mutually constitutive rather than separable systems—a lesson [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] applies directly to how social justice discourse politicizes both within and across Twitter and YouTube. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys the resulting fragmentation in "algorithm literacy" scholarship, while [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] look forward, speculating on LLM-mediated "digital public squares" and reconceiving answer-engines as communication partners—suggesting that the next iteration of this topic's core questions (affordances, audience, community) will need to be asked all over again for a landscape increasingly organized around synthetic interlocutors rather than peer networks.
