# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## From networked publics to clustered publics

The oldest layer of this literature supplies the vocabulary everything else argues with or against. boyd's account of [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] established social network sites as "networked publics" defined by persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability, while the edited collection on the [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] framed identity and community as ongoing, platform-mediated performances rather than fixed traits. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] specified the interactional consequence of these affordances—context collapse—showing how Twitter users manage an unknowable, flattened audience through imagined-reader strategies and the strategic performance of "authenticity." [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] meanwhile exposed the discursive labor the word "platform" itself performs, letting intermediaries claim neutrality toward users, advertisers, and regulators simultaneously while making substantive editorial interventions.

[[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues that this entire apparatus describes a "first generation" of social media that TikTok has superseded: rather than networked publics organized around explicit interpersonal ties, TikTok produces "clustered publics" assembled algorithmically from implicit behavioral signals, in which follower counts barely predict reach and "silosociality" replaces self-selected community. This morphological claim reverberates through [[Tornberg2026-lc]], which extends it into a full periodization—social-graph circulation giving way to algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—and through [[Boyd2026-op]]'s proposal to rename the field's object "parasocial media," since scrolling and one-sided attention to professionalized creators have displaced peer reciprocity. [[Helmond2026-ll]] supplies an infrastructural correlate: Meta's retirement of the Like button and Big Tech's pivot toward "Big AI" and space-based compute mark platformization's shift from harvesting the open web's social graph to AI-driven inference, a transformation she urges scholars to theorize precisely rather than let the term dissolve into a synonym for "digitization." [[De2026-ld]] shows platforms actively narrating these very shifts—through "changecraft" tropes of creator benevolence, democratization, and connectivity—that stage disruptive change as continuity and mask power consolidation.

## Anniversary reckonings: has "social media" ended?

A cluster of reflective essays, several written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth-anniversary issue, converges on the question of whether the field's founding object still exists. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own 2015 diagnosis of inequality, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, and data extraction and finds all four worse, entangled now with "broligarchy" and generative AI's appropriation of user content; she calls for engaged, cross-sector collaboration rather than critique for its own sake. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] approach the same disillusionment autobiographically and structurally: Marwick's fieldnotes on LiveJournal recover a lost intimacy and reciprocity that today's extractive, surveillant platforms foreclose, insisting this trajectory was contingent rather than inevitable, while Swartz argues that scam logics—not sociality—now organize platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself becoming a mechanism of governance and surveillance. Together with [[Boyd2026-op]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Tornberg2026-lc]], these pieces stage a collective genre: the "post-social" retrospective, in which the analytic and normative vocabulary built for Web 2.0—participatory culture, networked publics, the struggle for a democratic social web—is declared inadequate to platforms optimized for AI inference, influencer spectacle, and semi-private retreat.

## Testing the algorithm: causal and quasi-causal audits

Where the essays theorize transformation, a second group supplies empirical purchase on what algorithmic curation actually does. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed shifts political attitudes rightward and, crucially, does so asymmetrically: the effect persists after the algorithm is switched off because users have already followed new accounts, resolving the puzzle of Meta's earlier null results. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complements this with an audit of Twitter's feed immediately after Musk's acquisition, showing that apparent right-leaning amplification is better explained by rewarded "agitation" and proximity to Musk's own interactions than by ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lost visibility outright. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s registered report on Bluesky pushes the same question toward intervention, demonstrating that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, and toxic content and distorts perceived social norms of political dialogue, but that a "diversified extremity" algorithm can suppress this without harming platform enjoyment—reframing amplification effects as a problem of algorithm-mediated social learning rather than persuasion. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s systematic review of "algorithm literacy" research shows how fragmented the field's conceptual response to this same phenomenon has been, calling for the kind of integrative framework these audits implicitly demand.

## Platform vernaculars: creators, genres, and the cultures algorithms reward

A substantial cluster documents how specific platform cultures metabolize algorithmic and economic pressure into vernacular genres. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers turning second-hand fashion into a low-cost, algorithm-legible niche that fuses care rhetoric with reputational self-branding under conditions of dual precarity. [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s study of "ConspiracyTok" finds a demographically unexpected cast of young, non-white, and female creators building "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence and intimacy cues—a "generous epistemology" that can voice marginalized standpoints even as it circulates disinformation. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents a parallel vernacular in the Philippines, where Gen Z "dogshow" humor normalizes legal-but-harmful incivility, and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds that pro- and anti-immigration TikTok creators in Canada deploy the platform's affordances—duets, sounds, community hashtags—almost symmetrically, complicating assumptions drawn from X-centric research. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] extend this vernacular lens to extremism: far-right Instagram content mainstreams itself through humor and "affective-discursive" softening, while Andrew Tate's persistent "ambient ideology" on YouTube survives deplatforming through a diffuse ecology of interviews, remixes, and memeified clips that outpaces moderation. [[Graham2026-fb]]'s account of Reddit "botting" reframes even ostensibly inauthentic mass-posting as participatory culture, arguing that authenticity is a community-negotiated performance rather than a technical fact—a claim [[Luceri2025-tr]] approaches from the opposite direction, adapting coordinated-inauthentic-behavior detection to TikTok's video-first structures where text-centric methods do not transfer cleanly.

## Intimacy, community, and identity across platform eras

Set against the vernacular-genre studies is a smaller thread asking what "community" itself has meant across platform generations. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s study of Italian Facebook users identifies a repertoire of "networked intimacy" practices—showing rather than telling, tagging, liking, implicit sharing—through which users collaboratively negotiate public/private boundaries with their networks' cooperation, challenging narcissism-centered readings of social media disclosure. Read alongside [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s LiveJournal fieldnotes, this work suggests continuity as much as rupture: both find genuine relational labor sustained through platform-specific affordances, even as the aggregate argument of the anniversary essays ([[Boyd2026-op]], [[Tornberg2026-lc]]) insists such reciprocity has become residual rather than central to contemporary platforms.

## Polarization, boundary work, and the alternative-media ecosystem

A distinct set of papers interrogates fragmentation and polarization as socio-technical, not purely discursive, phenomena. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s sixteen-year study of political subreddits finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even between discursively similar communities, arguing that polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, platform design, and discourse rather than reducible to any one factor. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] takes the opposite methodological tack, tracing "Great Reset" conspiracy discourse across six platforms to show that identity work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label, converting personal hardship into shared grievance—matters more than platform affordances or content per se, uniting ideologically disparate publics into a "diagonal" coalition. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] asks directly whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization, while [[Zhu2026-tn]] operationalizes "alternative" media relationally, finding that audience overlap with mainstream outlets depends less on content than on populist parties' institutional access—alternative media form an isolated enclave in Sweden but blur into the mainstream where populists are institutionally embedded. [[Mosleh2024-op]] broadens the empirical base further, showing that engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges substantially across seven platforms, undercutting single-platform (typically Twitter-centric) generalizations that much of this literature still relies on.

## Migration, decline, and the search for alternatives

Several papers track what happens when users try to leave dominant platforms. [[Wang2026-ub]]'s longitudinal study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration after Musk's acquisition finds that even highly motivated, well-networked early adopters largely failed to sustain the move, with retention concentrated in field-specific servers and diverse cross-server engagement rather than raw network size. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the broader arc from Twitter's flat, open architecture—valuable for crisis reporting and hashtag activism—through commercial mismanagement and "enshittification" under Musk, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) merely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. Both studies temper the more hopeful diagnoses elsewhere in this collection: decentralization and exit are, in practice, harder to achieve than platform critique implies.

## Infrastructures of information, trust, and deliberation

A final cluster turns from sociality to epistemics. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google's AI Overviews being discursively contested by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers alike, with user and societal concerns notably absent, while [[Wedel2026-ix]] proposes "informativeness" as an audience-centered alternative to institutional news categories, showing how young adults extend journalistic norms to influencers based on perceived expertise rather than credentials. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] looks forward normatively, surveying how large language models might build more deliberative "digital public squares" through collective dialogue systems, bridging algorithms, and AI-assisted moderation—an optimistic counterpoint to [[Swartz2026-zb]]'s portrait of a scam-saturated platform economy and to [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s and [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s accounts of moderation's structural limits. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]], considerably earlier, anticipates aspects of this infrastructural turn by arguing that Internet architecture itself should be redesigned around social-capital principles (trust, reciprocity, influence) for a "prosumer" internet—a design-level proposal that the platformization and changecraft literature ([[Helmond2026-ll]], [[De2026-ld]]) shows was never realized in practice.

## Methods for a post-social-graph landscape

Running underneath these substantive debates is a methodological reckoning with how to study platforms whose interactions are now algorithmic, multimodal, and cross-platform. [[Arminio2025-tw]] shows that vision-language models can recover "connotative" meaning from Instagram images that CNN-based clustering misses, restoring interpretability to visual computational social science. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping" via vector embeddings as an alternative to hairball-prone network visualization for multimodal interactions, while [[Fan2026-af]] reframes digital trace data as temporally ordered "user-sequences," applying six computational approaches to cross-platform smartphone data to recover routines invisible to cross-sectional measures. [[Smith2025-kc]] isolates a causal micro-mechanism—the "attention broker"—showing experimentally how amplification by high-degree accounts accelerates triad formation in follower networks, offering a structural complement to the audits of feed algorithms discussed above. Taken together with the coordinated-behavior and clustering challenges raised by [[Luceri2025-tr]] and [[Graham2026-fb]], this methodological cluster makes concrete the diagnosis shared across the whole topic: as platforms move from networked to clustered, parasocial, and AI-mediated publics, the tools built for the social-graph era must be reinvented alongside the theories.
