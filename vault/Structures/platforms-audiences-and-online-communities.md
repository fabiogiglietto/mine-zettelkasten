# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The arc from networked publics to parasocial, clustered, and post-social media

A clear historical narrative runs through this literature, and several papers explicitly write its periodization. The foundational framework is boyd's "networked publics" [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and the broader "networked self" paradigm [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], which theorized social network sites as spaces structured by affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—that produced invisible audiences and collapsed contexts. Marwick and boyd's classic study of Twitter [[Marwick2011-sl02]] gave this empirical texture, showing how users manage "context collapse" by imagining audiences and performing strategic authenticity. This is the baseline against which nearly every other paper in this set measures change.

That baseline is now under sustained revision. Gerbaudo [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media, replacing interpersonal "networked publics" with algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" organized around interest rather than connection. Törnberg [[Tornberg2026-lc]] radicalizes this further, arguing that the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—citing Meta's own data that friend-content now constitutes a vanishingly small share of feed time. boyd's own anniversary essay [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes renaming the object of study "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has displaced posting and polished creator content has displaced peer exchange. Helmond [[Helmond2026-ll]] traces a parallel infrastructural story: platformization itself is shifting from capturing open-web behavioral signals toward AI-driven inference, as symbolized by Facebook's retirement of the Like button. Chancellor and colleagues [[Chancellor2026-sld9]] formalize this transition conceptually, arguing that "context collapse" itself needs retheorizing via a structural grammar (user, platform, data/interaction) to account for algorithmic, consumption-oriented platforms, proposing new collapses—identity, algorithmic, situational—suited to this environment.

Two elegiac essays supply affective counterpoint to this periodization. Marwick's autoethnography of LiveJournal [[Marwick2026-ss]] and Bruns's history of Twitter's "death" [[Bruns2026-yv]] both narrate loss: granular privacy, reciprocal writing, and prosocial crisis communication gave way to surveillance, enshittification, and algorithmic amplification of bad actors. Both insist the trajectory was contingent—a product of political-economic choices (advertising, consolidation, mismanagement)—not technological inevitability, and both are skeptical that successor platforms (Mastodon, Bluesky, Threads) can recover what was lost. Wang's empirical study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration [[Wang2026-ub]] substantiates this pessimism directly: even a coordinated, motivated cohort largely failed to sustain migration, with retention hinging on niche server communities and cross-server engagement rather than network strength alone.

## Algorithms as lived, felt, and gamed infrastructure

A second cluster treats algorithms not as abstract technical systems but as something users feel, theorize, and strategically negotiate. Bucher's "algorithmic imaginary" [[Bucher2017-qh]] established that users experience Facebook's algorithm affectively—through "whoa moments," "faulty predictions," and "cruel connections"—and that these folk theories recursively shape algorithmic behavior itself. This affective-governance lens extends into the influencer economy: Cotter's "visibility game" [[Cotter2019-js]] and Petre et al.'s "platform paternalism" [[Petre2019-zh]] both examine how platforms draw moral boundaries between "legitimate" and "gaming" behavior that actually reflect platform self-interest rather than coherent norms, disadvantaging under-resourced creators. Gagrcin's review of "algorithm literacy" [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys this terrain more systematically, finding the construct itself conceptually fragmented across 169 studies. De et al.'s concept of "changecraft" [[De2026-ld]] extends the analysis from algorithms to platform discourse writ large, showing how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X narrate infrastructural and ideological change as continuity and creator benevolence, masking power consolidation.

Gillespie's foundational discursive analysis [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] anticipates all of this: the very word "platform" does rhetorical work, allowing firms to claim neutrality toward users, advertisers, and regulators simultaneously while making substantive editorial interventions. Van Dijck's "platform society" [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and Castells's "network society" [[Castells2009-sle8]] supply the macro-structural registers—platforms as contested terrain between corporate and public value, and networks as the new organizing logic of social structure—against which these micro-level studies of algorithmic negotiation should be read.

## Creator labor, platform demands, and the political economy of visibility

Several papers extend this visibility-politics lens into creator labor specifically. Volpe's study of Italian micro-influencers [[Volpe2026-um]] shows second-hand fashion content functioning simultaneously as ethical performance and survival strategy under algorithmic production pressure—care and self-branding are intertwined, not opposed. Tang's study of Chinese "AI slop" creators [[Tang2026-gu]] pushes this further: virality itself becomes an object of systematic engineering via benchmarking and matrix-account production, unsettling what "creator" even means. Li's folk-conceptualization study of AI slop from the audience side [[Li2026-zv]] complements this, showing users contesting AI content through constitutional-defect critiques, algorithmic-overreach frustration, and ambivalent enjoyment—slop as a relational, platform-enabled category rather than a fixed aesthetic. Di Domenico et al. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] examine the darker edge of influencer visibility, showing how influencer-sourced misinformation generates more toxic audience response than identical content from regular users, because credibility and parasocial bonds become infrastructural amplifiers of collective antagonism. Divon's comparative study of TikTok/Douyin influencers [[Divon2026-wx]] shows apolitical creators becoming situational political brokers during crises in both democratic and authoritarian contexts, treating the state itself as a visibility resource.

## Platform-specific cultures of humor, incivility, and identity

A recurring theme is how platform-specific vernaculars shape what counts as acceptable sociality. Cabbuag's study of Filipino "dogshows" [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and Graham's study of Reddit "botting" [[Graham2026-fb]] both show communities negotiating the boundary of legitimate/illegitimate participation through humor and play—dogshows normalize legal-but-harmful incivility, while fungi-emoji spam becomes a participatory ritual whose "bot" status matters less than its cultural freshness. Cerulli et al.'s linkage of dark personality traits to Reddit behavior [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] offers a cautionary empirical counterpoint, finding that self-reported dark traits do not reliably predict observable toxic language, complicating assumptions that online incivility straightforwardly expresses psychological disposition. Farci's study of Italian Facebook users [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] documents an earlier, more intimate register—"networked intimacy" achieved through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking—that resonates with Marwick's LiveJournal nostalgia as a lost mode of collaborative, community-oriented disclosure.

## Audience fragmentation, polarization, and the limits of cross-ideological contact

A substantial cluster interrogates whether platforms produce echo chambers, polarization, or something more entangled. Dehghan's sixteen-year study of political Reddit [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar subreddits, challenging "town square" assumptions and proposing a "sedimentation of polarisation" model in which platform design, moderation, and discourse are inseparably entangled. Green's "curation bubbles" [[Green2025-ap]] offers a methodological corrective: domain-level measures of partisan audience mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning moderate-seeming sources often carry sharply divergent partisan audiences for individual stories. Grusauskaite et al.'s cross-platform study of "Great Reset" discourse [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes against techno-centric echo-chamber explanations altogether, arguing that conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity—reclaiming stigma, sharing "deep stories"—consistently across six platforms regardless of affordance differences. Marwick's study of ConspiracyTok [[Marwick2025-ov]] complements this by showing conspiracy content authored by young, non-white, and female creators who build intimacy and "generous epistemology" through platform-native evidentiary forms, destabilizing the stereotype of the isolated white male conspiracist. Kristensen's brief note on alternative platforms [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and Zhu's cross-country audience-overlap study [[Zhu2026-tn]] extend this inquiry into comparative and infrastructural territory, the latter showing that alternative-mainstream audience overlap depends heavily on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access rather than being a fixed property of "alternative" media.

## Multi-platform and transnational far-right ecosystems

A distinct but related cluster examines how extremist and hyperpartisan actors exploit platform-specific affordances across an increasingly fragmented ecosystem. Kakavand's five-platform study of the German far right [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that identical actors produce structurally distinct networks on each platform—Twitter as broadcast arena, Instagram as fragmented echo chamber, Facebook as hierarchical party apparatus—reframing affordances as relational and sociotechnical rather than fixed. Groebner's study of transnational hyperpartisan YouTube channels [[Groebner2026-pc]] and Heft et al.'s hyperlink analysis of right-wing news sites [[Heft2021-ky]] both find genuine transnational integration, with the U.S. (and Fox News specifically) acting as a central hub—though Heft notably finds legacy mainstream media, not insulated alt-right sites, as the primary shared transnational reference points. Lukito's study of cross-platformization between Twitter and Truth Social [[Lukito2026-il]] and Balluff's entity-network analysis of German alternative media [[Balluff2026-ev]] both complicate simple insulation narratives: outlets curate selectively rather than duplicating content, and alternative media reference largely the same actors as legacy media while organizing them into more cohesive network structures. Nangle et al.'s Instagram study [[Nangle2026-yo]] and Hollingshead et al.'s TikTok immigration study [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] both show platform affordances (humor, memes, non-original audio, community Toks) being deployed symmetrically across ideological divides, suggesting a shared vernacular grammar that can normalize extremism as easily as it enables solidarity.

## Methodological and infrastructural self-reflection

Finally, several papers turn inward toward method, asking how researchers can know platforms at all under conditions of API closure and distributional bias. Zheng's TubeStats/TokStats project [[Zheng2026-bi]] builds random-sampling infrastructure to counter popularity-biased samples in the "post-API age." Arminio et al. [[Arminio2025-tw]] propose VLLM-based image clustering to capture connotative rather than merely denotative meaning in visual platform data. Mosleh et al. [[Mosleh2024-op]] argue for genuinely cross-platform comparison given how divergent engagement with partisan/low-quality news is across seven platforms, correcting the field's historical overreliance on Twitter and Facebook. Sofia's early position paper [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] anticipates much of this literature's concerns from an infrastructural-design angle, calling for internet architecture itself to incorporate social-capital metrics as users shifted from consumers to "prosumers." Goldberg et al.'s civil-society convening on LLMs [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and Aharoni et al.'s cross-national study of lay news definitions [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] and Wedel's study of youth "informativeness" judgments [[Wedel2026-ix]] all push toward audience- and user-centered redefinition of core concepts (public square, news, information) that institutional/elite definitions no longer adequately capture—a fitting bookend to a literature increasingly convinced that the platforms, and the audiences who inhabit them, have outgrown the vocabulary coined to describe the first generation of social media. Chadwick's "hybrid media system" [[Chadwick2013-ns]] [[Chadwick2015-ii]] and the Charlie Hebdo counter-hashtag case [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]], alongside the Vietnam–China Facebook firestorm study [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] and Swartz's account of platforms as infrastructures of scam [[Swartz2026-zb]], round out this set by insisting that any single explanatory frame—technological, economic, or cultural—is insufficient: online sociality is produced at the intersection of shifting affordances, discursive self-justification, and the always-contested practices of the audiences who inhabit it.
