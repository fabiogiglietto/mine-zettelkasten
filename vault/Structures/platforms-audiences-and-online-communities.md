# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## Foundational grammar: publics, audiences, platforms

Any account of this literature has to begin with the vocabulary it inherited and is now revising. boyd's original theorization of social network sites as [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and the companion volume [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] established the terms still doing work three decades later: persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability, and a "networked self" performed for an audience one can never fully see. Marwick and boyd's [[Marwick2011-sl02]] gave that invisibility a name—context collapse—and showed how users manage it by imagining an audience and performing authenticity as a strategic accomplishment rather than a natural state. Gillespie's [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the third leg: platforms are not neutral conduits but discursive constructions that intermediaries deploy to manage the competing claims of users, advertisers, and regulators while making substantive editorial interventions. Together these three set up the questions this whole topic keeps returning to: who is the audience, what does the platform afford, and whose interests does the "platform" label conceal?

## Is "social media" over? Anniversary reckonings

A cluster of essays marking the twentieth anniversary of platformed sociality converges on a single diagnosis: the object once called "social media" has mutated into something else, and the field's vocabulary has not caught up. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," arguing that scrolling has replaced posting and one-sided attention to influencers has replaced reciprocal friendship. [[Marwick2026-ss]] arrives at a kindred conclusion from the opposite direction, using her own LiveJournal years to show what has been lost—granular privacy, reciprocal writing, vulnerability—and insisting this was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes the argument into a full paradigm shift, identifying three "post-social" formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, AI-mediated communication) replacing the user-generated-content-through-social-graph model, a claim echoed in [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s account of platformization itself entering an "AI-centered" phase as Meta abandons the Like button and social-graph signals in favor of behavioral inference. [[Swartz2026-zb]] extends the declension narrative into political economy, arguing scams have become a central organizing logic of platform life as institutions wither. [[Bruns2026-yv]] closes the loop with the concrete case of Twitter/X, tracing how a flat, open network built for prosocial crisis communication and hashtag activism was progressively "enshittified" into a vector for coordinated disinformation, with successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely replicating its form without solving its governance failures.

## From networked to clustered publics: the algorithmic turn

If the anniversary essays diagnose decline impressionistically, [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the sharpest theoretical mechanism: a shift from "networked publics" organized around explicit interpersonal ties to "clustered publics" statistically assembled from implicit behavioral signals like watch time, with TikTok as the paradigm case and other platforms undergoing "TikTokification." This dovetails directly with [[Tornberg2026-lc]]'s post-social diagnosis and with [[Boyd2026-op]]'s parasociality thesis. Several empirical papers test what algorithmic curation actually does once this shift is underway. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] shows that Twitter's algorithmic feed rewarded agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk more than partisan identity per se, centralizing attention around the platform owner. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but only mild, largely user-driven echo chambers and a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates source-level accounts of polarization by showing that individual stories are unbundled and re-bundled through networked curation into "partisan curation bubbles" invisible to domain-level measures. [[Smith2025-kc]]'s concept of the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) offers a causal micro-mechanism—amplification by high-degree nodes accelerates the very triad closure that produces network-level polarization—linking structural network theory back to platform affordances for attributed resharing.

## Platform vernaculars: creators, ideology, and the mainstreaming of extremity

A large empirical seam documents how specific platform affordances shape creator culture and the circulation of ideology. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Instagram/TikTok micro-influencers performing sustainability and community care as much for algorithmic survival as ethical conviction, entangling care and self-branding under conditions of labor precarity. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] both trace how deplatformed or extremist figures persist through decentralized "ambient" ecosystems—Tate's diffuse "Tate-space" on YouTube, the Swedish far-right's "alternative influence network"—in which recommendation-driven circulation outpaces moderation and commercial microcelebrity techniques fuse with propaganda. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Nangle2026-yo]] examine the affective register of this normalization: ConspiracyTok's "generous epistemology" and populist knowledge production, and Instagram far-right Reels' use of humor and positive affect to soften extremist worldviews, both showing ideology traveling through platform-native aesthetic form rather than explicit argument. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] finds a parallel dynamic in Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" culture, where TikTok affordances normalize legal-but-harmful incivility as entertainment. Cross-platform comparative work extends this: [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric differently to Facebook/X versus TikTok, prioritizing memetic content for younger audiences; [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds, against the X-centric literature, that popular Canadian TikTok immigration content actually skews pro-immigration, with opposing camps deploying the platform's affordances in strikingly symmetrical ways; and [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-examined, platform-specific vector for wartime disinformation on TikTok. [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" reframes all this from the platform side, showing how Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok discursively manage their own continual feature and policy changes to appear responsive while consolidating power—an infrastructural counterpart to Gillespie's original argument about the word "platform" itself.

## Community, intimacy, and boundary work

Against the ideological and commercial dynamics above, several papers foreground the relational and identity-work side of platform sociality. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] recovers a more intimate register, showing how Italian Facebook users collaboratively negotiate "networked intimacy" through tagging, implicit sharing, and liking—practices that reinforce rather than corrode community, in productive tension with [[Marwick2026-ss]]'s elegy for LiveJournal-era intimacy. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through platform affordances or shared ideology than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor and converting personal grievance into collective identity—consistent across six platforms, a finding that pushes back against techno-centric echo-chamber explanations. [[Graham2026-fb]] extends this interactionist sensibility to nonhuman-adjacent participation, arguing that Reddit's "botting" culture is best understood as a spectrum of culturally negotiated authenticity performance rather than a human/bot binary. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complements these community-level accounts at the level of platform structure, showing that even discursively resonant political subreddits exhibit minimal cross-ideological interaction, arguing that Reddit's design, moderation, and discourse are entangled in a "sedimentation" of polarization that precedes any actual content difference.

## Migration, fragmentation, and the search for alternatives

A related set of papers tracks what happens when audiences try to leave dominant platforms or route around mainstream media entirely. [[Wang2026-ub]] offers the most granular case: even a highly motivated cohort of academic early adopters largely failed to sustain migration from Twitter to Mastodon, with retention hinging on field-specific servers and cross-server engagement rather than internal network density—a sober complement to [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s argument that successor platforms clone Twitter's form without its governance. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] asks whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization, while [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] approach the alternative/mainstream relationship structurally: audience-overlap networks show generally low but context-dependent overlap shaped by populist parties' institutional access, and named-entity network analysis shows German alternative media referencing largely the same actors as legacy outlets while organizing them into more cohesive, self-referential clusters.

## Audiences, trust, and the informational environment

A final cluster asks what audiences actually want from platformed information and how they evaluate it. [[Wedel2026-ix]] proposes "informativeness" as an audience-centered alternative to institutional news categories, showing young adults extend journalistic norms to influencers and platform-specific logics (algorithmic TikTok curation versus self-selected YouTube). [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] examine the affective register of engagement—emotionality in fact-checking, and Facebook Reactions as a paralinguistic register especially potent for populist anger—while [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] show how dissenting hashtag publics (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) and cross-platform news-sharing patterns resist single-platform generalization. The rise of generative AI adds a new axis of trust: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds audiences cannot distinguish AI-assisted from human-written news quality-wise but read differently once disclosure occurs, [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows trust penalties for AI disclosure vary sharply by journalistic task, and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys how LLM-based bridging, dialogue, and moderation systems might reshape digital public squares toward more deliberative ends—an optimistic counterpoint to the decline narratives above. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy that underlies all these audience-perception questions, and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] shows the gatekeeping logic operating one level up, as Google News Italia's aggregation choices shape which health topics reach Facebook audiences and how they engage with them.

## Infrastructure and method for a post-API platform studies

Underwriting much of this research is a quieter methodological argument about how platforms can be studied at all once API access has narrowed. [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats infrastructure exploits YouTube and TikTok's ID-indexing schemes to produce genuinely random samples correcting for the popularity bias of opportunistic datasets, directly addressing the "denominator problem" that haunts claims about platform-wide phenomena (including several papers above). [[Fan2026-af]] proposes treating digital trace data as time-evolving "user-sequences" rather than aggregated cross-sections, illustrated across Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] extend methodological reflection to survey recruitment via Facebook ads and to hybrid TV/Twitter audience analysis during Italian elections, while [[Gardam2025-er]] argues that image-first platforms like Instagram demand visual-first discourse methods rather than text-centric ones, and [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] shows the limits of linking validated psychometric traits to observable Reddit language, cautioning against overconfident computational proxies for personality and toxicity. Read together, these methods papers make explicit what the rest of the topic argues implicitly: as platforms churn through algorithmic, infrastructural, and now AI-driven transformations, the tools for studying audiences and communities must be reinvented almost as often as the platforms themselves.
