# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The unraveling of "social media" as a paradigm

A striking cluster of anniversary-style reflections opens this topic by questioning whether "social media" still names a coherent object of study. [[Boyd2026-op]] argues that the reciprocal, peer-to-peer sociality that once justified the term has given way to one-sided, influencer-dominated "parasocial media." [[Tornberg2026-lc]] pushes this further, proposing that the social-graph-based paradigm is fragmenting into three "post-social" formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies a structural account of one wing of this shift, theorizing TikTok's move from "networked publics" organized around explicit ties to algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" organized around interest similarity—a transformation [[Helmond2026-ll]] situates within a longer arc of "platformization," now entering an AI-centered phase as platforms decouple from open-web signals like the Like button in favor of in-app inference. [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] add historical and moral counterpoints: Marwick's LiveJournal fieldnotes recover a lost register of intimacy and reciprocity foreclosed by extractive contemporary platforms, while Swartz reframes the entire platform economy through the lens of scams, arguing that fraud and legitimacy have become co-constitutive rather than opposed. Together these pieces suggest that the very vocabulary inherited from two decades of "networked publics" scholarship ([[Slack1783508184-sld3]], [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]) needs updating for a landscape of algorithmic curation, synthetic content, and platform consolidation. [[De2026-ld]] shows how platforms themselves manage this narrative of transformation discursively, coining "changecraft" to describe how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X stage infrastructural and ideological pivots as continuity or benevolence rather than rupture.

## Affordances, algorithms, and the shape of publics

A second thread interrogates how specific platform architectures produce specific social and political effects. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates empirically that the same actors (German far-right groups) build structurally distinct networks on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, arguing that affordances are relational outcomes of platform-practice interaction rather than fixed technical properties—a claim echoed by [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], which show immigration and populist campaign discourse being reshaped to fit TikTok's memetic, sound-and-remix logics versus Facebook/X's argumentative registers. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends this to the sonic dimension of TikTok propaganda around the invasion of Ukraine. Audit-style studies interrogate whether algorithms independently bias visibility: [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] finds Twitter/X's post-Musk algorithm rewards proximity to Musk and "agitating" content more than raw political ideology, [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows X's recommender inadvertently encodes a strong linear ideology signal in its embedding space, and [[Brown2026-br]] carefully separates algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding strong "rabbit holes" but weak echo chambers and no radicalization pathway—complicating alarmist narratives. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] examine how deplatformed or fringe figures nonetheless sustain "ambient" visibility through diffuse, recommendation-amplified ecosystems that outpace moderation. Methodologically, several papers push toward better tools for capturing these dynamics: [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] reviews the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy, [[Fan2026-af]] and [[Bruns2025-fz]] propose new frameworks (user-sequences, practice mapping) for modeling temporal and multimodal platform behavior, and [[Zheng2026-bi]] builds infrastructure for genuinely random—rather than popularity-biased—platform samples in a "post-API" research environment.

## Polarization, echo chambers, and the politics of visibility

A substantial cluster revisits classic questions about polarization with more skeptical, structurally attentive eyes. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains a foundational reference point, showing algorithmic curation matters less than individual click choices in suppressing cross-cutting exposure on Facebook. Building on this lineage, [[Green2025-ap]] shows that domain-level partisanship measures obscure enormous story-level heterogeneity, arguing that "curation bubbles" are produced through networked re-bundling rather than simple source selection. [[Schulz2026-ts]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] converge on a related insight: visible online discourse is a skewed, self-selected sliver of opinion, producing "false polarization" that misleads both citizens and researchers who treat social media traces as public-opinion proxies. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates the picture further by showing that political subreddits exhibit minimal cross-ideological interaction even where content is discursively similar, arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than reducible to any one factor—a materialist counterpoint to purely discursive accounts like [[Grusauskaite2026-po]], which locates the cohesion of "Great Reset" conspiracy communities in symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity across six platforms rather than platform affordances per se. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] extend the polarization question to alternative and mainstream media ecosystems, with Zhu showing audience overlap between alternative and mainstream outlets is contingent on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access, while [[Balluff2026-ev]] finds German alternative media reference largely the same named entities as legacy media, differing more in relational network structure than content selection.

## Political communication, emotion, and mobilization

Several papers examine how platform affordances channel political emotion and campaigning. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both center Facebook's Reactions feature as an affective affordance driving mobilization, finding anger reliably predicts sharing while love does not—a pattern that recurs in far-right and populist communication across contexts, including [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s account of positively-valenced, humor-laden softening strategies used by far-right Instagram accounts to normalize extremist content, and [[Groebner2026-pc]]'s mapping of a transnational far-right YouTube audience organized around shared enemies and hyperpartisan hubs like Fox News. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] shows coordinated amplification operating at the level of state-aligned Telegram channels in authoritarian contexts, while [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] offer earlier case studies of hashtag dissent and second-screen political talk-show engagement, respectively, illustrating how platform-specific vernaculars shape political discourse well before the TikTok/algorithmic era. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] extend this to cross-platform curation strategies, showing right-leaning outlets selectively differentiate content between Twitter and Truth Social, and social-justice discourse is politicized both within and across Twitter and YouTube.

## Creator labor, influence, and platform vernaculars

A cluster of papers examines ordinary and professional users navigating platform demands. [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] both theorize content creation as a form of managed, precarious labor: Italian micro-influencers frame second-hand fashion content as care and sustainability while actually pursuing algorithmically-legible survival strategies, and Chinese "AI slop" creators industrialize virality itself through benchmarking and matrix-account systems. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates stereotypes of who produces fringe content, showing ConspiracyTok is dominated by young, non-white, and female creators deploying "populist knowledge production" and a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinforming. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] examine the toxic underside of influencer visibility—showing influencer-sourced misinformation generates more, and qualitatively different, toxicity than equivalent user content, and that Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" humor normalizes cyberbullying-adjacent incivility. [[Graham2026-fb]] offers a lighter counterpoint, reading Reddit "botting" as a participatory-culture phenomenon where authenticity is community-negotiated rather than technically determined. Underlying all of these is Marwick and boyd's foundational account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience ([[Marwick2011-sl02]]), and Farci's account of "networked intimacy" as collaborative, audience-dependent disclosure on Facebook ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]])—both establishing the audience-management logics that later creator-economy studies presuppose.

## AI, trust, and the future of platformed publics

The topic closes on an emergent front: generative AI's incursion into search, news, and deliberation. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google's AI Overviews are discursively contested by platform, journalist, and marketer actors alike, while [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] find audiences largely cannot distinguish, and differentially trust, AI-generated versus human content depending on its institutional framing. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Ng2026-og]] look forward normatively and formally: the former surveys how LLMs might reshape digital public squares through bridging systems and community moderation, the latter argues agentic AI research needs social theory as a "structural prior," using an AI social network (MoltBook) to demonstrate that multi-agent AI populations already exhibit network-dependent, co-evolutionary dynamics familiar from human platform sociality. Read alongside [[Wang2026-ub]]'s account of academic Twitter's failed migration to Mastodon and [[Bruns2026-yv]]'s obituary for Twitter as a space of public debate, this cluster suggests the field's next task is tracing community and publicness not just across human-populated platforms, but into semi-human, algorithmically-generated, and AI-mediated successors—continuing, in transposed form, the questions [[Wedel2026-ix]] poses about what counts as "informative" to audiences no longer anchored in institutional journalism at all.
