# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## Foundational Frameworks: Publics, Audiences, Context

The topic's conceptual bedrock is boyd's account of social network sites as "networked publics" ([[Slack1783508184-sld3]]) and the companion volume theorizing a "networked self" ([[Slack1783508042-sl69]]): platforms are genres of public life structured by affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—that generate collapsed contexts and invisible audiences. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this for Twitter, showing how users manage an "imagined audience" through micro-celebrity, self-censorship, and strategically performed authenticity. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] complicates the privacy-loss reading of context collapse by showing Italian Facebook users turning collapsed, public visibility into a collaborative "networked intimacy," reviving community-like friendship rather than merely eroding it. Fifteen years on, [[Chancellor2026-sld9]] argues that context collapse itself needs retheorizing: algorithmic, multimodal, consumption-oriented platforms require a structural "grammar" of user, platform, and data dimensions rather than the original audience-disclosure model—an early signal, within this very vocabulary, that something foundational has shifted.

## The Unraveling of "Social" Media

A cluster of anniversary essays diagnoses that shift directly. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues TikTok inaugurates "clustered publics"—item-centric, opaque, behaviorally inferred groupings that supplant the interpersonal "networked publics" of the first generation, a transformation other platforms are now mimicking. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] generalizes this into three "post-social" formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—citing Meta's own data that friend-content now occupies a small fraction of feed time. [[Boyd2026-op]] names the resulting mode "parasocial media," where scrolling and one-sided attachment to influencers displace reciprocal posting. [[Helmond2026-ll]] traces the same inflection through political economy, using the death of Facebook's Like button and Big Tech's turn toward AI infrastructure to argue that "platformization" is entering an AI-centered phase that no longer depends on open-web behavioral capture. [[Marwick2026-ss]] supplies the affective counter-memory to these structural diagnoses, contrasting LiveJournal's granular, reciprocal, vulnerability-tolerant intimacy with today's alienating, extractive environment, insisting the trajectory was contingent rather than inevitable. [[Swartz2026-zb]] radicalizes the political-economy thread further, arguing that scams—not sociality—are becoming the organizing logic of platform economies, with anti-fraud infrastructures doubling as governance and surveillance mechanisms.

## Algorithmic Experience, Visibility Labor, and the Platform Game

A parallel lineage tracks how users and creators affectively and strategically inhabit opaque ranking systems. [[Bucher2017-qh]] coins the "algorithmic imaginary" to describe how Facebook users come to feel algorithms through moments of breakdown, profiling, and surprise. [[Cotter2019-js]] extends this into Instagram influencers' deliberate "playing" of the visibility game, distinguishing "relational" from "simulated" influence strategies. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows micro-influencers folding ethical/community-care rhetoric into strategic self-branding as a survival response to the same algorithmic production demands, while [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] synthesizes this fragmented literature into a broader framework of "algorithm literacy." The generative-AI era pushes this logic further: [[Tang2026-gu]] documents Chinese "AI slop" creators who industrialize virality itself through benchmarking and matrix-account systems, while [[Li2026-zv]] shows ordinary users developing folk taxonomies to contest and occasionally enjoy the resulting flood of derivative content—audiences now theorizing not just the algorithm but the machine-authored content it rewards.

## Platform Vernaculars: TikTok, Instagram, and Genre-Specific Cultures

Several studies examine how platform-specific grammars shape substantive discourse. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds pro- and anti-immigration TikTok creators in Canada deploying the same affordances (sound, duet, community hashtags) in symmetrical ways, suggesting a shared vernacular that cuts across ideological divides. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound itself as an underexamined propaganda technique in TikTok disinformation about Ukraine, while [[Cabbuag2024-me]] shows Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" culture normalizing legal-but-harmful humor through platform-specific performance. [[Divon2026-wx]] compares TikTok and Douyin influencers who broker state narratives during crises, showing how ostensibly apolitical creators convert national emergencies into visibility capital differently under democratic versus authoritarian platform governance. [[Tiggemann2020-sl44]] turns to audience reception, showing that "Instagram vs reality" posts can neutralize upward appearance comparison—evidence that audiences actively decode, rather than passively absorb, platform-native authenticity claims, echoing Cotter's and Bucher's emphasis on strategic negotiation from the viewer's side.

## Communities of Belief: Conspiracy, Polarization, and Symbolic Boundary Work

A substantial cluster moves from information to identity and infrastructure in explaining polarized and conspiratorial online communities. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] finds minimal cross-ideological overlap across political subreddits, arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than discourse alone. [[Tuters2026-vo]] extends this infrastructural turn, showing that Great Reset conspiricization is produced by platform grammars of repetition and adjacency that stabilize alignment before any coherent narrative exists. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this with an interactionist account: across six platforms, GR participants construct a shared "critical thinker" identity and reclaim stigma through affective storytelling, regardless of platform-specific affordances. [[Marwick2025-ov]] disrupts the stereotype of the lone white male conspiracist, showing ConspiracyTok's young, diverse creators building intimacy and "generous epistemology" through populist knowledge production. [[Graham2026-fb]] pushes the authenticity question further still, showing Reddit's "botting" culture treats the human/bot distinction as culturally negotiated rather than technical. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues visual-first methods are needed to capture Instagram's image-driven conspiracy and climate-denial discourse, while [[Esau2025-tf]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] bracket the cluster normatively and empirically, asking whether deliberative reciprocity can counter "destructive polarization" and whether alternative platforms actively drive it.

## Migration, Decline, and the Search for Alternatives

Two studies chronicle the empirical collapse of Twitter as a public-communication space. [[Bruns2026-yv]] traces Twitter's arc from prosocial, flat-network public sphere to Musk-era "enshittification," finding that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely clone Twitter's form without resolving its moderation failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] substantiates this with longitudinal data on academic migration to Mastodon, showing that even a highly motivated, well-connected cohort mostly failed to sustain the move, with field-specific servers and cross-server engagement the main predictors of retention. Both findings dovetail with [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Boyd2026-op]]'s broader claim that no simple platform substitution can restore lost reciprocal sociality.

## Multi-Platform Ecosystems, Alternative Media, and the Far Right

A cohesive set of studies insists that extremist and alternative-media communities must be studied across platforms rather than within one. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] shows German far-right networks taking structurally distinct shapes on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, arguing affordances are relational sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed features. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents Swedish far-right YouTubers fusing influencer microcelebrity with political propaganda, using YouTube as a "sanitised" gateway toward more extreme alt-tech platforms. [[Groebner2026-pc]] finds a genuinely transnational far-right audience across Sky News Australia, Fox News, Rebel News, and GB News, with the US occupying a central hub. [[Balluff2026-ev]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] complicate simple alternative/mainstream binaries: German alternative outlets reference largely the same named entities as legacy media but organize them into more cohesive network structures, while audience overlap between alternative and mainstream outlets across six European countries depends heavily on populist parties' institutional access. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] extend the ecosystem view to politicization itself, arguing that discourse (from #StayWoke to "culture wars") is shaped both within and across platforms, and that even ordinary, non-ideological content becomes "weaponized" once embedded in partisan networks.

## Audiences, News, and the Informational Public Sphere

A related set centers audience vernacular theorizing about what counts as legitimate information. [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] both move from institutional to audience-centered definitions—"news-ness" and "informativeness," respectively—showing that young users extend journalistic criteria to influencers and non-journalistic actors while applying their own boundary work to exclude outdated or purely entertaining content. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] supply the overarching "hybrid media system" framework within which such audience practices unfold, arguing power flows through adaptive interdependence between old and new media rather than displacement. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] illustrate specific emergent genres within that hybrid system—counter-hashtag dissent (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) and online firestorms revealed, in the Vietnam-China case, to be coordinated political protest rather than simple reputational crisis.

## Platform Political Economy, Governance, and AI Horizons

Framing the whole topic is [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s foundational argument that platforms constitute a contested "platform society" in which private corporate logics and public values collide across sectors. [[De2026-ld]] shows how platforms discursively manage this contestation through "changecraft"—narrating infrastructural and ideological shifts as continuity or creator benevolence to mask power consolidation. Looking forward, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] examine how large language models might reshape digital public squares and reframe AI systems as "communication partners" rather than intelligences, while [[Ng2026-og]] argues that agentic AI populations require social theory itself as a structural prior—picking up [[Tornberg2026-lc]]'s "AI-mediated communication" formation and extending it into questions of multi-agent governance.

## Methods for an Infrastructure in Flux

Finally, two methodological interventions underwrite much of the above empirical work. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping" via vector embeddings to overcome the "hairball" limits of conventional multimodal network visualization, while [[Zheng2026-bi]] builds genuinely random-sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) to counter the popularity-biased, API-restricted data environment of the "post-API age." Both respond to the same infrastructural condition diagnosed across this topic: as platforms become more opaque, algorithmically mediated, and resistant to independent scrutiny, the tools for studying audiences and communities must themselves be reinvented alongside the platforms they study.
