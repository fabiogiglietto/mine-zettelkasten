# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## From Networked Publics to Post-Social Formations

The corpus opens with a foundational conceptual vocabulary — boyd's account of social network sites as "networked publics" structured by persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability ([[Slack1783508184-sld3]]), and the edited collection theorizing the "networked self" as an identity performed through these affordances ([[Slack1783508042-sl69]]). Marwick and boyd's classic treatment of "context collapse" and the imagined audience ([[Marwick2011-sl02]]) and Gillespie's dissection of the discursive work done by the word "platform" itself ([[Gillespie2010-sla2]]) round out this inherited toolkit: platforms as neutral-sounding infrastructures that nonetheless make substantive editorial and economic interventions.

A striking arc across the most recent papers in this collection is a sustained argument that this vocabulary has expired. Gerbaudo ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) proposes that TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media in which "networked publics" give way to "clustered publics" — item-centric, algorithmically inferred neighborhoods rather than person-centric graphs. Törnberg and colleagues push this further, arguing for a wholesale "post-social media studies" ([[Tornberg2026-lc]]) organized around three emergent formations: algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, citing Meta's own admission that friend-content now constitutes a tiny fraction of feed time. Helmond's decade-later reflection on "platformization" ([[Helmond2026-ll]]) narrates a parallel shift — from the infrastructural spread of Like buttons across the web to an AI-centered phase in which platforms no longer need social-graph signals at all. boyd's own anniversary essay ([[Boyd2026-op]]) proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media," diagnosing a shift from reciprocal peer exchange to one-sided attention toward influencers, a claim that resonates with Swartz's argument that platform economies are increasingly organized around scam logics rather than sociality per se ([[Swartz2026-zb]]). Marwick's companion essay ([[Marwick2026-ss]]) supplies the elegiac counterpoint: a first-person account of LiveJournal-era intimacy, granular privacy, and reciprocal writing, framed as evidence that today's alienating, extractive platforms represent a contingent political-economic choice rather than an inevitable evolution. Together these anniversary reflections stage a genuine debate about whether "social media" remains a coherent object of study at all, and what — parasociality, clustering, scam culture, post-sociality — should replace it analytically.

## Algorithmic Curation, Visibility, and Its Discontents

A second cluster interrogates what recommendation systems actually do to visibility and discourse, often complicating popular narratives. Efstratiou et al.'s audit of pre-X Twitter ([[Efstratiou2025-gs]]) finds that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage was largely explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk rather than ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lost visibility. Bouchaud et al. ([[Bouchaud2026-lr]]) demonstrate more directly that X's recommender embeds a strong latent left-right axis independent of demographics, raising governance and privacy questions, and propose a method for stripping this signal out. Brown and colleagues' careful audit design ([[Brown2026-br]]) separates algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but no evidence for the popular "radicalization pathway" narrative, alongside a platform-wide mild conservative tilt. Smith's "attention broker" study ([[Smith2025-kc]]) offers a causal mechanism — amplification with attribution — for how influential accounts accelerate triad formation and reshape local network structure, a finding that dovetails with Green et al.'s "curation bubbles" ([[Green2025-ap]]), which show that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, and with Bakshy et al.'s earlier, foundational finding that individual click choices narrow cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does ([[Bakshy2015-rn]]). Oswald's "tip of the iceberg" argument ([[Oswald2025-km]]) and Schulz's cross-national study of "false polarization" ([[Schulz2026-ts]]) extend this skepticism about inferring public opinion from platform traces, showing that a vocal, ideologically extreme minority disproportionately produces visible discourse. Gagrcin's review of algorithm literacy ([[Gagrcin2024-dl]]) surveys how users cognitively engage with (or misunderstand) these systems, a fitting bridge between the technical audits and the more interpretive studies of platform vernacular.

Several papers extend this algorithmic lens to specific platform ecologies and genres. Dehghan et al.'s study of political Reddit ([[Dehghan2026-sy]]) argues polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of moderation, discourse, and platform design rather than reducible to any one factor — a methodological cousin to Kakavand's multi-platform mapping of the German far right ([[Kakavand2026-kt]]), which shows the same actors producing structurally distinct networks (broadcast-like on Twitter, hierarchical on Facebook, echo-chambered on Instagram) depending on platform affordances. Rieder's "Tate-space" study ([[Rieder2026-pp]]) and Askanius's mapping of the Swedish far-right YouTube ecosystem ([[Askanius2026-de]]) both show how deplatforming and moderation are structurally outpaced by recommendation-driven circulation and by actors' hybridization of activist and influencer tactics. Groebner's transnational audience-overlap analysis of Anglophone hyperpartisan YouTube news ([[Groebner2026-pc]]) and Balluff's entity-network comparison of German alternative and legacy media ([[Balluff2026-ev]]) similarly use network methods to show that "alternative" media ecosystems are relationally, not just topically, distinct — echoed by Zhu's cross-country audience-overlap study, which finds that the alternative/mainstream boundary is contingent on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access ([[Zhu2026-tn]]).

## Platform-Specific Vernaculars: Genre, Affect, and Performance

A substantial group of papers treats individual platforms as sites with distinctive cultural grammars. On TikTok, Gerbaudo's clustering thesis ([[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]) is complemented by empirical genre studies: Marwick's account of "ConspiracyTok" ([[Marwick2025-ov]]) documents young, disproportionately non-White and female creators building a "populist knowledge production" that functions simultaneously as marginalized standpoint epistemology and disinformation vector; Hollingshead et al. ([[Hollingshead2026-vx]]) find Canadian immigration discourse on TikTok skews pro-immigration and that ideologically opposed users deploy the same affordances symmetrically; Cabbuag documents Filipino "dogshow" culture as a normalized form of legal-but-harmful humor among Gen Z influencers ([[Cabbuag2024-me]]); and Bosch et al. identify sound itself as an underexamined vector of computational propaganda around the Ukraine invasion ([[Bosch2024-hj]]). Tang's study of Chinese "AI slop" creators ([[Tang2026-gu]]) extends this genre-attentiveness to a moment where virality itself becomes an engineered, benchmarked object of production, foreshadowing the volume's turn toward generative AI.

Instagram and Facebook receive complementary treatment. Volpe's ethnography of Italian micro-influencers ([[Volpe2026-um]]) shows how care-branding around second-hand fashion functions as survival strategy under algorithmic and labor precarity — a finding that resonates with Nangle et al.'s analysis of far-right Instagram's "affective-discursive ecology," in which humor and positive affect normalize extremism ([[Nangle2026-yo]]), and with Gardam's call for visual-first methods to analyze Instagram conspiracy discourse ([[Gardam2025-er]]). Farci's earlier study of "networked intimacy" among Italian Facebook users ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]) offers a historical baseline — showing how tagging, liking, and implicit sharing sustain collaborative, public forms of intimacy — against which the volume's newer, more alienated accounts of platform sociality (boyd, Marwick) can be read as a diagnosis of decline. Facebook's Reactions feature receives sustained attention from Kalsnes ([[Kalsnes2025-zb]]), whose Scandinavian election study shows anger reactions predict sharing while love reactions suppress it, and Anwar's systematic review synthesizing this broader "Reactions" literature ([[Anwar2024-34dba628]]).

## Migration, Governance, and the Search for Alternatives

Another throughline concerns what happens when users or content try to move across, or are pushed out of, platform ecosystems. Wang et al.'s study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration ([[Wang2026-ub]]) documents a largely failed exodus despite motivated early adopters, with retention concentrated in discipline-specific servers. Bruns's essay on "the death of Twitter" ([[Bruns2026-yv]]) narrates the platform's decline through mismanagement and weaponized disinformation, arguing that Mastodon, Threads, and Bluesky merely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures — a claim methodologically supported by Wang's findings. Lukito's study of "cross-platformization" among right-leaning outlets moving between Twitter and Truth Social ([[Lukito2026-il]]) offers a case study in selective, non-duplicative platform curation, while De's concept of "changecraft" ([[De2026-ld]]) theorizes how platforms discursively manage and legitimate their own infrastructural changes — Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok all narrating disruption as continuity, benevolence, or democratization. Kristensen's brief inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization ([[Kristensen2025-ni]]) and Kuznetsova's study of coordinated pro-government Telegram amplification in Russia and Belarus ([[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]) extend this governance concern to authoritarian contexts, while Goldberg et al.'s position paper on AI and "digital public squares" ([[Goldberg2026-eb]]) looks forward, proposing LLM-enabled bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, and community moderation as correctives to engagement-optimized decay — a hopeful counterpoint to Bruns's elegy.

## Political Communication, Hashtags, and Hybrid Media

A cluster of papers examines how political communication and collective identity are enacted through platform-specific practices. Giglietto's study of the counter-hashtag #JeNeSuisPasCharlie ([[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]) and Iannelli's account of Twitter as "second screen" during Italian televised talk shows ([[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]) both predate the volume's algorithmic turn, documenting earlier moments when hashtags and hybrid TV/Twitter publics structured political dissent and participation — the latter finding narrow, elite-dominated access rather than genuinely open deliberation. Grusauskaite et al.'s cross-platform study of "Great Reset" conspiracy discourse ([[Grusauskaite2026-po]]) makes an important interactionist argument against techno-centric echo-chamber explanations: symbolic boundary work — reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor, sharing "deep stories" of hardship — constitutes collective identity independent of, and consistently across, six different platforms. Holland-Levin's study of #StayWoke's evolution into "culture wars" framing across Twitter and YouTube ([[Holland_Levin2026-qx]]) and Bouchafra et al.'s cross-platform visual analysis of the Sweden Democrats' EU campaign ([[Bouchafra2026-ts]]) both demonstrate that politicization and securitization operate both within and across platforms simultaneously — the latter showing how TikTok's memetic affordances are mobilized differently than Facebook's or X's argumentative registers to target younger, more affectively responsive audiences.

## Toxicity, Dark Traits, and the Limits of Anonymity

A smaller but pointed group interrogates incivility and toxicity as source- and trait-dependent phenomena rather than simple platform byproducts. Di Domenico et al. ([[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]) show that influencer-sourced misinformation generates more, and qualitatively different, toxicity than identical content from regular users — notably on low-pseudonymity platforms, contradicting assumptions that anonymity alone drives toxicity. Cerulli et al.'s linkage of validated dark personality questionnaires to actual Reddit text ([[Cerulli2026-sl75]]) complicates computational personality-inference research, finding that self-reported trait scores predict self-reported incivility but not measurable linguistic toxicity — a caution against over-reading textual signals as proxies for underlying psychology. Graham's study of Reddit "botting" ([[Graham2026-fb]]) extends this skepticism about clean categorization to the human/bot boundary itself, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical fact, sustained by participatory culture and community indulgence rather than fame-seeking.

## Methods and Infrastructure for a Post-API Landscape

A final connective thread concerns the methodological apparatus available to study all of the above, especially as platforms restrict access. Zheng's TubeStats/TokStats tools ([[Zheng2026-bi]]) address the "denominator problem" of representative sampling in the post-API age; Fan's framework for mining temporal "user-sequences" from cross-platform trace data ([[Fan2026-af]]) argues for a "temporal turn" beyond aggregated cross-sectional measures; Bruns's "practice mapping" approach ([[Bruns2025-fz]]) proposes vector embeddings as a remedy to the "hairball" problem in multimodal network visualization; and Lai's method for scaling YouTube video ideology via Reddit-sharing patterns ([[Lai2024-to]]) and Mosleh's seven-platform comparison of partisan/low-quality news engagement ([[Mosleh2024-op]]) both push back against the field's historical overreliance on Twitter and Facebook as proxies for "social media" writ large. Ng et al.'s formal "Multi-Agent Social Systems" framework ([[Ng2026-og]]) gestures toward where this methodological project may be heading: treating agentic AI populations themselves as objects requiring the same structural-sociological priors long applied to human platform publics.

## Generative AI as the New Frontier

Closing out the topic, several papers turn explicitly to generative AI's incursion into platform information ecosystems — the empirical leading edge of the "post-social" diagnoses opening this note. Weinbrand's discourse analysis of Google's AI Overviews ([[Weinbrand2026-sf]]) shows Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contesting the meaning of generative search, with the user's own voice conspicuously absent from all three framings. Gilardi et al.'s survey experiment ([[Gilardi2026-hw]]) finds that audiences cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, though disclosure paradoxically boosts short-term engagement without shifting long-term acceptance. Sbaraini-Fontes's Italian survey ([[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]) complements this by finding that AI-generated search results are trusted more than AI-produced news outright — suggesting that where AI is institutionally positioned matters as much as what it produces. Wedel's audience-centered concept of "informativeness" ([[Wedel2026-ix]]) reframes what young adults consider valuable content beyond journalistic norms, a fitting final note for a topic that, across sixty-two papers, keeps circling back to the same question in new technological guises: what "social," "public," and "audience" even mean once platforms — and now their algorithms and AI systems — stand between people and each other.
