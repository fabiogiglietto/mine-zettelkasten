# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## From Networked Publics to Post-Social Media

The topic's intellectual spine runs through a foundational vocabulary—boyd's account of social network sites as "networked publics" structured by persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability [[Slack1783508184-sld3]], the edited collection theorizing the "networked self" as an emergent unit of identity and community [[Slack1783508042-sl69]], and Marwick and boyd's classic analysis of "context collapse," in which Twitter users manage an audience they can never fully know [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. These pieces set the terms—affordances, imagined audience, self-presentation as performance—that almost every later paper in this collection either extends or explicitly abandons.

Two decades on, a striking cluster of anniversary essays for *Social Media + Society* declares this founding paradigm exhausted. boyd herself argues that "social media" has become "parasocial media," dominated by scrolling and influencer content rather than reciprocal peer exchange [[Boyd2026-op]]. Baym revisits her own 2015 diagnosis and finds every threat she named—inequality, opacity, precarious labor, data extraction—has worsened into a "broligarchy" [[Baym2026-tr]]. Marwick turns autoethnographic, contrasting LiveJournal's granular privacy and reciprocal writing culture with today's alienating, surveilled platforms, insisting the current configuration was a political-economic choice, not a technological inevitability [[Marwick2026-ss]]. Helmond, revisiting her own coinage of "platformization," traces its drift from open-web infrastructural expansion to an AI-centered phase symbolized by the retirement of the Like button and the migration of Big Tech toward "Big AI" infrastructure [[Helmond2026-ll]]. Törnberg and colleagues formalize this mood into a thesis: the social-media paradigm is dissolving into three "post-social" formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—each demanding new methods [[Tornberg2026-lc]]. Bruns supplies a platform-specific case history of this decline, narrating Twitter's fall from a uniquely open, prosocial space for hashtag activism and crisis communication into a "enshittified" fringe platform under Musk, and finding that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) replicate its form without solving its governance failures [[Bruns2026-yv]].

## The Algorithmic Reconfiguration of Sociality

Gerbaudo gives this shift its sharpest architectural theorization: TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media built on *clustered publics*—statistically inferred neighborhoods of shared interest, grounded in implicit signals like watch time—superseding the *networked publics* of friending and following that boyd had theorized [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]. This item-centric, opaque, "TikTokified" architecture is precisely what Helmond and Törnberg describe as displacing the social graph, and Fan et al.'s cross-platform trace-data study offers empirical texture to the claim: users' TikTok sessions are shorter, more habitual, and less prone to cross-platform switching than Facebook/Instagram sessions, suggesting genuinely different temporal rhythms of engagement across "generations" of platform [[Fan2026-af]]. Ng et al. push the algorithmic-architecture argument into the domain of AI agents themselves, arguing that multi-agent AI systems require social-theoretic priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) rather than single-task framings—an argument for treating even non-human platform populations as governed by the same relational logics that have long structured human networked publics [[Ng2026-og]].

## Creators, Labor, and Platform Culture

A second cluster examines how ordinary users and micro-influencers *inhabit* these algorithmic architectures. Volpe's ethnography of Italian second-hand-fashion micro-influencers on Instagram and TikTok shows care, sustainability rhetoric, and community-building operating simultaneously as genuine ethical stance and as reputational labor demanded by platform culture—survival strategies under dual precarity rather than either pure authenticity or pure branding [[Volpe2026-um]]. Marwick's study of U.S. "ConspiracyTok" complicates the demographic stereotype of the conspiracy theorist, showing young, non-white, and female creators using platform-specific evidentiary aesthetics (greenscreen, deep lore, personal testimony) to construct a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone [[Marwick2025-ov]]. Nangle and colleagues extend this into far-right normalization on Instagram, where humor and positive affect—not overt hostility—soften extremist content within an "affective-discursive ecology" shaped by algorithmic proximity [[Nangle2026-yo]]. Cabbuag's account of Filipino Gen Z "dogshows" on TikTok shows how legal-but-harmful humor is collectively negotiated as an accepted cultural practice, again with platform affordances actively shaping the genre [[Cabbuag2024-me]]. Hollingshead et al.'s content analysis of Canadian TikTok immigration discourse offers a partial counterweight: across ideological positions, users deploy the platform's affordances symmetrically, and pro-immigration content substantially outweighs hostile framing—suggesting TikTok's memetic, content-centric architecture is a "double-edged resource" rather than uniformly toxic [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]. Together these studies suggest platform culture is less a fixed determinant than a set of demands—for constancy, affect, humor, evidentiary style—that users of every ideological stripe learn to perform.

## Continuities of Intimacy and Audience

Set against the anniversary essays' narrative of loss, a smaller thread asks what intimacy and audience-sense actually look like across platform eras. Farci's interview study of Italian Facebook users identifies "networked intimacy"—a repertoire of collaborative disclosure practices (showing rather than telling, tagging, liking, implicit sharing) that depends on friends' cooperation to blur public and private boundaries, refuting narcissism-centered readings of social media intimacy [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]. This resonates with, but predates, Marwick's LiveJournal nostalgia [[Marwick2026-ss]], suggesting the reciprocal, curated intimacy she associates with the "old internet" persisted well into the Facebook era before the parasocial/algorithmic turn boyd and Baym describe. Wedel et al. extend the audience-centered lens to informativeness rather than intimacy, showing young adults judge YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok content as "informative" through platform-specific criteria—self-selection, actor-mediation, algorithmic curation—that extend journalistic norms to influencers and peers alike, again complicating any single narrative of platform-driven decline [[Wedel2026-ix]].

## Polarization, Conspiracy, and the Fragmented Public

A substantial subset interrogates whether platform architecture drives ideological fragmentation. Bakshy, Messing, and Adamic's foundational Facebook study complicates strong filter-bubble narratives: friend-network composition and individual clicking, more than the News Feed algorithm, suppress cross-cutting exposure [[Bakshy2015-rn]]. Giglietto's analysis of the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag shows how dissenting discourse forms within moments of platformed solidarity [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]. Dehghan et al.'s sixteen-year study of political Reddit finds polarization is "sedimented"—an entanglement of moderation, platform design, and discourse—rather than reducible to filter bubbles, directly challenging the town-square framing of cross-ideological subreddits [[Dehghan2026-sy]]. Grusauskaite et al. push past platform-centric explanations altogether: across six platforms, "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity—reclaiming stigma, sharing "deep stories"—largely independent of platform affordances [[Grusauskaite2026-po]]. Zhu et al.'s cross-country audience-overlap networks similarly relativize alternative media's marginality to national media systems and populist parties' institutional access rather than platform design per se [[Zhu2026-tn]]. Kristensen's brief note on alternative platforms and discursive polarization and Mosleh et al.'s seven-platform comparison of partisan/low-quality news engagement both reinforce the collection's recurring methodological argument: single-platform (especially Twitter-centric) findings do not generalize [[Kristensen2025-ni]] [[Mosleh2024-op]]. Hourigan et al.'s digital-diary study of everyday misinformation extends this beyond politics into banal domains like cost-of-living content, finding mainstream/alternative news outlets, not fringe actors, are the perceived main propagators—a sociotechnical, trust-crisis account of misinformation perception [[Hourigan2026-oc]].

## Methods for a Platformized, Algorithmic Field

A final cluster supplies the methodological infrastructure this topic increasingly requires. Smith et al. formalize "attention brokerage" (tertius amplificans) as a causal mechanism of triad formation on Twitter/X, showing amplification by high-degree nodes reshapes follower networks beyond background virality [[Smith2025-kc]]. Graham et al.'s study of Reddit "botting" destabilizes the human/bot binary itself, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance within participatory culture rather than a technical property [[Graham2026-fb]]. Arminio et al. demonstrate that vision-language models substantially improve interpretable, connotative image clustering over CNN pipelines, addressing the "visual turn" in computational social science [[Arminio2025-tw]]. Cerulli et al. link validated dark-personality measures to Reddit text, finding self-reported toxicity aligns with observable language while trait scores themselves do not—a caution against over-reading computational proxies for personality [[Cerulli2026-sl75]]. Anwar et al.'s review of Facebook Reactions research and Gagrcin et al.'s integrative review of algorithm literacy both survey fragmented sub-literatures in search of conceptual coherence [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]. Iannelli et al. demonstrate a Facebook-ad-based recruitment method for reaching niche, hard-to-find populations (here, conspiracy believers), useful precisely as API access to platforms tightens [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]. Wang et al.'s longitudinal study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration shows that even highly motivated, well-networked communities largely failed to sustain migration, with retention hinging on discipline-specific servers and cross-server engagement rather than internal cohesion—an empirical coda to Bruns's narrative of platform succession [[Wang2026-ub]]. Fattorini et al., finally, gesture toward where this infrastructure is heading, documenting Italian publics' "critical ambivalence" toward AI—rising AI-tool use alongside rising perception of AI as threat—a reminder that the platforms this topic studies are themselves being remade by the generative-AI shift that Törnberg, Helmond, and Ng et al. all identify as the next post-social frontier [[Fattorini2026-bo]].
