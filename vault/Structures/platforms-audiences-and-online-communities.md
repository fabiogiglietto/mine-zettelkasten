# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Unraveling of "Social" Media

The most striking pattern across this collection is a wave of anniversary and reflective essays—many written for *Social Media + Society*'s twentieth-anniversary issue—that converge on a single diagnosis: the thing scholars once called "social media" no longer exists, or exists only as a residue. [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes that platforms should be renamed "parasocial media" since scrolling has displaced posting and one-sided attention to influencers has displaced mutual friendship. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] extends this into a full paradigmatic argument, identifying three "post-social" formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—that have fractured the unified "social media" object of study. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] supplies the mechanism: a shift from "networked publics" organized around explicit ties to "clustered publics" organized by algorithmically inferred interest, with TikTok as the template that other platforms are now imitating. [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits her own foundational concept of platformization a decade later, using Facebook's retirement of the Like button to argue that platforms have decoupled from the open-web social graph entirely in favor of AI-driven inference—entering an "AI-centered phase" of platformization that increasingly touches infrastructure well beyond social media (space, energy, defense). Together these pieces trace a periodization: platforms built on reciprocal connection giving way to platforms built on algorithmic prediction, spectatorship, and synthetic content.

Two elegiac companions to this diagnosis—[[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]]—supply the affective and political-economic underside of the story. Marwick's autoethnographic return to LiveJournal insists that the intimacy and reciprocity of the "old internet" were not naive illusions but real affordances of small-scale, semi-private, granularly filtered communities, foreclosed by the surveillance-and-scale logics that came after. Swartz, writing from research on cryptocurrency and fintech, argues that scam culture is not peripheral noise on today's platforms but a central organizing logic, with anti-fraud infrastructure itself becoming a governance mechanism that authorizes surveillance. Read alongside [[De2026-ld]], which shows how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively manage—rather than conceal—their own transformations through "changecraft" (visible aesthetics, continuity-framing, incremental patchworking), this cluster paints platform change as an actively narrated, legitimated process rather than a neutral drift, one that consistently benefits platform power while eroding older forms of sociality.

## Foundational Frameworks and Their Afterlives

Underpinning all of this is the earlier theoretical vocabulary the newer essays are explicitly arguing with or extending. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] (boyd's "networked publics" chapter) and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] (the *Networked Self* volume) established the baseline account of SNS affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—and their dynamics of invisible audiences and context collapse. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalized this for Twitter specifically, showing how users manage context collapse by imagining audiences and performing "authenticity" as a strategic accomplishment rather than an essence—a theme that recurs, decades later, in [[Volpe2026-um]]'s account of micro-influencers performing care and expertise as survival strategy, and in [[Graham2026-fb]]'s argument that authenticity itself (human vs. bot) is a culturally negotiated performance on Reddit rather than a technical fact. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] rounds out this foundational layer by showing how the very word "platform" was rhetorically constructed to claim neutrality while enabling editorial power—a move that the AI-era essays ([[Helmond2026-ll]]; [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] on Google's AI Overviews) show recurring in new form, as companies again claim infrastructural neutrality while exercising epistemic authority.

## Platform-Specific Cultures: TikTok, Instagram, and the Politics of Visibility

A dense cluster of papers examines how specific platforms' affordances shape identity performance, ideology, and commerce. On TikTok, [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s study of "ConspiracyTok" shows young, disproportionately non-White and female creators building "populist knowledge production" through visual evidence and intimacy cues, complicating the stereotype of the conspiracy theorist and revealing a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously empowering and disinformation-prone. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds, contrary to Twitter-based immigration research, that Canadian TikTok skews pro-immigration, with ideologically opposed users nonetheless deploying the platform's affordances (hashtags, sound, community Toks) in strikingly symmetrical ways—suggesting a shared platform vernacular cross-cutting ideology, echoed in [[Bouchafra2026-ts]]'s finding that the Sweden Democrats shift from argumentative Facebook/X posts to memetic TikTok content aimed at younger voters, and in [[Cabbuag2024-me]]'s account of Filipino Gen-Z "dogshows" normalizing legal-but-harmful humor. [[Bosch2024-hj]] adds a sonic dimension often neglected in disinformation research, showing sound as a platform-specific vector for Ukraine-war propaganda, while [[Luceri2025-tr]] argues that coordinated-inauthentic-behavior detection built for text platforms cannot simply be ported to TikTok's video-first structure. On Instagram, [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right actors using positive affect and humour as "multimodal softening" within an "affective-discursive ecology," and [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] both push methodology toward visual-first and connotative analysis, arguing text-centric tools miss how image-driven platforms carry ideological and conspiratorial meaning. [[Volpe2026-um]] closes this cluster by showing how Instagram/TikTok's demand for constant, algorithmically-legible output reshapes even ethically-framed content (second-hand fashion) into a survival strategy under precarity—directly continuing Marwick and boyd's earlier account of strategic self-presentation, now intensified by platform-imposed production demands.

## Polarization, Toxicity, and the Limits of Platform-Level Explanation

A second major thread interrogates whether polarization, toxicity, and radicalization are produced by platforms themselves or by user self-selection and community dynamics that platforms merely channel. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s sixteen-year study of political subreddits finds "sedimented" polarization irreducible to discourse, design, or moderation alone, arising instead from their entanglement—directly challenging the idea of any subreddit as a cross-ideological town square. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] similarly resists techno-centric explanation, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities across six platforms are held together by symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label—rather than by platform-specific mechanics. [[Brown2026-br]] intervenes directly in the YouTube radicalization debate, using an audit design to show that echo chambers are driven mainly by user choice, rabbit holes are real but Markovian rather than escalating, and radicalization pathways don't exist—although the algorithm nudges everyone moderately rightward. [[Schulz2026-ts]] generalizes this skepticism about pure platform-effects explanations across a 40-country dataset, showing that "false polarization" online stems substantially from self-selection: people with stronger attitudes post more, distorting the visible discourse. Against this, [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment offers causal evidence that engagement-based ranking algorithms *do* amplify moralized, emotional content and distort perceived social norms, and that redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithms can mitigate this—suggesting platform design matters even if it isn't the whole story. [[Esau2025-tf]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] frame the normative stakes and the role of alternative platforms in this same polarization debate.

Toxicity research within this cluster increasingly locates cause not in content or platform alone but in source and personality. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows influencer-sourced misinformation generates more toxicity than identical content from regular users, driven by credibility legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment," notably strongest on identity-linked (low-pseudonymity) platforms—complicating anonymity-centric accounts of online toxicity. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] complements this at the individual level, finding dark personality traits correlate with self-reported incivility but not with computationally detected linguistic toxicity, exposing a gap between trait and textual manifestation. [[Graham2026-fb]]'s Reddit "botting" study and [[Smith2025-kc]]'s concept of the "attention broker" (tertius amplificans) both examine how attention and participation propagate through platform-specific mechanics—amplification-driven triad formation in the latter case—showing structural rather than purely cultural drivers of visibility.

## Cross-Platform and Multi-Platform Methodologies

A cluster of papers argues that single-platform study designs, long dominant in the field (especially Twitter/Facebook-centric research), systematically misrepresent the broader ecosystem. [[Mosleh2024-op]] shows partisan/low-quality news engagement diverges meaningfully across seven platforms. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that German far-right networks take structurally different shapes on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, arguing affordances are relational sociotechnical outcomes rather than fixed platform properties—a claim [[Lukito2026-il]] operationalizes empirically by showing right-leaning outlets curate distinct content for Twitter versus Truth Social rather than duplicating it. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] examines politicization of social-justice discourse as both platform-specific and cross-platform "synergistic," while [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] extend cross-source comparison to alternative-versus-mainstream media audience and entity networks, both finding less structural divergence than assumed. Methodologically, [[Fan2026-af]], [[Bruns2025-fz]], [[Zheng2026-bi]], and [[Arminio2025-tw]] push computational innovation to match this cross-platform ambition: temporal sequence-mining of donated trace data, "practice mapping" via vector embeddings to escape the network-visualization "hairball," random-sampling infrastructure to counter the post-API "denominator problem," and VLLM-based connotative image clustering, respectively—all responding to the sense that existing tools, built for an earlier social-graph paradigm, are no longer adequate (a concern [[Tornberg2026-lc]] states explicitly).

## Twitter/X's Decline and the Unfinished Migration

A tight sub-cluster tracks the specific unraveling of Twitter/X as a public-communication infrastructure. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter's arc from prosocial, flat-network openness to Musk-era "enshittification," arguing that successor platforms merely clone Twitter's form without solving its governance failures. [[Wang2026-ub]] provides granular empirical confirmation: even highly motivated academic early adopters largely failed to sustain migration to Mastodon, with retention driven by niche server communities and cross-server engagement rather than strong internal networks—a sobering complement to Bruns's broader claim that no successor platform has yet built adequate moderation and community infrastructure to replace what Twitter once offered.

## Political Communication, Affect, and Emotion on Platforms

Rounding out the topic, several papers examine how platform affordances structure specifically political-emotional communication. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] both interrogate Facebook's Reactions feature as an "affective affordance," finding anger reactions predict sharing while love reactions suppress it, and that populist actors exploit this asymmetry—directly extending [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s earlier account of "networked intimacy," where Liking and Tagging function as low-cost relational signals, into the terrain of political mobilization. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation itself as cross-platform cultural narrative rather than discrete false claims, echoing the emotion/identity emphasis found in [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Nangle2026-yo]]. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s early case study of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie anticipates this narrative-and-dissent framing on hashtag activism. Finally, [[Much2026-gu]] extends the affective/identity lens to podcasts as "gendered media spaces," showing how non-political entertainment media can produce incidental ideological sorting—a reminder that the platform-audience dynamics this topic traces are no longer confined to conventionally defined "social media" at all, looping back to the opening cluster's claim that the object of study itself is dissolving.
