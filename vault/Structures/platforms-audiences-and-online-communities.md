# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The Arc: From Networked Publics to Post-Social Fragments

The papers filed here trace an arc that begins with foundational theorizing of social media as "networked publics" and ends with scholars questioning whether "social media" remains a coherent object of study at all. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] establish the conceptual baseline: social network sites are genres of publics structured by affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—that generate predictable dynamics like context collapse and invisible audiences. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this for Twitter, showing how users manage an "imagined audience" through strategic self-presentation and micro-celebrity performance. These early-2010s accounts assume a world of explicit, navigable social graphs.

That world, this collection insists, is gone. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] provides the pivot: TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media organized around algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" rather than explicit "networked publics," a shift several papers treat as epochal. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] radicalizes this into a full periodization, arguing that algorithmic recommendation, generative AI, and user retreat into private spaces are dissolving the "social media" paradigm itself, replaced by algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication. [[Helmond2026-ll]] narrates the same transition from the infrastructural side, revisiting "platformization" a decade on and finding Meta's retirement of the Like button emblematic of a move from social-graph capture to AI-driven inference. The anniversary-essay cluster from *Social Media + Society*—[[Boyd2026-op]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Marwick2026-ss]]—converges on a shared verdict: platforms once built for reciprocity now reward scrolling, parasocial attachment to influencers, and algorithmic curation over peer exchange, a trajectory Boyd proposes renaming "parasocial media" and Marwick mourns through autoethnographic contrast with LiveJournal's granular, intimate publics. [[Bruns2026-yv]] supplies the case study of this decline for Twitter specifically, tracing its fall from prosocial hashtag activism and crisis communication to "enshittification" under Musk, and arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely fail to solve the underlying governance problem. [[Wang2026-ub]] offers granular empirical confirmation: even highly motivated academic early-adopters could not sustain migration to Mastodon, with retention hinging on niche server communities rather than replicated social capital—evidence that decentralization alone cannot resurrect networked-public intimacy.

## Platform Affordances as Differentiators

A second thread treats affordances not as epochal ruptures but as variables that produce divergent outcomes across platforms and contexts, resisting any single "social media" logic. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] is exemplary: far-right actors build structurally distinct networks on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram, because affordances are relational and sociotechnical rather than fixed—connectivity, replicability, and scalability manifest differently depending on user practice. [[Mosleh2024-op]] extends this comparative logic to news-sharing quality across seven platforms, critiquing the field's Twitter/Facebook myopia, a critique [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] answers directly by showing that TikTok's immigration discourse in Canada skews pro-immigration—inverting patterns found on X—even as ideologically opposed users deploy the platform's affordances symmetrically. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric differently per platform—argumentative text on Facebook/X, memetic virality on TikTok—while [[Zhu2026-tn]] and Balluff2026-po (network-analytic studies of alternative-mainstream media relations) similarly find that "alternative" media's distinctiveness is structural and contextual rather than an inherent property, varying by national media system and political access. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] poses the underlying question these studies answer empirically: do alternative platforms drive discursive polarization, or does polarization depend on entangled contextual factors? [[Dehghan2026-sy]] gives the most theorized version of this entanglement, arguing that political polarization on Reddit is "sedimented" through an inseparable mesh of platform design, moderation, and discourse—not reducible to any single affordance.

## Audiences, Attention, and the Politics of Circulation

Where the first cluster asks what platforms have *become*, a parallel cluster asks how audiences make sense of and act within algorithmically mediated attention economies. [[Smith2025-kc]] formalizes a causal mechanism—the "attention broker" or *tertius amplificans*—showing how amplification by high-degree accounts accelerates the formation of new social ties, offering a structural account of how virality reshapes networks from within. [[Schulz2026-ts]] complicates perceptions of that visible discourse, demonstrating that apparent political polarization online is substantially an artifact of self-selection: ideologically stronger users post more, distorting the visible opinion landscape relative to underlying attitudes—a finding that dovetails with [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theoretical case for "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as correctives to perceived (if partly illusory) polarization. [[Wedel2026-ix]] shifts to reception, proposing "informativeness" as an audience-centered alternative to institutional definitions of news, showing young adults judge TikTok, YouTube, and Instagram content by relevance, usefulness, and perceived expertise rather than journalistic credentials—a democratization of epistemic authority that resonates with [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]'s finding that Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, marking automated intermediaries as an ascendant, under-scrutinized locus of trust.

## Identity, Intimacy, and Community Formation

A throughline concerned with identity and belonging connects the platform-history essays to more localized ethnographies. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]'s concept of "networked intimacy" among Italian Facebook users—collaborative, public disclosure practices like tagging and implicit sharing—anticipates Marwick's later elegy for LiveJournal-style intimacy, both suggesting that meaningful community formation depends on affordances (privacy filters, small audiences) that mega-platforms have since stripped away. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes this into ideological territory, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through platform-specific mechanics than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming stigmatized identity labels, sharing "deep stories" of grievance—consistent across six platforms, a finding that qualifies techno-deterministic accounts of radicalization. [[Marwick2025-ov]] extends this identity-centered lens to ConspiracyTok, revealing that conspiracy content is now produced disproportionately by young, non-white, and female creators using "generous epistemology" and affective intimacy to build trust—simultaneously empowering marginalized standpoints and laundering disinformation. [[Graham2026-fb]] takes participatory identity to its most playful extreme, showing Reddit's "botting" culture treats authenticity as a negotiated performance rather than a technical fact, challenging the human/bot binary itself. [[Volpe2026-um]] grounds these dynamics in labor: Italian micro-influencers' care-branded second-hand-fashion content is best read as survival strategy under precarity and algorithmic demand, echoing [[Tang2026-gu]]'s account of Chinese "AI slop" creators who convert virality itself into an engineered, industrialized production process—both signaling a broader reconfiguration of "creator" identity under platform and AI pressure.

## Extremism, Moderation, and the Limits of Governance

A dense sub-cluster examines how platform architectures enable, launder, and resist far-right and extremist content. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s sweeping scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research supplies the field-level frame: online/offline separation is untenable, and mechanisms operate simultaneously across macro, meso, and micro levels. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] each detail specific "ambient" strategies of normalization—Instagram's affectively "softened" far-right Reels, YouTube's diffuse "Tate-space" that outpaces moderation via recommendation-driven circulation. [[Groebner2026-pc]] scales this up transnationally, finding sustained cross-national commenter overlap among English-language hyperpartisan YouTube channels, with the US far right as central hub. [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates a platform-specific propaganda vector—TikTok's sonic affordances—in coverage of the Ukraine invasion, while [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents a parallel normalization of incivility through Filipino Gen Z "dogshow" humor. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s earlier case of #JeNeSuisPasCharlie shows dissenting discursive strategies emerging even within moments of mass platformed solidarity, a reminder that counter-discourse and boundary-work long predate the algorithmic era. Across this cluster, [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" supplies a meta-level account of how platforms themselves narrate their own governance and feature changes to mask power consolidation—suggesting that moderation failures documented elsewhere are not incidental but discursively managed as acceptable costs of continuous "improvement."

## Method and Infrastructure

Finally, several papers are less about sociality than about the tools needed to study it under these conditions. [[Arminio2025-tw]] advances computational method by using VLLMs to capture connotative (not merely denotative) meaning in image clustering, addressing the "visual turn" the far-right and conspiracy studies above depend on. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] reviews the fragmented construct of "algorithm literacy," an increasingly urgent competency given the clustered-public logics [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] describe. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] jointly interrogate Facebook's Reactions feature as an affective affordance shaping political virality—anger consistently outperforming love in driving shares—linking platform-design granularity back to the political-communication and polarization concerns of Dehghan, Kristensen, and Schulz. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s hybrid-media analysis of Italian second-screen politics, though earlier, anticipates this collection's persistent argument: that social media practices are never autonomous but always co-constituted with older media logics, institutional context, and audience self-selection—an insight that, decades on, the collection's newest entries suggest may finally be outrunning the very category of "social media" itself, and finally [[Swartz2026-zb]] closes the loop by arguing that scam culture—rather than sociality per se—has become platform capitalism's organizing logic, the endpoint of the extractive drift Baym, Boyd, and Marwick diagnose from the vantage of the old internet.
