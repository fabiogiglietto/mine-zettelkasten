# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

### The Political Economy of Platform Sociality

A throughline across these papers is the recognition that platform architectures are not neutral stages but active shapers — and often deformers — of online sociality. [[Marwick2026-ss]] provides the most explicit statement of this thesis in autoethnographic register: the extractive, surveillance-driven mega-platforms of today were a contingent political-economic choice, and their affective texture (alienation, boredom, hostility to vulnerability) contrasts sharply with the reciprocal intimacies once possible on LiveJournal. That framing serves as a useful lens for reading the more empirical contributions here, each of which documents how contemporary platform logics inflect what users can be, say, or build together.

[[Wang2026-ub]] operationalises this concern at infrastructural scale, showing that even coordinated, motivated academics could not sustain migration from Twitter to Mastodon after Musk's takeover. Their finding that field-specific servers (infosec.exchange) retained users at nearly six times the rate of general-purpose ones echoes Marwick's intuition that small, coherent, subcultural spaces sustain community in ways scale-oriented platforms cannot — even as their evidence that large prior Twitter networks *predicted* attrition on Mastodon suggests that social capital built under one platform logic does not migrate cleanly to another.

### Creator Labour and the Performance of Care

Where Marwick reads the platform environment from the position of the disenchanted user, [[Volpe2026-um]] takes up the perspective of those actively producing under its constraints. Italian micro-influencers in second-hand fashion perform "care," sustainability, and expertise, but Volpe refuses to treat these as either cynical branding or authentic ethics; they are survival strategies through which precarious workers meet algorithmic demands for constant output while accruing reputational capital toward more stable employment. This dovetails with Marwick's critique — the extractive infrastructure conscripts even ostensibly ethical performances — but it also complicates any nostalgic reading, since these creators have no "old internet" to return to; they must instead craft affective legibility inside the current regime.

[[Cabbuag2024-me]] tracks a darker variant of the same dynamic among Filipino Gen Z TikTok influencers, where "dogshows" normalise legal-but-harmful humour that shades into cyberbullying. Here platform affordances amplify not care but incivility, and the audience is not a passive receiver but a co-producer of the practice. Read together, [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] suggest that influencer cultures crystallise whatever affective register best exploits algorithmic amplification — care in one niche, cruelty in another — with audience collaboration shaping the outcome as much as creator intent.

### Audience Practices and the Reconstitution of Publics

The remaining papers redirect analytic attention from platforms and creators toward audiences themselves. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] identifies "networked intimacy" on Facebook — a repertoire of showing rather than telling, implicit sharing, tagging, tacit mutual understanding, and liking through which intimate meaning is co-constructed with a friendship network. This is a suggestive counterpoint to [[Marwick2026-ss]]: where Marwick sees intimacy foreclosed by the current platform order, Farci finds it reconstituted, though in more oblique and semi-public forms that depend on audience literacy rather than granular privacy controls.

[[Wedel2026-ix]] extends the audience-centered turn to the question of what counts as informative, arguing through a Hallian encoding/decoding lens that young adults judge informativeness across five dimensions — practices, relevance, actor cues, usefulness, and platform cues — largely detached from institutional journalism. Influencers can rank as more informative than legacy news when they perform expertise and thematic focus, and platform logics (self-selection on YouTube, algorithmic on TikTok) shape which content is deemed suitable for which epistemic purpose. This complements [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] in treating audiences as competent interpreters, and speaks back to [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] by clarifying the audience-side criteria — expertise, relatability, usefulness — that reward care-performances and dogshows alike.

### Convergences and Tensions

Two arguments run through the set. First, platforms matter as infrastructures with distinctive affordance-profiles: LiveJournal's filters ([[Marwick2026-ss]]), Mastodon's federation ([[Wang2026-ub]]), TikTok's algorithmic amplification ([[Cabbuag2024-me]], [[Wedel2026-ix]]), Facebook's network-visibility structure ([[Farci2017-2a88c00e]]), and Instagram/TikTok's creator economies ([[Volpe2026-um]]) each produce distinct sociabilities. Second, community and meaning are increasingly *collaborative accomplishments* that require audience competence — whether decoding implicit intimate cues, granting influencer expertise, or normalising harmful humour.

A productive tension remains between Marwick's declensionist framing and the more agentive audience-and-creator practices documented by the others. If the political economy is as constraining as [[Marwick2026-ss]] argues, how do we account for the durable intimacies of [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] or the discerning informativeness judgments of [[Wedel2026-ix]]? Conversely, if audiences and creators are this competent, what precisely was lost with the old internet? The corpus points toward a synthesis: platform logics do not determine sociality but powerfully bias it, and the analytic task is to map how audience practices, creator strategies, and infrastructural affordances co-produce the specific textures of online community in each site.
