# Platforms, Audiences, and Online Communities

## The unraveling of "social media" as a category

A striking number of papers in this collection are less empirical studies than diagnostic essays asking whether "social media" still names a coherent object. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues that TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of platforms organized around algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" rather than the explicit, people-centric "networked publics" that defined the Facebook/Twitter era. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] radicalizes this claim, proposing that the entire social-media paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations—algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication—as feeds shift from friend content to recommendation and users retreat into bounded spaces. [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Helmond2026-ll]] arrive at kindred conclusions from different angles: boyd proposes renaming the object "parasocial media" now that scrolling and influencer-consumption have displaced reciprocal posting, while Helmond, revisiting her own coinage of "platformization," traces how Meta's abandonment of the Like button signals a move from social-graph capture to AI-driven inference and a new frontier of "Big AI" infrastructure. [[Swartz2026-zb]] extends the diagnosis into political economy, arguing that scam logics—not sociality—now organize platform business models and governance. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates this same arc through the specific case of Twitter/X, showing how a once-prosocial, flat network structure was progressively "enshittified" through mismanagement and weaponized disinformation, leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to inherit its unsolved governance problems. Read together, these essays suggest the field is at an inflection point where the founding vocabulary of networked publics and participatory culture may no longer fit its object.

## Roots: networked publics, context collapse, and the platform trope

That founding vocabulary is itself represented here, giving the newer essays a baseline to argue against. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] (boyd) and [[Slack1783508042-sl69]] theorize social network sites as "networked publics" defined by affordances like persistence, replicability, scalability, and searchability, which in turn produce dynamics of invisible audiences and collapsed contexts. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this for Twitter, showing how users manage "context collapse" by imagining audiences and performing strategic, branded authenticity—a concept whose afterlife is visible throughout the corpus, from micro-influencer self-branding to AI-slop production. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the companion critique of infrastructure: the term "platform" itself is shown to be a discursive technology that lets intermediaries claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions, a tension that resurfaces directly in [[De2026-ld]], which documents how Meta, YouTube, X, and TikTok discursively manage—rather than conceal—their own policy and feature changes through "changecraft." [[Marwick2026-ss]] closes the loop autobiographically, using LiveJournal to recover what has been lost since this earlier theoretical moment: granular privacy controls, reciprocal long-form writing, and intimacy-supporting infrastructure that she argues was a political-economic choice now largely foreclosed.

## TikTok and the platform-specific production of culture

TikTok functions almost as a shared laboratory across many empirical papers, each testing Gerbaudo's clustered-publics thesis against a different cultural domain. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds that Canadian immigration discourse on TikTok skews more positive than prior Twitter-based research would predict, while showing that pro- and anti-immigration creators deploy the platform's affordances—sound, hashtags, humor—in symmetrical ways. [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents "ConspiracyTok" as a genre of "populist knowledge production" enacted by young, disproportionately non-white and female creators who build intimacy and evidentiary authority through platform-specific visual vernaculars. [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates sound itself as an under-examined vector of computational propaganda around the Ukraine invasion, while [[Nangle2026-yo]] shows far-right Instagram Reels using positive affect and memetic humor as "multimodal softening" that normalizes extremism through algorithmic proximity. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] localizes these dynamics in the Philippines, where "dogshow" humor among Gen Z influencers blurs entertainment and cyberbullying, and [[Tang2026-gu]] extends the frame to China, where AI-slop creators reverse-engineer virality itself into a managed industrial process, unsettling what counts as a "creator." Across these cases, platform affordances are never neutral scaffolding but actively co-constitute the extremism, humor, or epistemics that circulate.

## Creator labor, influence, and the toxic economies of visibility

A related cluster examines the labor and affective costs borne by those producing content within these systems. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers using second-hand fashion content as a low-cost survival strategy against algorithmic production demands, in which "care" and "self-branding" are inseparable rather than opposed. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] complicates influencer visibility from the audience side, demonstrating that misinformation attributed to influencers generates more frequent and qualitatively distinct toxicity than identical content from ordinary users, because credibility and parasocial bonds convert into collective antagonism. Both papers extend Marwick's earlier theorization of micro-celebrity and imagined audience into a harsher contemporary register, where visibility labor is simultaneously a survival tactic and a liability.

## Polarization, boundary work, and the far right across platforms

The largest thematic cluster interrogates how platform architecture and audience practice jointly produce ideological sorting and extremist mobilization. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] challenges the "town square" model of Reddit, finding minimal cross-ideological overlap even among discursively similar subreddits and arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than reducible to any one factor. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes back against purely platform-driven echo-chamber accounts, showing that "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective storytelling—reclaiming stigma, sharing hardship—consistently across six platforms regardless of architecture. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] approach polarization more theoretically, the former asking whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization and the latter proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as normative correctives. Several papers empirically map far-right networked publics: [[Kakavand2026-kt]] shows German far-right actors adapting distinct strategies to the connectivity, replicability, and scalability affordances of five different platforms rather than reproducing one network; [[Groebner2026-pc]] finds a genuinely transnational far-right YouTube audience anchored by US hyperpartisan channels; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric platform-by-platform, prioritizing TikTok memes to reach younger voters; and [[Balluff2026-ev]] and [[Zhu2026-tn]] examine alternative media's relational position vis-à-vis legacy outlets, finding surprising overlap in named entities but distinct network topologies, and audience overlap patterns shaped by national media systems and populist parties' institutional access. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] rounds out this cluster by showing how Facebook's Reactions feature itself operationalizes and amplifies affective polarization, with anger disproportionately rewarded in political content.

## Methods for studying multi-platform, temporal, and non-human sociality

A final set of papers is more methodological, developing tools adequate to these platform environments. [[Smith2025-kc]] identifies "attention brokers" as a causal mechanism generating triad transitivity in follower networks, extending brokerage theory into many-to-many amplification systems. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes "practice mapping" via vector embeddings to overcome the "hairball" problem in multimodal network visualization, while [[Fan2026-af]] mines timestamped cross-platform trace data to recover the temporal, sequential structure of platform-switching behavior that cross-sectional methods erase. [[Graham2026-fb]] uses Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon to destabilize the human/bot binary itself, arguing authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical fact—an argument that resonates with [[Tang2026-gu]]'s AI-slop creators and with [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s integrative review of the fragmented "algorithm literacy" literature. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] contributes a practical methodological innovation for reaching niche populations via Facebook ad targeting, while [[Wang2026-ub]] empirically tests platform migration itself, showing that even a coordinated academic community failed to sustain its move from Twitter to Mastodon, with retention hinging on cross-server diversity and discipline-specific communities rather than internal cohesion.

## Intimacy, informativeness, and the future of public deliberation

Two studies recover the intimate, communal register that the opening essays lament losing. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] shows Italian Facebook users constructing "networked intimacy" through collaborative, semi-public disclosure practices—tagging, implicit sharing, liking—that sustain community-like friendship despite (or through) publicness. [[Wedel2026-ix]] shifts the analytic lens from institutions to audiences, showing how young adults construct their own criteria of "informativeness" across YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok that extend journalistic norms to influencers and platform-specific selection logics. Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] looks forward rather than back, surveying how large language models might reconstruct digital public squares through collective dialogue systems, bridging algorithms, and community moderation—an explicitly normative counterpoint to the entropic accounts of platform decline that open this collection, suggesting that the same infrastructural forces diagnosed as corrosive elsewhere in this topic might yet be redirected toward more deliberative and inclusive ends.
