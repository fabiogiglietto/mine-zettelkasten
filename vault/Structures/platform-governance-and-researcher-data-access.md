# Platform Governance and Researcher Data Access

## The arc: from open data to governed access

The papers filed here trace a single institutional story told from many angles: the collapse of the informal, API-based data ecosystem that enabled the first generation of social media research, and the uncertain emergence of a regulated successor regime centered on the EU's Digital Services Act. [[Freelon2018-ao]] supplies the founding diagnosis—Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure inaugurated a "post-API age" in which platform-specific methods are permanently fragile—and [[Bruns2019-nr]] names the precipitating event, the "APIcalypse," arguing that post-Cambridge-Analytica restrictions functioned less as privacy protection than as a means of frustrating critical scrutiny. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extend this periodization across platforms and years, both converging on a shared structural claim: voluntary, platform-led access (variously "data philanthropy," "independence by permission," or discretionary API tiers) is inherently reversible, contingent on corporate mood, scandal, and leadership turnover rather than durable norms. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] document the scholarly cost of this collapse specifically for Twitter/X, with the latter offering bibliometric evidence—a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024—that operationalizes what the others describe qualitatively. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review generalizes the finding across social science disciplines: empirical social-media research tripled from 2010 but has plateaued and declined since 2022, with researchers only beginning to migrate toward alternative platforms and donation-based methods.

## Measuring the damage: dataset artifacts and epistemic bias

A cluster of papers moves from documenting *that* access has narrowed to showing *how* even the data that remains is structurally distorted. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] both interrogate Meta's URL Shares Dataset's 100-public-shares threshold, the former showing it inflates fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to a representative panel, the latter showing it produces suspiciously uniform cross-country trends that likely reflect feed-ranking changes (MSI optimization) rather than genuine local dynamics—together a cautionary methodological pairing on how anonymization-by-threshold can masquerade as signal. [[Holt2026-zq]] extends use of this same dataset to a substantive question (what users flag as "false news"), finding that flagged content is overwhelmingly political-disagreement rather than falsehood—newly urgent given Meta's 2025 pivot to Community Notes, a shift also interrogated in [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], which finds post-verification removal is rare and empirically undercuts Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing. [[McNally2025-dn]] pushes back against the "black box" metaphor itself, showing that Facebook's News Feed algorithmic interventions leave detectable, laggard fingerprints in engagement data—an argument for systemic auditability that anticipates DSA Article 40(4). [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] applies Meta Content Library data to detect an undisclosed political-content-suppression policy's real rollout timeline, explicitly framing this as evidence both of transparency deficits and of DSA infrastructure's research value. [[Rogers2026-zz]] historicizes this whole trajectory of narrowing access and shifting terminology ("fake news" → "misinformation" → "borderline"), showing how Meta's own redefinitions have tracked, and perhaps enabled, its retreat from independent scrutiny.

## Auditing the replacement infrastructure

As platforms stood up DSA-mandated or DSA-adjacent tools, a wave of audits emerged testing whether these mechanisms deliver on their transparency promise. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API persistently limited and inconsistent; [[Rieder2025-ju]] subjects YouTube's search API to a six-month audit and finds it "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and irreproducibility that compromise DSA systemic-risk research; [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content moderation and labeling practices around the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application of publisher-context labels and opaque removal communications, and calls for expanded researcher programs. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency reports themselves to expose linguistic disparities in human moderation staffing, showing Global South languages are systematically under-resourced relative to English. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive alternative—random-sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) built to circumvent platforms' refusal to provide representative samples. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] turn reflexively to the *experience* of researchers navigating these infrastructures: CrowdTangle's API shaped what could be seen and known ("seeing like an API"), while its successor, the Meta Content Library's "clean room" model, imposes new constraints—web disconnection, mandatory data deletion, code-only analysis—that systematically disadvantage qualitative, longitudinal, and Global South researchers. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] generalize these infrastructural frustrations to election research specifically, the latter formalizing a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework for who can actually use the 72 access tools and programs they catalog.

## The DSA as regulatory experiment: promise, resistance, contestation

At the center of the topic sits the DSA's Article 40 regime itself, treated across several papers as a live regulatory experiment rather than settled infrastructure. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] together offer a layered assessment: access mechanisms exist and matter, but legal, technical, and organizational friction—narrow eligibility interpretations, bureaucratic delay, platform resistance—persists even after the 2025 Delegated Act. De Vreese and Tromble's contribution is notably counter-despairing, arguing that platform pushback is evidence the regulation is finally biting rather than proof of failure, and urging a "marathon" posture of sustained implementation rather than retreat. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Annabell2026-ev]] dig into the conceptual substrate of this contestation, showing that core terms—"data quality," "data" itself—are not neutral technical categories but "floating signifiers" actively negotiated by platforms (who favor narrow, aggregated, pre-structured definitions), academics, and NGOs during EU consultation processes, with platforms like Meta and Amazon pushing definitions that minimize their obligations. [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out further, situating the DSA comparatively alongside US state legislation and German/Austrian law as one of three distinct "circulation regimes" (free circulation, patrolling, flow optimization) converging on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation"—transparency and auditing as modes of governing platforms at a distance rather than direct content control. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA's data-access regime should prompt a conceptual shift in political communication research itself, away from individual causal effects toward platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure.

## Industry influence and the political economy of dependence

A final, increasingly urgent strand interrogates *why* independent access matters so much: because the alternative is structural dependence on industry. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document this most sharply, the latter empirically showing that roughly half of high-profile platform research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring set of scientists who also populate editorial and review pipelines, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as structurally worse than pharma or tobacco precedents, since platforms uniquely control the only data that can study them, and explicitly proposes DSA-style mandated access as the remedy. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a skeptical coda from inside one of the most prominent collaborations (Meta2020), arguing that even well-resourced platform partnerships cannot generate durable knowledge because platforms change faster than science can validate—an argument for *regulatory* rather than *collaborative* solutions to platform self-knowledge. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds methodologically, highlighting platform-independent experimental designs (browser extensions, LLM-based reranking) as a pragmatic hedge against both API closures and the rarity of platform cooperation. Together with [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]], which supply the foundational theoretical vocabulary—platforms as rhetorically self-legitimating intermediaries, and as sites where private and public values collide—this cluster frames the entire topic's throughline: researcher data access is not a technical afterthought but the central battleground on which platform accountability, and the possibility of independent knowledge about platforms, will be decided.
