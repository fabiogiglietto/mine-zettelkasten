# Generative AI & Synthetic Media

## The persuasion frontier: how good is conversational AI at changing minds?

A tight cluster of large-scale field and survey experiments forms the empirical backbone of this topic, tracing an escalating claim about AI's persuasive capacity. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] establishes the baseline mechanics: across 19 LLMs and tens of thousands of conversations, persuasiveness is driven less by model scale than by post-training and prompting choices, with *information density*—the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims—doing most of the work, at the cost of a measurable accuracy penalty. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this further, pitting AI against elite human persuaders (professional canvassers, championship debaters) and finding that AI wins even against coached experts, with the advantage collapsing only once AI is throttled to human writing speed—confirming throughput, not charisma, as the operative mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends the paradigm from lab attitudes to real elections across three countries, showing AI dialogue outperforms traditional political ads and works through facts and evidence rather than manipulation, while noting a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes the effect across issues (climate, vaccines, inequality) and finds it strongest among skeptics, complicating assumptions that AI persuasion mainly reaches the already-convinced. Read together, these papers substantiate the "current-agenda" worry about AI-driven persuasion with hard numbers, but also domesticate it: the mechanism is mundane (more facts, faster), not mysterious manipulation—a nuance [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] operationalizes for governance, showing that disclosing AI *identity* (as under the EU AI Act) barely dents persuasion, whereas disclosing *intent* cuts it in half, reframing the regulatory target from "is it AI" to "what is it trying to do."

## Visual and multimodal disinformation: from gambling ads to deepfaked confessions

A second strand asks whether generative AI's visual and audiovisual capacities change disinformation's persuasive calculus, with mixed and context-dependent answers. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] is the topic's paradigm case: coordinated Facebook gambling networks show a 13,000%+ volume surge after ChatGPT's launch, with generative AI intensifying—rather than replacing—established persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, cultural localization) through hyper-real synthetic aesthetics that exploit the paid/organic regulatory grey zone. This industrial-scale, amplification-driven account contrasts instructively with [[Hameleers2026-mc]], which finds experimentally that AI-generated images do *not* universally outperform text or low-tech video decontextualization—visual disinformation's edge is issue-bound, emerging only where authentic footage already lends plausibility (as with the Ukraine war), suggesting the gambling case's virality owes as much to coordination and volume as to any modality-specific persuasive superiority. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a cognitive layer, showing that difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over, modality-congruently, into confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fakes—implying repeated exposure reshapes discernment capacity itself. [[Wack2026-bt]], working in Kenya, shows deepfake impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated: audience comments and partisanship determine whether a fabricated confession damages or is dismissed, framing deepfakes as "flexibly deceptive cues" within participatory disinformation ecosystems rather than standalone artifacts—a finding with particular force for under-moderated, low-resource platforms exactly like those hosting the gambling networks in [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]].

## Coordinated and emergent manipulation: from swarms to industrialized slop

Beyond individual persuasive artifacts, several papers theorize manipulation as an emergent property of networked, agentic, or industrialized production. [[Orlando2025-ul]] offers the clearest mechanistic demonstration: simulated LLM agents given only mutual awareness—not explicit coordination—spontaneously produce network cohesion, narrative convergence, and synchronized amplification nearly matching deliberate collective coordination, empirically grounding the more speculative warnings in [[Schroeder2026-im]] about "malicious AI swarms" fabricating consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and eroding institutional trust at a scale beyond prior botnets. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] supplies a real-world instance of this industrialization, as does [[Tang2026-gu]], which documents Chinese "AI slop" creators who transform virality itself into a managed production pipeline via benchmarking and matrix account systems—turning the diffuse coordination logic of [[Orlando2025-ul]] into concrete creative labor practice. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates the swarm/industrialization narratives by insisting on structural variation: comparing six democratically weakened states, it argues GenAI propaganda's form and effect are contingent on local political-economic conditions rather than following one universal trajectory, a useful corrective to the more totalizing claims elsewhere in this cluster.

## Epistemic infrastructure: trust, facts, and knowledge platforms under strain

A more theoretical current asks what generative AI does to the underlying conditions of knowledge and trust rather than to any single persuasive episode. [[Emilio2026-ik]] is the most ambitious synthesis, proposing "synthetic realities" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and naming the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content proliferates, rational actors may discount *all* digital evidence, taxing truth itself. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] arrives at a cognate diagnosis from fact-checking epistemology, arguing that GenAI outputs fit none of the field's traditional fact categories and require a new one—"emergent facts"—evaluated relationally rather than as true/false. [[Suk2026-ai]] situates this epistemic anxiety within the longer arc of media trust scholarship, proposing a multilevel framework linking trust in generative AI to earlier trajectories of trust in mass and social media. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical audit of Grokipedia versus Wikipedia gives these abstractions teeth: a large-scale AI-generated encyclopedia proves derivative yet ideologically inflected, citing more unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics—a concrete case of institutional-layer erosion as theorized by Emilio and Dierickx. Audience-facing studies temper the alarm somewhat: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news quality and even engage more after disclosure, while [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows trust penalties for AI involvement are task-specific (harshest for fact-checking) and segmented across distinct reader profiles—suggesting the erosion-of-trust thesis needs finer-grained, task- and audience-level specification than the systemic accounts provide.

## Governance, countermeasures, and public-interest safeguards

Running alongside the diagnostic literature is a policy-oriented strand asking how institutions might respond. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] maps the deepfake financial-fraud "scam ecosystem" and argues liability should shift from victims to gatekeepers—platforms, telecoms, banks—echoing [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]]'s call to regulate algorithmic amplification infrastructure rather than only individual posts. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] translates this gatekeeper logic to conversational AI specifically, showing that meaningful transparency must disclose persuasive intent, not mere AI identity. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] offers a rare positive-countermeasure result at platform scale: a 19-second Instagram prebunking ad durably improves users' ability to spot emotional manipulation months later, demonstrating that inoculation—like manipulation—can be industrialized and deployed cheaply in feeds. [[Unknown2025-qj]] rounds out the governance cluster by examining red-teaming as a public-interest, sociotechnical practice for surfacing generative-AI harms before deployment, complementing the after-the-fact detection and disclosure mechanisms discussed elsewhere.

## Generative AI as medium and culture

A final, more reflective set of papers steps back from harm-and-countermeasure framing to ask what kind of cultural object generative AI actually is. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately brackets ethics and manipulation to analyze GenAI as a creative medium with its own logic—probabilistic, aesthetically conservative, "a medium that thinks"—offering a useful counterweight to the topic's dominant harms framing by asking what the medium enables rather than what it threatens. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Tang2026-gu]] pick up this cultural register from the opposite end, examining "AI slop" and "brainrot" as an ambivalent gimmick-aesthetic (per Ngai) that is simultaneously deskilled and overworked, wasteful and industrially managed—bridging Manovich's medium-theoretic curiosity with the coordinated, engineered virality documented in the gambling-promotion and swarm-coordination literatures above. Together, these pieces suggest that the pedagogical anchor of this topic—the new "IA Generativa e Media" course—must reckon not only with disinformation and persuasion as discrete threats but with generative AI's emergence as a full-fledged medium and cultural form whose manipulative and creative affordances are, per Manovich and Giglietto alike, two faces of the same underlying probabilistic recombination engine.
