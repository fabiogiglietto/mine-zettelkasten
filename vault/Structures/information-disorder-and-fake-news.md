# Information Disorder and Fake News

## The Shape of the Field: From "Fake News" to Information Disorder

The corpus traces a conceptual migration away from "fake news" as a discrete, binary category toward "information disorder" as a systemic, relational phenomenon. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] crystallizes this shift in its notion of "Deceptive Information Operations," arguing that misinformation need not be false at all — what matters is orchestrated, affordance-exploiting, coordinated participation. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates this move theoretically, using second-order cybernetics to relocate analytical attention from the creator's intent to the propagator's judgment, arguing that "fake news" cascades are composite, emergent, and irreducible to a single typology. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] chart the institutional history of this reframing, with Bennett and Livingston's "disinformation order" positioning strategic disinformation as a symptom of democratic legitimacy crisis rather than a technological aberration — a claim empirically substantiated at scale by [[Benkler2018-lw]], whose "propaganda feedback loop" locates the crisis asymmetrically in a radicalized right-wing media ecosystem rather than in Russian interference, Facebook's algorithm, or technology per se. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] pushes this systemic view furthest, arguing that even sophisticated single analogies — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each illuminate only one level of a multilevel complex adaptive system, and that effective policy requires portfolios of interventions rather than silver bullets, a conclusion echoed in the large-scale expert Delphi study of [[Mahl2026-hc]] and in [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s call to de-Westernize the field's WEIRD-centric assumptions.

## Measuring the Problem: Scale, Concentration, and the Ecosystem

A foundational empirical thread interrogates just how large the "fake news" problem actually is. [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Allen2020-nj]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Guess2020-rr]] collectively established a "minimalist" consensus: exposure to and sharing of fake news is heavily concentrated among a narrow, disproportionately older and more conservative slice of the public, and dwarfed by mainstream news and television consumption. [[Guess2021-ym]] extends this descriptive tradition to platform-scale data, showing low-credibility content is a small but non-trivial share of the Facebook ecosystem, skewed toward ideological congruence. Yet subsequent work complicates this reassurance. [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that link-based measures dramatically undercount misinformation because they ignore images — fully a fifth of political image posts on Facebook contain misinformation, heavily right-skewed. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] extend the platform-scale audit to diffusion trees and untrustworthy-source exposure respectively, the latter again finding concentration without measurable attitudinal effects. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] delivers perhaps the sharpest methodological corrective: the field's dominant "direct referrer paradigm" underestimates platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure by 1.5–15×, once indirect and counterfactual pathways are modeled — while also showing that banning platforms would disproportionately harm access to *good* information. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Luhring2025-od]] extend this measurement concern into ongoing monitoring (TikTok's persistently high mis/disinformation share) and methodological auditing (the instabilities of NewsGuard's binary trust classifications). [[Scalco2026-bd]] reframes the supply side entirely, operationalizing "information voids" — demand-supply imbalances — as a mechanistic precursor to misinformation uptake.

## Diffusion Dynamics: Virality, Cascades, and Coordination

A parallel strand examines *how* false content moves once released. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] establish the classic findings that falsehood travels farther, faster, and deeper than truth, and that echo-chamber homogeneity drives cascade size. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers a significant theoretical corrective, replacing the "echo chamber"/"filter bubble" metaphors with a feedback-loop framework that explains why reinforcing dynamics rarely produce runaway radicalization, given negative as well as positive feedback. Increasingly, however, the diffusion literature has shifted from spontaneous virality to orchestrated coordination. [[Starbird2019-qv]] reframes disinformation as *collaborative work* — participatory, sociotechnical, and stubbornly resistant to the bot/troll binary — a framing that [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] grounds empirically in its labor-market analysis of the IRA "troll factory," and that [[Mannocci2026-kc]] formalizes into a general taxonomy of coordinated online behavior spanning authenticity and harm as orthogonal dimensions. Detection methodologies proliferate accordingly: [[Slack1783507719-slc1]]'s CooRnet operationalizes the Actors-Behaviors-Content cycle for coordinated link-sharing, while [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] pushes toward real-time bot detection on emergent platforms like Bluesky, and [[Rogers2026-cy]] maps "post-truth spaces" for fact-checkers via network proximity to the mainstream.

## Narrative Turn: Beyond Discrete Claims

Perhaps the most consequential conceptual development across this literature is the "narrative turn" — a rejection of atomistic, true/false claim-checking in favor of understanding misinformation as story. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is foundational here, arguing that successful disinformation possesses characters, settings, plots, and catchphrases, and spreads because it is identity-affirming and cathartic rather than merely false. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical apparatus for this shift via hermeneutic realism and narrative ethics, showing content can be referentially and ethically compromised without being straightforwardly false. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this at the level of platform discourse, developing "deep story" and "evidence-frame" frameworks to show how election-fraud rumors are less about false facts than about collaborative framing performed across quote-tweets and comments. [[Waight2025-al]] provides a methodological breakthrough for this agenda, using LLM-based pipelines to measure "narrative similarity" — distinct from lexical or topical similarity — revealing that mainstream U.S. outlets shared narrative structure with Russian state media on the Ukraine "biolabs" story far more than exact-text-reuse methods could detect. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the insight in a different direction, showing that mainstream news content is itself routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives, evading source-level classification entirely. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] (PROMPT/Maldita's applied narrative-monitoring and argument-checking dashboards) translate this narrative ontology directly into countermeasure design, arguing that claim-level fact-checking cannot disrupt narrative momentum during crises like the Valencia floods.

## Platform Ethnographies: TikTok, Instagram, Telegram

A cluster of studies examines platform-specific vernaculars of misinformation and radical content. [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents "ConspiracyTok's" populist knowledge production and "generous epistemology," complicating stereotypes of who produces conspiracy content. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Gardam2025-er]] extend multimodal analysis to Instagram's affective-discursive far-right ecology and visual-first climate denial respectively. [[Bosch2024-hj]] isolates sound as an under-examined TikTok-specific propaganda vector in Ukraine war disinformation, while [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds TikTok's Canadian immigration discourse skews surprisingly pro-immigration, complicating assumptions drawn from X/Twitter research. Rothut2026-tz and [[Rothut2026-wt]] use Telegram data to trace how the Querdenken protest movement mainstreamed far-right adjacency through "protest-facilitated mainstreaming," complementing a 25-year scoping review of online radicalization research. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] cuts across six platforms to show that conspiracy culture (the "Great Reset") is sustained less by algorithmic architecture than by symbolic boundary work and reclaimed stigma.

## Political Elites, Parties, and Strategic Weaponization

Several papers relocate agency from algorithms and audiences to political elites. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing by parliamentarians — only their *combination*, radical-right populism, does. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines institutional and ideological checks on elected officials' sharing behavior, while [[Renault2025-uh]] shows Community Notes still flags Republicans 2.3× more than Democrats, undermining claims that crowdsourced moderation eliminates partisan asymmetry. [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election as an intensification (not rupture) of PR spin. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] both examine the *rhetorical weaponization* of the "fake news" label itself as a delegitimization tool rather than a description of falsity — Yoo's Hallin's-spheres analysis of Newsmax and Occupy Democrats showing the term functions almost entirely as an empty partisan cudgel. [[Makeev2026-ma]] extends the elite-strategy lens to authoritarian wartime propaganda's dual domestic/foreign objectives, and [[Copland2025-em]], [[Poliakoff2026-fa]], and [[Graham2025-gp]] examine specific propaganda infrastructures (Sky News Australia, the IRA, #IStandWithPutin) as network-embedded operations exploiting rather than evading the infrastructure of legitimate discourse.

## Effects on Belief, Trust, and Democratic Attitudes

A substantial cluster asks what misinformation *does* to citizens once encountered. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] remains foundational, documenting the "continued influence effect" and identifying which correction techniques actually work. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] reframes susceptibility as a function of inattention and poor reasoning rather than partisan motivation, a claim extended by [[Osmundsen2021-et]], who finds partisan animus (not ignorance) drives sharing specifically. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows unsubstantiated voter-fraud claims erode confidence in elections even when fact-checked, concentrated among sympathetic partisans. This electoral-integrity thread continues in [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], which use Brazilian panel data to show misinformation belief drives both political intolerance and eroded electoral trust, with messaging apps as a key indirect vector — a finding paralleled by [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation experiment. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect," extending the hostile media effect to perceptions of who is targeted by misinformation. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] both interrogate the meta-level consequences of *discourse about* interference and platform accountability, suggesting alarmist narratives can themselves inflict democratic harm. [[Adam2026-tz]] models the joint contagion/mitigation dynamics of alternative versus mainstream media exposure on COVID conspiracy belief, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] connects far-right electoral performance to voters' disinformation concerns.

## Interventions: Prebunking, Debunking, and AI-Assisted Correction

The corpus documents an active experimental literature on countermeasures. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates prebunking can scale cheaply via Instagram ads with durable effects; [[Costello2024-bg]] shows personalized LLM dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief by ~20%, challenging motivational-immunity accounts; [[Szabo2026-rd]] extends this into "Conversational Inoculation" via chatbot interaction. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] takes a structural rather than individual-level approach, showing that blocking political elites from feeds—not correcting individual beliefs—reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing the problem as one of exposure architecture. [[Lieu2025-nl]] and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] extend intervention science to climate misinformation specifically, the latter importing hard-won lessons about behavioral measurement and vested-interest resistance from climate psychology into misinformation research broadly. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] examines the underexamined middle ground between removal and inaction—algorithmic "reduction"—as itself a governance technology requiring scrutiny.

## Institutional Response: Fact-Checking Under Pressure

A final cluster examines the fact-checking institution itself amid platform retrenchment. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] document fact-checkers' precarious rhetorical and material position vis-à-vis platform patrons, especially after Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking; [[Holt2026-zq]] shows user-flagged "false news" reports skew toward polarized disagreement rather than demonstrable falsity, questioning the "wisdom of crowds" rationale for Community Notes-style systems. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Dierickx2026-tw]] turn to generative AI's implications for fact-checking, the former showing LLMs require curated retrieval context to perform adequately, the latter proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category for AI outputs. [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Choi2026-bz]], [[Wack2026-bt]], [[Hameleers2026-mc]], and [[Triedman2025-uy]] collectively interrogate the generative-AI inflection point: synthetic realities, modality-congruent carryover effects on truth discernment, socially-negotiated deepfake interpretation in Kenya, and the finding that AI-generated "encyclopedias" like Grokipedia reproduce ideological bias in sourcing despite being textually derivative of Wikipedia. Across this thread runs a shared caution — voiced explicitly in [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s "Generative AI Paradox" — that technical fixes (watermarking, detection, verification) cannot resolve what is ultimately an institutional and epistemic crisis of trust, returning the field full circle to the systemic diagnosis with which it began.
