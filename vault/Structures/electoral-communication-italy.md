# Italian Electoral Communication

## The Italian core: three elections, one hybrid media system

The programme's founding observations are Italian and longitudinal. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establishes the baseline logic a decade before the rest of the corpus: during the 2013 campaign, Twitter functioned as a second screen bolted onto television's agenda, with audience participation narrow and largely imitative of broadcast logic rather than disruptive of it. This hybrid-media starting point is picked up and radicalised by [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which turns to the 2018 general election and shows that populist parties — especially the Five Star Movement — cultivate genuinely insular news ecosystems on Twitter while simultaneously mobilising supporters on Facebook to amplify favourable stories and contest unfavourable ones, a dual strategy the paper reads as evidence of a "cyber party" model of digital-native partisan organisation. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] extends the same 2018–2019 window to ask not just *who* is insular but *how* coordination is manufactured, introducing coordinated link sharing behavior as a unit of analysis and showing that networks of pages, groups and profiles sharing URLs near-simultaneously are disproportionately linked to problematic domains. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] carries this lineage into the 2022 snap election, upgrading the method into a self-updating workflow that surfaces new coordinated actors in near-real time and documents three qualitatively distinct operations — a hyperpartisan M5S network, a clickbait economy parasitic on religious pages, and a proselytising network exploiting Messenger bots — demonstrating that coordination is a structural feature of the Italian information environment that persists and mutates across cycles rather than a one-off phenomenon tied to 2018. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] closes this founding arc by turning the lens onto the platform itself: using the Meta Content Library, it shows that Meta's political-content-reduction policy quietly suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach starting nearly a year before its announced global rollout, while extremist accounts offset the loss through sheer posting volume — a finding that reframes the earlier insularity and coordination results as partly a function of algorithmic infrastructure rather than purely organic partisan behaviour.

## Methodological throughline: from counting shares to reading meaning

A second axis running through the Italian work concerns how to analyse the enormous corpora these elections generate. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] form a matched pair applied to the same 2018/2022 Facebook datasets: the former benchmarks embedding models for unsupervised clustering of Italian political news, finding that general-purpose LLM embeddings (text-embedding-3-large) outperform Italian-tuned BERT variants, while the latter builds and validates a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline — classification, clustering, labelling — explicitly grappling with the problem that LLM outputs resist the validation protocols built for earlier transformer-based tools. [[Paci2025-ag]] pushes this agenda toward pragmatics, testing whether LLMs can recover the implicatures and presuppositions embedded in Italian political speeches (via the IMPAQTS corpus) and finding a persistent gap between model and expert interpretation — a sobering check on how much interpretive weight the clustering/labelling pipelines above can actually bear. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] generalises the underlying concern methodologically, arguing that news-sharing research (including, implicitly, the Italian insularity work) has relied on unidimensional measures of political leaning and outlet-level aggregation, and proposing a multi-level "political cartography" of story, outlet, and content as a corrective. [[Mosca2026-yh]] closes this strand by reorienting the object of analysis itself: rather than treating "fake news" as a misinformation category to be detected, it proposes a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model that reads fake-news accusations in Italian newspapers as rhetorical weapons in negative campaigning — a reminder that the insularity and coordination patterns documented elsewhere are entangled with elite rhetorical strategy, not just algorithmic amplification.

## Platform governance and the DSA turn

As the empirical base matured, the programme increasingly had to reckon with the infrastructure that makes (or prevents) this research possible. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] already models this move by treating Meta's Content Library as both object and enabling condition of study. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys the wider terrain, cataloguing how the EU Digital Services Act has forced platforms to reopen "public" data access through APIs and portals, unevenly and incompletely. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] supplies the longue-durée policy backdrop, tracing election-related platform governance from near-total absence before 2018 through a "civic integrity" expansion (2020–2022) to a recent phase of rollback, a trajectory that frames the Italian Meta-policy findings as a local instance of a global pattern of "platform transience." [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Oprea2025-lf]] translate these governance constraints into concrete fieldcraft problems: the former, working on the 2025 German election, argues that multi-platform electoral research is fundamentally an infrastructure problem — parties strategically differentiate across platforms, and TikTok's textual/vocal/facial channels barely correlate — while the latter shows, in the 2024 Romanian EP campaign, that restrictive API access lets hyperactive and plausibly inauthentic accounts amplify party content largely undetected. Together these papers make the case that the Italian findings on coordination and visibility are themselves artefacts of a shifting, imperfectly observable data regime, and that method and governance can no longer be treated as separable from substantive findings.

## Comparative widening: does the Italian pattern travel?

The remaining papers test, extend, or complicate the Italian findings by moving outward in space and platform. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] directly triangulates Italy against France and Germany, confirming that untrustworthy-URL sharing on Facebook spikes in Italian and German election years (reaching 22% in Italy in 2022) while remaining stable in France — a cross-national validation of the problematic-coordination dynamics first mapped by [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]]. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] take the coordination and strategic-falsehood concepts to the UK, with the former refining coordination detection into a continuous (not binary) measure during the 2019 General Election and the latter theorising "strategic lying" around Brexit as an intensification, rather than rupture, of established spin tactics — both resonating with the Italian insight that falsehood and coordination are strategic resources rather than pathological noise. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extend the populist-amplification thesis of [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] into Scandinavia and Sweden respectively, showing that populist parties systematically harvest "Angry" reactions and shares on Facebook, and that the Sweden Democrats tailor securitising visual rhetoric — argumentative on Facebook/X, memetic on TikTok — to different platform publics. [[Darius2026-xl]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] scale this logic to the full 2024 European Parliament election: the former finds populist radical right parties receive disproportionate audience "likes" (not just posting volume) across platforms, especially TikTok, while the latter shows this translates into disproportionate *media* visibility for the Radical Right across five countries' news outlets — jointly suggesting that the insularity/amplification dynamics first observed in the Italian M5S case generalise into a broader European pattern linking platform engagement and journalistic salience. Finally, [[Rodarte2026-dk]] pushes the comparative frame furthest afield, to Brazilian parliamentarians' pursuit of epistemic authority during a health crisis — formally outside electoral campaigning but conceptually continuous with the programme's central question of how political actors contest and appropriate journalistic gatekeeping once social platforms let them become media producers in their own right.

Read as a whole, the structure moves from a tightly Italian, Facebook/Twitter-centred investigation of political parallelism and coordinated manipulation (2018–2022) toward a methodologically reflexive and increasingly comparative research programme — one that treats Italy as the originating case against which platform governance regimes, LLM-based methods, and populist communication strategies elsewhere in Europe and beyond are continually tested.
