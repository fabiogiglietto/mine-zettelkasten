# MINE — Mapping Italian News Program

## The shape of a research program

MINE is not a single study but the administrative and intellectual scaffold under which a decade of empirical work on Italian media has been organized since 2017. As a structure note it necessarily looks different from topic notes built around a substantive question: here the "argument" is less about a claim than about a *method of seeing* — a commitment to combining platform-level trace data (Twitter, Facebook, CrowdTangle-style engagement metrics) with attention to Italy's specific political-media configuration, in order to track how news, opinion, and health information circulate through the country's hybrid media system. So far only one paper is filed directly under this umbrella, but it is a representative one, and it is worth reading as a template for how the program tends to operate rather than as an isolated case.

## The exemplar: elections as a stress test for the program's methods

[[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows the MINE approach in miniature. It takes a bounded, high-stakes political moment — the 2018 Italian general election — and layers two distinct data sources (Twitter-derived measures of partisan "insularity" among news sources, and Facebook engagement metrics on tens of thousands of political stories) to ask a question that is simultaneously about media structure and about political behavior: how do different partisan publics *use* social media affordances (sharing vs. commenting) to amplify or contest news? This dual-platform, mixed-metric strategy — never relying on a single platform's data to characterize "the audience," always cross-referencing source-level and engagement-level signals — is characteristic of how the broader program tends to instrumentalize social media data as a lens on national political communication rather than treating any one platform as self-sufficient evidence.

The substantive finding — that populist parties, and the Five Star Movement in particular, exhibit a distinctive capacity to mobilize asymmetric sharing/commenting patterns that amplify favorable coverage and contest unfavorable coverage — also signals a recurring interest of the program: not just *whether* echo chambers or selective exposure exist, but how partisan communities strategically exploit the attention economy of specific platforms, and how this varies by party organization and ideology (the contrast between M5S's "cyber party" infrastructure and the more conventional digital presence of PD, Forza Italia, and the League). This attentiveness to intra-national variation, rather than treating "Italian social media users" as a monolithic bloc, is a hallmark that recurs across the program's later work on public opinion and health information as new sub-projects were added.

## Reading this note going forward

Because MINE functions as an umbrella rather than a bounded inquiry, this structure note will necessarily grow rather than resolve: as further sub-projects on elections, public opinion, and health communication are filed here, the task will be to trace how each extends, complicates, or departs from the insularity/amplification logic first worked out in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] — for instance, whether the same platform-specific asymmetries between sharing and commenting reappear in non-electoral contexts such as health misinformation, or whether they are a feature specific to the adversarial dynamics of campaign periods. For now, this single paper stands as the founding methodological gesture of the program: an insistence that studying "media coverage" in Italy means studying the interaction of multiple platforms' affordances with the country's polarized-pluralist party system, not measuring exposure or sentiment on any one platform in isolation.
