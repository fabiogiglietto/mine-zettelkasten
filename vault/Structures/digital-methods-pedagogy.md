# Digital Research Methods Teaching

## From Platform Access to Platform Precarity

The throughline connecting these four papers is less a single method than a shifting terrain of access that any methods curriculum must now teach around. Bastos2025-ya opens the arc retrospectively: Twitter's layered API ecosystem (REST, Search, Streaming, Academic, Compliance) did not just enable research, it *produced a subfield* — "Twitter studies" — by making large-scale social data unusually tractable for teaching and training purposes. That same infrastructure's disappearance after the 2023 rebrand to X is treated not as an isolated platform event but as a disciplinary rupture, closing off a pedagogical pathway that had trained a generation of computational social scientists on relatively accessible, well-documented data.

[[Freelon2018-ao]] names this rupture earlier and more generally, coining the "post-API age" in direct response to Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure. Its real contribution for a teaching-focused structure note is methodological-pedagogical: Freelon argues explicitly that graduate training built around any single platform's API is fragile by design, since access is a corporate grant that can be revoked overnight. His concrete recommendation — that web scraping, despite being harder to teach and more legally fraught, is a more durable skill than API fluency — reads as an early curricular response to exactly the kind of foreclosure Bastos2025-ya later documents for Twitter. Read together, these two papers bracket a five-year period in which "learn the API" stopped being viable advice and had to be replaced with "learn the harder, more general thing, and learn the law around it."

## Building Infrastructure to Teach With, Not Just Around

[[Zheng2026-bi]] picks up where Freelon's diagnosis leaves off, showing what a mature post-API research practice looks like once scraping and ad hoc workarounds are formalized into reusable infrastructure. TubeStats and TokStats are presented as public dashboards — teaching tools as much as research tools — that let students and practitioners query genuinely random samples of YouTube and TikTok content without needing to build scraping pipelines themselves. The paper's emphasis on "denominator" and "distribution" problems is itself a methods-training argument: it demonstrates, with concrete cases (Hindi YouTube's distinct video ecology, TikTok's geographic shift away from India, the skew revealed by percentile tools), why popularity-biased convenience samples mislead, and why representativeness has to be taught as a design problem rather than assumed. In this sense [[Zheng2026-bi]] operationalizes Freelon's call for methodological resilience, converting it into an actual platform that lowers the barrier to teaching rigorous, non-opportunistic social media sampling even as the underlying platforms grow more hostile to automated queries.

## AI as a New Object and Instrument of Methods Training

[[Ober2026-vd]] extends the arc from data-access infrastructure to analytic infrastructure, addressing a different but complementary training need: how to teach qualitative analysis in an era when LLMs are available as coding assistants. Its hybrid topic-modeling-plus-prompt-engineering framework is explicitly pitched as a human-in-the-loop alternative to both fully manual coding and opaque end-to-end LLM automation, with the explicit goal of preserving interpretive depth while gaining scalability. The empirical demonstration — refining a codebook on teacher perceptions of communication and digital literacy through iterative LLM-assisted labeling — doubles as a template that could itself be taught to students learning qualitative-quantitative hybrid methods, including concrete cautions (label instability across LLMs, the necessity of human oversight against hallucination) that any training module would need to surface.

## A Shared Pedagogical Lesson

Across the set, the papers converge on a single lesson worth foregrounding in a structure note on teaching digital methods: methodological literacy for students and practitioners now has to be built around *instability* — of platform access (Bastos2025-ya, [[Freelon2018-ao]]), of sampling validity ([[Zheng2026-bi]]), and of the reliability of AI tools themselves ([[Ober2026-vd]]). Where early API-era training could treat access and tooling as relatively stable givens, the current task is to teach adaptable competencies — scraping and sampling design, critical evaluation of representativeness, and disciplined human oversight of generative AI — rather than any single platform-specific skill. The four papers, read as a sequence, effectively document the curriculum's own evolution: from API-based convenience, through its collapse, to infrastructure-building and AI-augmented analysis as the new baseline for training the next cohort of computational social scientists.
