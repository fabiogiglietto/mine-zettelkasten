# Computational and LLM-Assisted Content Analysis

## The arc from automation to validation

The papers filed here trace a shift that has unfolded over roughly the last three years: from asking *can LLMs classify political text as well as humans or earlier supervised models* to asking *how do we know, and what breaks when we stop checking*. Early entries in this set are optimistic proofs of concept — fine-tuned and prompted LLMs applied to conflict events, policy positions, political sentiment, and image content — while later and more reflective pieces turn the same tools on themselves, probing bias, hallucination, pragmatic failure, and the sheer difficulty of validating a pipeline in which the model is not a single classifier but an interacting cascade of AI judgments. Read together, the collection is less a toolkit than an argument about what LLM-assisted content analysis can responsibly claim to measure.

## Classification and fine-tuning: open versus closed, scaled versus small

A cluster of papers directly compares fine-tuning against prompting and open-weight against proprietary models. [[Meher2025-qb]] shows that QLoRA-adapted Llama 3.1 dramatically outperforms its own zero-shot baseline on terrorism-event classification, with the largest gains precisely for rare categories that supervised pipelines historically struggle with. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] generalizes this finding across three social-science classification tasks, arguing that small, properly fine-tuned open models can match or beat GPT-4 while solving the reproducibility and cost problems that closed APIs create — and proposes a human-rejects-rather-than-assigns labeling workflow that exploits annotators' superior error detection over error generation. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] extend the comparison to non-English and cross-method settings: the former finds OpenAI's embeddings consistently beat an Italian BERT model for clustering political news, the latter validates GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification against human coders in Norwegian-language Facebook data, modeling LLM-assisted content analysis as a genuinely cross-linguistic methodology rather than an English-centric convenience. Together these papers converge on a pragmatic lesson: careful fine-tuning or embedding choice, not simply model scale, drives performance, and open-source alternatives are increasingly viable substitutes for proprietary tools — a point [[Balluff2026-if]] later turns into a normative argument for *preferring* smaller, open, task-appropriate models on environmental, reproducibility, and epistemic grounds.

## Embeddings, clustering, and the meaning they do or don't capture

A parallel strand interrogates what embeddings represent and how to make unsupervised grouping more faithful to social-scientific constructs. [[Fan2025-ut]] demonstrates that raw sentence embeddings encode confounds like source or language that distort similarity measures, and that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove this contamination cheaply and generally — a foundational methodological fix with implications for every embedding-based clustering exercise in this set. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] operationalize embedding choice and validation concretely in a shared Italian political-news pipeline, the latter going further to document the practical difficulty of validating an "LLMs-in-the-loop" system where fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated narrative labels are chained together — each stage needing its own tailored validation rather than a single omnibus check, and crowdsourced human benchmarks proving inadequate once LLMs begin to outperform low-skilled annotators. [[Arminio2025-tw]] pushes embedding-based clustering into the visual domain, showing that captioning images with a vision-LLM before embedding and clustering captures *connotative* meaning (cultural and symbolic content) that CNN pipelines miss, at only a small cost to denotative coherence, and that this produces human-interpretable clusters via TF-IDF summaries. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Waight2025-al]] extend unsupervised pattern discovery to framing and narrative diffusion respectively; the latter is particularly notable for explicitly separating narrative similarity as a distinct estimand from lexical, topical, or purely semantic similarity, and for showing empirically that cruder methods (exact text reuse, topic models, Relatio) systematically undercount real narrative diffusion — with consequential effects for substantive claims about how Russian "biolab" narratives spread into low-quality versus mainstream US outlets.

## Measuring latent positions, frames, and beliefs

Several papers use LLMs not to assign discrete labels but to recover continuous or latent constructs. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking an instruction-tuned LLM to place a short text on an ideological or policy dimension, and averaging many such judgments, reproduces established scaling benchmarks across languages and text types — an elegant alternative to Wordscores/Wordfish-style supervised or unsupervised scaling. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] applies a related logic to open-ended survey responses, pairing LLM judgments with paired-comparison methods to build continuous latent scales from free text that would otherwise require prohibitively expensive manual coding. [[Lee2026-je]] inverts the direction of inference, showing LLMs can recover users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse by exploiting subtle lexical and cultural cues, dramatically outperforming supervised baselines once predictions are aggregated across a user's post history — a capability the authors frame as both a methodological advance and a serious micro-targeting and privacy risk, echoing concerns raised more broadly in [[Balluff2026-if]]. On framing specifically, [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] both argue that automated frame analysis has been too narrow — the former reframing LLMs as "analytic collaborators" that surface ambiguities in deductive codebooks rather than as replacement coders, the latter extending frame extraction beyond text to visual and cross-modal cues in news coverage of gun violence. [[Elfes2026-jb]] adds a structuralist twist, using an LLM to operationalize Greimas's actantial model and showing that narrative polarization between partisan videos is substantially muted — though not eliminated at a deeper motif level — once one looks at the comment sections beneath them.

## Multimodal extensions

Visual and multimodal content analysis forms a connected sub-thread. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] benchmarks GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in campaign Instagram content, finding the multimodal LLM substantially more accurate and more robust to the kind of demographic edge case (the one female candidate) that biased a dedicated face-recognition model. [[Arminio2025-tw]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] round out this theme by showing that treating images as sources of generated *text* — captions, descriptions, frame labels — rather than as raw pixel input to CNNs is what unlocks both semantic depth and interpretability, a pattern that recurs across the visual-political-communication and framing literatures filed here.

## Validation, bias, and the limits of LLM judgment

A decisive portion of this topic is explicitly about what can go wrong, and how to know it has gone wrong. [[Brown2025-jk]] offers a reassuring but nuanced finding: systematic demographic bias in LLM annotation is modest and dataset-specific rather than a stable property of any one model, while item difficulty (human label entropy) is by far the dominant predictor of LLM-human disagreement — a call to build item difficulty into fairness audits rather than chasing model-switching as a fix. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] complicates optimism about LLM capability growth for fact-checking: neither reasoning-enabled models nor built-in web search meaningfully solve political fact-checking, and only curated retrieval (RAG) over vetted fact-check summaries yields reliable performance — with search-enabled models additionally showing a tilt toward citing left-leaning (if credible) sources. [[Paci2025-ag]] documents a related ceiling effect in a very different register, showing that even strong LLMs struggle to recover the implicatures and presuppositions embedded in manipulative Italian political speech, falling well short of expert human interpretation despite chain-of-thought prompting. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] generalizes this skepticism theoretically, arguing via Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis that chatbot "competence" is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding — a conceptual counterweight to the more instrumental validation papers in this set. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] remains the methodological anchor for this cluster, explicitly naming the validation problem that the others illustrate piecemeal: LLM-integrated pipelines are general-purpose, variably granular, and resistant to crowdsourced benchmarking, demanding bespoke, expert-driven, stage-specific validation protocols.

## Applications that stress-test the methods

A final group applies LLM-assisted or adjacent computational methods to substantive questions, each implicitly testing the broader methodology against real-world complexity. [[Ober2026-vd]] combines topic modeling, LLM-assisted labeling, and human codebook refinement for qualitative interview analysis, modeling a human-in-the-loop workflow for a domain (education research) outside typical political-communication use cases. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines emotionality in fact-checking posts, a substantive question that depends on reliable sentiment/emotion annotation at scale. [[Kim2026-br]] and [[Minici2024-tf]] both tackle coordinated inauthentic behavior — explainable hierarchical classification of suspected state-linked trolls in Korean news comments, and a graph-foundation-model approach to detecting information-operation drivers across six campaigns — demonstrating that LLM/embedding-based text signals are most powerful for this kind of detection when fused with network or behavioral features rather than used in isolation. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] offers a cautionary data point from an adjacent domain, finding that validated psychological constructs (dark personality traits) largely fail to show up in computationally extracted linguistic features, a reminder that textual signal does not automatically track the underlying theoretical construct researchers care about, closely paralleling [[Waight2025-al]]'s estimand/estimator argument. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the broader landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis methodologies, functioning as a map of the methodological territory this whole topic occupies. Finally, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] push the validation question up a level, asking whether AI agents can themselves verify computational social science — reproducing published findings and auditing scientific manuscripts — and finding real but conditional success (strong reproduction accuracy undermined by sycophantic prompt nudging; strong error detection alongside persistent hallucination risks), which closes the loop on the topic's central tension: LLMs are powerful annotators and analysts of political text, but every layer of automation — classification, embedding, clustering, labeling, even replication — requires its own skeptical, domain-expert validation before its outputs can be trusted as communication-research findings.
