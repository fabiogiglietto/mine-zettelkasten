# Elections and Political Communication

## From Content to Coordination: A Behavioural Turn

A first arc running through these papers is the move away from studying "misinformation" as false content toward studying the *behaviours* and *infrastructures* that produce and propagate it. The MINE line on coordinated link sharing behaviour [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] argued early that disinformation research needed an ecological, action-based reframing — an argument extended into a continuously updated detection workflow around the 2022 Italian election [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] and echoed in Brazilian WhatsApp coordination during 2022 [[Kansaon2025-id]], in Facebook inauthenticity during the 2024 Romanian EP elections [[Oprea2025-lf]], and in a graph-theoretic critique arguing that observed coordinated accounts fall far short of optimal influence and behave near-randomly [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]. The Meta-collaborative work on deceptive networks in the US 2020 elections [[Appel2026-qr]], [[noauthor_undated-bm]] complements this by insisting on integrating politically- and financially-motivated networks under one conceptual umbrella, while showing that reach depends on unaffiliated resharers rather than the networks themselves. Together this cluster reframes "fake news" as a problem of coordination and amplification architectures — a reframing pushed further by [[Mosca2026-yh]], which treats accusations of fake news as strategic rhetorical weapons rather than truth claims.

## Elites, Factions and Hyper-Partisan Ecosystems

A second arc concerns political elites as producers, not just sources, of partisan information. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians competing for epistemic authority during the Manaus crisis, while [[Knupfer2025-vt]] formalises a "logic of connective faction" in which digitally networked US Republican elites bind themselves to hyper-partisan outlets around triggers like CRT. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] provides the comparative anchor: across 26 countries, it is specifically radical-right populism — not populism or right-wing ideology alone — that predicts elite misinformation sharing. [[Tai2026-qk]] pursues the same question through institutional and ideological checks on US elected officials, and [[Larsson2026-ro]] traces a decade of negative sentiment under successive Norwegian Progress Party leaders. The Italian party-press hybrid appears in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which links populist success to insular news ecologies and shows M5S uniquely capable of orchestrating asymmetric share/comment patterns, and in the older TV-Twitter hybrid work [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]. Media capture appears as its structural cousin: the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre" [[Balluff2026-bv]] shows how covert government-advertising arrangements produce a "pay positive to go negative" bias detectable via difference-in-differences.

## Visibility, Salience and the Media Environment

A third strand focuses on how attention is distributed. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate Radical Right visibility across five countries in the 2024 EP elections, using LLM-based extraction to demonstrate a structural — not outlet-specific — bias. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] complements this qualitatively by showing how the Sweden Democrats tailored securitising visual discourse across Facebook, X and TikTok. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for concentrated-visibility analysis of German candidates on Instagram, while [[Schulte2026-df]] argues for treating cross-platform observability itself as a research infrastructure. Complementing salience with algorithmic gatekeeping, [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's promotion of "authoritative" sources during the 2024 EP elections, and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] shows that Meta's political-content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by 72%, while extremists compensated by posting more. Agenda-building dynamics also extend to non-human actors: [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] positions social bots as a fourth category of political communicator, most effective at transferring negative attribute salience.

## Effects, Persuasion and Epistemic Consequences

The effects literature in this file is unusually cautious. The pre-registered Meta collaboration papers [[Allcott2025-jb]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] found either null effects of ad removal or highly concentrated but limited misinformation diffusion. [[Munger2025-cz]] provides a meta-scientific critique of that whole project, arguing that platforms mutate too quickly for one-off field experiments to yield durable knowledge. Against this null-leaning backdrop, [[Kim2026-wg]] recovers substantial heterogeneous effects of Russian voter-suppression ads by measuring targeted subgroups directly, and [[Lin2025-xp]] shows that human–AI dialogues persuade voters across four elections primarily via facts rather than manipulation. On the belief side, [[Rossini2026-jn]] links electoral misinformation belief to declining political tolerance in Brazil, [[Ventura2025-sw]] uses WhatsApp deactivation to isolate messaging-platform effects, [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans see their own side as the primary target, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] ties disinformation concerns to far-right electoral performance. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] provides a rare positive intervention result: hiding elite content on X reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing misinformation as an exposure-architecture problem.

## Sensemaking, Narratives and Rhetoric

A more interpretive arc treats election discourse as collaborative meaning-making. [[Starbird2025-jj]] develops an evidence-frame framework showing how largely-accurate evidence becomes misleading through partisan framing in quote-tweets, and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this into "deep stories" that carry across the 2020 and 2022 US elections. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows how deplatforming the Arizona audit network flipped its authority structure and shifted discourse from mobilisation to abstract defiance. [[Gerard2025-br]] provides a computational counterpart, identifying "bridge users" who structurally seed cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X. Influence operations acquire an organisational dimension in [[Gaw2025-ru]]'s brokerage account of the 2022 Philippine elections, echoing the intermediary logic that also appears in Brazilian ad auditing [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]].

## Methods and Infrastructure

Woven through all of the above is a preoccupation with how to do this research at all. The Italian LLM pipeline work [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and embedding comparison [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] articulate an LLMs-in-the-loop programme for validating unsupervised clustering of political discourse, extending long-running MINE concerns [[F2020-6278a4aa]] about big-data effects on political communication scholarship. Systematic reviews such as [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] map how Facebook Reactions have been operationalised, while [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] uses them substantively across Scandinavian elections. Governance-level infrastructure appears in [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] on DSA-mediated access, in [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s three-phase account of platform election policies (protection → civic integrity → rollback), and in [[Votta2025-xz]] on the opacity of ad-delivery algorithms. Cross-EU comparative infrastructure work [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] links these methodological questions back to substantive findings about untrustworthy URL sharing in France, Germany and Italy. Collectively, this strand argues that empirical claims about elections and political communication are inseparable from the observability regimes — regulatory, computational, and organisational — that make them possible.
