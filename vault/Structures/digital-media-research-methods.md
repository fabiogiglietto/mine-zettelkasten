# Digital and Social Media Research Methods

## The arc of infrastructural precarity

The corpus's spine is a historical narrative about how researchers have accessed, and progressively lost access to, platform data. [[Freelon2018-ao]] inaugurates the "post-API age" framing at the moment of Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure, arguing that TOS-compliant extraction was already obsolete and that web scraping, with its attendant legal risk, was becoming unavoidable. [[Bruns2019-nr]] extends this into the "APIcalypse" thesis, reading post-Cambridge-Analytica restrictions not as privacy protection but as a strategy to frustrate critical, public-interest scrutiny—a reading echoed and updated by [[Freelon2024-sc]], which periodizes two decades of access regimes (laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation, pay-to-play) across six platforms, and by [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], whose "Wild West to Walled Garden" framing stresses the fragility and reversibility of any voluntary access regime. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantify what was lost when Twitter's APIs closed: a "Golden Age" of accessible data that fueled a measurable, citation-rich subfield of "Twitter studies," now in documented bibliometric decline. [[Yang2026-tq]] broadens this into a discipline-wide diagnosis, showing that social-media-based empirical publishing tripled and then plateaued/declined in 2022–2024 precisely as API access tightened, with research topically and platform-wise misaligned with actual public platform use (overreliance on Twitter/Facebook, underuse of YouTube/TikTok). Together these pieces establish data access as a historically contingent, politically negotiated condition rather than a stable research infrastructure.

## The DSA as contested remedy, not solved problem

A second cluster interrogates whether EU regulation—chiefly Article 40 of the Digital Services Act—actually repairs this precarity. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] and [[Pierri2026-ib]] offer the most optimistic-but-clear-eyed readings: platform resistance to Article 40 is read as evidence the regulation is finally biting on real asymmetries of power, even as implementation remains opaque, under-resourced, and unevenly enforced. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] complicate this further by showing how compliance is often gestural: "clean room" environments like the Meta Content Library impose technical and skill-based gatekeeping (Jupyter-only analysis, mandatory data deletion, no cross-platform linkage) that systematically excludes qualitative, mixed-methods, and Global-South researchers—reproducing exactly the WEIRD biases transparency regulation was meant to redress. [[Annabell2026-ev]] pushes the critique to the conceptual level, showing via discourse analysis that "data" itself is a floating signifier in DSA texts, with platforms strategically narrowing its meaning to aggregated, pre-structured quantitative datasets that marginalize humanities and qualitative epistemologies. [[Peters2026-mo]] performs a parallel move for "data quality," showing it is a contested, politically negotiated concept rather than a neutral methodological standard, with academic/NGO stakeholders fighting—only partially successfully—to get it written into the Delegated Regulation. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] extends DSA-oriented scholarship into design/HCI territory, mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto Article 25's autonomy-violation categories to support regulatory auditing. Several empirical audits test whether compliance tools actually function: [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] on TikTok's Research API, [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s rigorous demonstration that YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design" (severe temporal decay, ranking-parameter inconsistency, non-replicable queries), and [[Tonneau2025-bv]]'s comparative analysis of DSA-mandated transparency data revealing stark cross-lingual moderation-workforce disparities that disadvantage Global South languages. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys this toolscape directly, while [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s "Seeing like an API" study of CrowdTangle users theoretically frames all of this: APIs are not neutral conduits but active epistemic mediators whose governance rules and technical design shape what can be known.

## Infrastructure as public good, and its costs

A constructive counter-current proposes alternative or supplementary infrastructures. [[Zheng2026-bi]] builds TubeStats/TokStats as open tools for genuinely random (rather than popularity-biased) sampling of YouTube and TikTok, explicitly framing this as public-interest infrastructure against the "post-API age." [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet generalizes coordinated-behavior detection across platforms and modalities precisely because platform-specific tools are too fragile to rely on, a concern shared by [[Iannucci2025-eg]], [[Mannocci2025-ig]], and [[Luceri2025-tr]], all of which push coordinated inauthentic behavior detection toward multiplex, multimodal, and platform-agnostic methods (the latter tackling TikTok's unprecedented video-first ecosystem) and Bastos2025-ol's visual-identity analysis of troll farms via the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset. [[Schulte2026-df]]'s German-election case study crystallizes the infrastructural argument explicitly: the problem is not absence of findings but absence of reusable, standardized multi-platform observability, a diagnosis that resonates with [[Oswald2025-km]]'s warning that visible platform discourse (what any infrastructure can observe) is itself a skewed "tip of the iceberg" misrepresenting public opinion—a distortion compounded when [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]'s volunteer browser-plugin auditing of Facebook political ads shows crowdsourced workarounds as a feasible, if laborious, substitute for platform transparency. Underscoring the human cost of pursuing such workarounds, [[Park2026-tr]] documents the lived legal risks (CFAA, CMA, DMCA) researchers face when official channels fail, revealing chilling effects, institutional abandonment, and even "vulnerability stockpiling" as researchers route around overbroad computer-crime law—directly paralleling Bruns's and Freelon's warnings about scraping as a necessary evil.

## Measurement artifacts and the hidden biases of "available" data

Even where data is accessible, a cluster of papers shows that its very structure distorts findings. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both dissect the same artifact—Meta's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the Social Science One URL dataset—demonstrating respectively that it masks an algorithm-driven cross-national convergence trend (easily mistaken for a COVID-19 "signal") and that it inflates apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to a representative Nielsen panel. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] extends this logic to web-tracking methodology, showing that the collection environment itself (ex-situ scraping vs. in-situ capture), not just temporal delay, is the dominant and systematically biased source of measurement error in online news-exposure studies. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of the NewsGuard trustworthiness database, finding that continuous scores are fairly stable post-2022 but that binary thresholding can flip substantive conclusions due to a handful of boundary-crossing sources. These pieces collectively argue that "big data" is never raw: anonymization choices, scraping architecture, and third-party rating infrastructures encode biases that researchers must explicitly model rather than treat platform-provided data as ground truth—an argument [[Fan2025-ut]] generalizes methodologically by showing that embedding-space confounds (source, language) can be linearly erased to recover cleaner similarity estimates.

## Recruitment, sampling, and survey validity

A parallel strand concerns getting human respondents rather than platform traces. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] pioneers Facebook-ad-based recruitment using Pixel-based conversion tracking to reach niche, stigmatized populations (conspiracy believers) cheaply and efficiently, though its test of whether ad-targeted "interests" actually proxy for underlying beliefs is inconclusive—a caution about over-trusting platform-inferred attributes that recurs throughout the corpus. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] offers a rigorous comparative audit of nine opt-in online panels, showing a representativeness/response-validity trade-off and that simple attention checks can improve data quality cheaply; it functions as a methodological complement warning that "online samples" are no more a monolith than "platform data" are. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]'s Instagram field experiment demonstrates that platform ad infrastructure (poll stickers, scaled video ads) can itself be repurposed as an intervention and measurement vehicle, showing durable real-world prebunking effects—an optimistic counterpoint to the dataset-bias literature, illustrating that the same commercial ad tooling that constrains research access can also enable rigorously quasi-experimental, platform-native research designs. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s earlier field experiment on cross-partisan exposure and [[Wan2026-ai]]'s study of political efficacy and algorithmic-feed selection round out this empirical-methods strand, both relying on bespoke recruitment/tracking infrastructures (bot-following panels, Google Discover histories) rather than platform-provided aggregates, again underscoring how substantive findings about platforms are hostage to the idiosyncrasies of whatever access method is available.

## LLMs, computational methods, and reflexive critique

A final cluster addresses the rapid incorporation of LLMs into this landscape, with a mix of enthusiasm and caution. [[Balluff2026-if]] is the most skeptical voice, cataloguing reproducibility, environmental, bias, and corporate-dependency costs of unreflective LLM adoption and urging a "least resource-intensive" methodological ethic. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] counters empirically that small, fine-tuned open-source LLMs (LLAMA) can match proprietary models while preserving transparency and reproducibility, directly operationalizing Balluff's call for alternatives. Several papers develop human-in-the-loop hybrid workflows that stake out a middle path: [[Ober2026-vd]] combines topic modeling, LLM labeling, and human codebook refinement for qualitative interview analysis; [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] reframes LLMs as "analytic collaborators" that surface codebook ambiguities in deductive framing analysis rather than as automated classifiers; [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates domain-specific QLoRA fine-tuning for conflict-event classification, emphasizing computational accessibility on consumer hardware. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends computational innovation to the visual domain, showing vision-LLMs capture "connotative" meaning in image clustering that CNN pipelines miss, addressing the broader methodological challenge—also raised by [[Gardam2025-er]]'s visual-first Instagram methodology and [[Bruns2025-fz]]'s "practice mapping" alternative to hairball network visualization—that platform content is increasingly multimodal and resists text-centric or single-modality methods. [[Fan2026-af]] and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] step back to synthesize method and theory: the former proposes a temporal/sequence-based reorientation of digital-trace analysis against time-aggregated conventions, the latter integratively reviews the fragmented algorithm-literacy literature, while [[Hartmann2025-px]] performs an analogous service for echo-chamber research, arguing that much apparent empirical disagreement is actually conceptual and operational heterogeneity. [[Volpe2026-um]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] sit more at the substantive-application end—platform-culture labor and ongoing disinformation-prevalence monitoring, respectively—but both depend on, and implicitly comment upon, the data-access and methodological infrastructures mapped by the rest of this collection, closing the loop between method, regulation, and substantive platform knowledge.
