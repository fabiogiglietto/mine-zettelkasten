# Platform Governance and Research Data Access

## The closing of the aperture: from open APIs to regulated access

The dominant story told across this collection is one of enclosure. [[Freelon2024-sc]], [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], and Bastos2025-ya each periodize roughly the same arc—an early "Wild West" of laissez-faire APIs, a brief "Golden Age" of sanctioned academic access (Twitter's Academic API, CrowdTangle, Social Science One), and a subsequent retreat into paywalled or heavily gated infrastructures. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the consequences bibliometrically: a 25% annual growth rate in Twitter-based scholarship collapsing into a 13% decline once Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month, while [[Yang2026-tq]] shows the broader social-science literature on social media plateauing and declining in 2022–2024 as Twitter/X and Facebook—long the dominant, if unrepresentative, data sources—become harder to study. [[Lukito2026-nb]] names the structural logic underneath this: a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem that filters who gets to do this research at all, disproportionately excluding non-Western and under-resourced scholars, a concern echoed in [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]]'s image of a "data abyss" opening between commercial paywalls and DSA-mediated legal channels.

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act—especially Article 40—emerges as the field's central hope and central disappointment simultaneously. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] all treat Article 40 as a genuine regulatory inflection point: a shift from platform "philanthropy" (researchers granted access at platforms' discretion) to a legal entitlement. But nearly every empirical audit of its implementation finds friction. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality" was barely addressed in the DSA's own texts and had to be fought into the Delegated Regulation by academic and NGO stakeholders, with Meta strategically inverting the argument to claim access was futile. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] document that even where APIs formally exist (TikTok's Research API, YouTube's Data API), they are riddled with undocumented limitations, temporal decay, and inconsistency that undermine their use for the systemic-risk research the DSA is meant to enable. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this critique to the "clean room" model exemplified by the Meta Content Library, arguing that web-disconnected, code-only environments structurally exclude qualitative and cross-platform researchers and widen North-South and disciplinary inequities. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] converge, however, on a shared strategic conclusion: this turbulence is not evidence of regulatory failure but of platforms resisting a genuine constraint on their power, and the correct posture is a "marathon" of continued implementation rather than retreat.

## What gets lost when infrastructure changes: measurement artifacts and epistemic instability

A second, more methodological thread concerns how changes to platform infrastructure—APIs, thresholds, ranking systems—introduce artifacts that researchers routinely mistake for social signal. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] is the paradigm case: the 100-public-shares threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical monthly trends across 46 wildly different countries, a pattern only explicable as a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely "Meaningful Social Interactions" tuning) rather than genuine cross-national convergence. [[McNally2025-dn]] pursues the same intuition from the other direction, showing that Facebook's News Feed changes leave detectable, laggard fingerprints (19–24 day delays) on engagement with hard news specifically—evidence, the authors argue, that the "black box" is not actually opaque to systematic long-run auditing, a claim with direct bearing on what DSA Article 40(4) audits might achieve. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], using the Meta Content Library itself, extend this into a sustained argument that Facebook is an active curator whose "political content reduction" policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach by 72% well before Meta's announced rollout date—and that extremist accounts adapted by posting more, not less. Together these papers reposition the platform not as a neutral pipe but as a variable, historically contingent instrument whose governance decisions are themselves the object of study, echoing [[Bechmann2026-dr]]'s call to treat platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure rather than chase discrete causal effects.

This concern with measurement fragility recurs at smaller grain too. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that ex-situ web scraping—the default method for studying news exposure—introduces ~34% content distortion dominated by paywalls and interactive elements, systematically biasing which content types are visible to researchers. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit of NewsGuard, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs are highly sensitive to single sources crossing a threshold, while continuous scores are comparatively stable—a methodological warning for the vast literature that treats source ratings as ground truth. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to the same underlying problem (popularity-biased, opportunistic sampling) with an infrastructural fix: genuinely random sampling tools (TubeStats, TokStats) built by exploiting platforms' ID-space architecture rather than their APIs. Read together, these papers suggest that the "post-API age" is not simply about less data but about data whose provenance and construction increasingly demand forensic scrutiny before substantive claims can be made.

## Industry entanglement and the limits of collaboration

A distinct but related concern is the compromised independence of platform-adjacent research itself. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] document, empirically and programmatically, how deeply industry ties permeate high-profile social media scholarship: roughly half of papers in Science, Nature, and PNAS have disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, with editors and reviewers implicated alongside authors, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this in terms familiar from tobacco and pharma studies—restricted data access, selective funding, subtle influence, institutional entanglement—as does the sharper case study by [[Munger2025-cz]], whose meta-scientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership ([[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Appel2026-qr]], [[noauthor_undated-bm]]) argues that even the most methodologically ambitious platform collaboration produced findings with poor "temporal validity" (platforms change too fast to replicate) and "poetic validity" (core concepts like "echo chamber" were operationalized inconsistently across the collaboration's own papers), concluding that reactive empirical study is structurally inadequate and that proactive disclosure mandates are the only durable fix. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Ohme2026-nv]] cite the same Meta2020 case as a cautionary tale for DSA-era research: undisclosed algorithmic changes mid-study can silently bias platform-mediated findings. [[Allen2025-ot]] and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] gesture toward one methodological workaround—platform-independent experiments using browser extensions and custom clients (echoing Piccardi et al. and Jaidka's "Elite Effect" study)—as a way of regaining causal leverage without needing platform cooperation at all, a strategy [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] extends by reverse-engineering X's recommender embeddings from donated data rather than requesting access.

The fragility of even the most celebrated access mechanism is dramatized by [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s interview study of CrowdTangle users, whose "Seeing like an API" framework shows how the tool's technical design and governance rules shaped not just what researchers could see but what questions they could even ask—before it was discontinued altogether, a discontinuation [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] note landed with damaging timing just before the 2024 US election.

## Discourse, framing, and the politics of platform self-presentation

A final cluster examines how platforms narrate their own governance to pre-empt or shape regulatory and public scrutiny. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the foundational move here, showing how the very term "platform" performs discursive work—claiming neutrality and openness while eliding the editorial interventions platforms actually make—and this framing is picked up and extended by [[Hurcombe2025-cs]], whose discourse analysis of Meta's Newsroom identifies four recurring frames (inauthenticity, political advertising, technological solutions, enforcement) that externalize blame for problematic content while positioning Meta as capable problem-solver, with striking thematic overlap with subsequent EU and Australian regulation. [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" generalizes this into a cross-platform pattern: Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively stage infrastructural and ideological shifts as continuity or creator-benevolent innovation, masking underlying power consolidation. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] applies a similar lens to Google's AI Overviews, showing competing framings by Google, journalists, and SEO marketers that together obscure the user's own voice and the societal stakes of generative search. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the most consequential recent instance of this discursive politics: Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors, which the authors show is empirically unfounded (removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases) but nonetheless justified Meta's shift toward Community Notes—a system [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] separately show is structurally prone to undermoderating polarizing content because its bridging algorithm requires cross-partisan consensus that adversarial content rarely achieves. [[Farkas2026-lr]]'s interviews with European fact-checkers reveal the resulting defensive posture: professional verifiers forced into elaborate rhetorical work to justify continued platform dependence even as they publicly reject platforms' self-serving narratives.

## Regulation as contested terrain, not settled outcome

Taken as a whole, the collection resists any triumphalist reading of the DSA as having "solved" the data-access problem. Instead it depicts a genuinely contested field: [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA alongside US state laws and Germany's NetzDG as one of several divergent "circulation regimes," each encoding different security rationalities rather than converging toward a single global model; [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] traces how platforms' own election-policy commitments have cycled through phases of protection, civic-integrity expansion, and recent rollback in direct response to political pressure rather than principle; and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] shows that even where DSA transparency mandates nominally apply, their substance (e.g., moderator allocation by language) reveals persistent Global-South blind spots that transparency reporting alone cannot fix. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] suggest the DSA's substantive provisions (Article 25's autonomy protections, ad-delivery transparency) open new terrain for audit-based accountability, while [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] show, through concrete election case studies, how far current infrastructure still falls short of enabling reliable multi-platform, cross-national observability. The throughline connecting nearly all these papers—whether concerned with API economics, discourse framing, industry entanglement, or measurement artifacts—is that platform governance and research access are not separable domains: every choice about what data researchers may see is itself an act of governance, and every empirical finding about platforms is conditioned by the infrastructural politics that made it visible in the first place.
