# Polarization & Partisan Media Dynamics

# Polarization & Partisan Media Dynamics: A Structure Note

## The problem of measurement itself

Before asking what polarization *does*, a striking cluster of papers in this collection asks whether we even know what it *is*. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds that depolarization recommendations in the literature are systematically disconnected from the evidence scholars themselves produce, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows the same disease afflicts echo-chamber research: 129 studies disagree less because reality is contested than because conceptualizations and operationalizations vary wildly. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this empirically—running 504 specifications of media-slant and polarization measures on the same panel data, they show that methodological choice alone swings coefficient magnitude nearly sevenfold, with audience-based scores systematically inflating effects relative to content-based ones. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] adds a geographic critique: polarization research is disciplinarily fragmented *and* overwhelmingly Western/US-centric, undermining any claim to general theory. Taken together, these pieces function as a methodological conscience for the rest of the topic, and their skepticism should discipline how the more confident empirical claims below are read. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]], a Nature editorial, closes this loop by insisting scientists bear responsibility for turning this fragmented evidence base into actionable depolarization strategy—precisely the gap [[van-Eck2026-xg]] diagnoses.

## Do platforms cause polarization, or does polarization use platforms?

A second axis of debate concerns causal direction and mechanism. The classical filter-bubble/echo-chamber thesis is challenged early by [[Bakshy2015-rn]], who show individual click choices suppress cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does—a finding [[Brown2026-br]] essentially reproduces for YouTube two eras later, finding "rabbit holes" (content-to-content Markov dynamics) but no algorithmic "radicalization pathway" independent of user choice. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers a conceptual resolution: replace the static bubble metaphor with a feedback-loop framework in which reinforcing and self-regulating dynamics coexist, explaining why catastrophic radicalization is rare even though real reinforcement occurs.

Yet more recent, higher-powered field experiments push back toward algorithmic causality. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds X's "For You" feed causally shifts attitudes rightward and, crucially, that this shift persists asymmetrically because the algorithm alters *who users follow*—a persistence mechanism that reconciles the null findings of [[Allcott2025-jb]] (which found no attitudinal effects of ad removal on Facebook/Instagram) with more dramatic effects elsewhere. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] locate the mechanism more precisely: right-leaning visibility gains on Musk-era Twitter are explained less by ideology than by agitation and proximity to Musk, while X's recommender embeds a nearly-perfect linear encoding of Left-Right position that can, in principle, be surgically removed. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Registered Report on Bluesky demonstrates that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts *perceived social norms* around partisan animosity—reframing the mechanism from direct persuasion to normative misperception—while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment. [[McNally2025-dn]] contributes a methodological bridge here, showing that Facebook's black-boxed feed changes leave detectable footprints in engagement time series, license for the kind of longitudinal auditing [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] then performs at scale, finding that Facebook actively (if unevenly) suppresses partisan-audience content and rewards journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate with governance crises like the 2020 "break the glass" interventions.

## Insularity, echo chambers, and the limits of the town-square metaphor

A related empirical strand tests whether platform spaces actually function as segregated enclaves. [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s sixteen-year study of political subreddits finds minimal cross-ideological overlap even between *ideologically similar* communities, arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition rather than emergent from discourse—directly undercutting the idea of r/politics as cross-ideological town square. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] finds a parallel dynamic in the 2018 Italian election: populist-aligned news sources are more insular on Twitter, and insularity predicts a distinct Facebook engagement signature (shares over comments) that the Five Star Movement exploits strategically. [[Zhu2026-tn]] complicates the picture cross-nationally, showing audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media is generally low but varies with media-system type and the institutionalized access of populist parties—Sweden's dense, isolated alternative cluster contrasts with more porous boundaries in Austria and France. [[Green2025-ap]] offers a methodological corrective underlying much of this work: domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous *story-level* heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" outlets often harbor sharply divergent partisan audiences story by story—a caution relevant to interpreting insularity metrics generally. [[Balluff2026-ev]] finds yet another twist: German alternative media reference largely the *same* named entities as legacy outlets, differing not in content universe but in network structure and in how often they cite mainstream media itself.

## Affective polarization and the emotional economy of partisan engagement

A dense cluster examines the affective, rather than ideological, register of partisanship. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] converge on a robust finding: anger-eliciting content, especially from populist actors, drives Facebook sharing more than love/positive content, which instead correlates with "likes"—a distinction extended longitudinally by [[Larsson2026-ro]], who tracks the Norwegian Progress Party's shift toward negativity under a new leader and its consistent reward in shares and comments. [[Marino2026-slef]] pushes this into the heart of a hyperpartisan community, finding that pro-Bolsonaro Facebook engagement is *unstable* rather than static—shifting abruptly around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt, with love-to-anger transitions and rising internal debate suggesting echo chambers are contested, permeable, even subject to deliberate "brigading" by out-group users. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a cognate bias at the perceptual level: a "hostile misinformation effect" in which partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, amplified by political interest and identity strength, and—counterintuitively—stronger among election *winners*. [[Esau2025-tf]] proposes a normative response, theorizing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to what it calls destructive polarization, an ambition that dovetails with the interventionist logic of [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]].

## Elite strategy: who spreads misinformation, and why

A major thread reframes misinformation not as a diffuse cognitive failure but as elite political strategy. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary Twitter analysis is pivotal: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts low-factuality sharing, but their *interaction*—radical-right populism—is the strongest predictor, tied to a legitimacy crisis vis-à-vis liberal institutions and symbiosis with clickbait media ecosystems. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] traces a parallel mechanism inside the US Republican party: the CRT issue reveals a "logic of connective faction" whereby ideologically extreme members forge ties to hyperpartisan digital networks, rewarded with engagement and coverage that further radicalizes intra-party dynamics. [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes this into a "communication-as-organization" framework, arguing that digitally networked "surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys) pull conservative parties toward illiberalism in ways that both technocentric disinformation research and institutionalist backsliding theory miss in isolation. [[Renault2025-uh]] supplies a striking empirical test of partisan asymmetry via a genuinely bridging (not fact-checker-driven) system, finding Republicans flagged 2.3x more than Democrats on Community Notes—directly undermining the Musk/Zuckerberg rationale for replacing professional fact-checking. Yet [[Bouchaud2026-np]] warns the same bridging mechanism structurally *undermoderates* the most polarizing content by design, creating electoral risk regardless of partisan direction. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] extend the elite-strategy lens to institutional position: officials' misinformation sharing is conditioned by institutional checks, while Brazilian parliamentarians pursue distinct "epistemic authority" strategies (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, brokerage) during crisis, revealing politicians as media producers contesting journalistic gatekeeping rather than passive sources.

## Disinformation reconceived as narrative and identity

Running alongside the elite-strategy literature is a parallel move to redefine disinformation itself. [[Marwick2026-qd]] and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] argue disinformation should be understood as cross-platform *narrative*—identity-affirming, cathartic, resistant to fact-checking because it satisfies desire rather than describes fact. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes this computationally via Greimas' Actantial Model, finding that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are sharply polarized narratively, but that comments *converge* on surface narrative even as deeper "motifs" preserve partisan difference—complicating assumptions that comment sections simply reinforce echo chambers. [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame framework similarly shows that election-integrity rumors are less about false facts than about politically-charged *framing* of accurate evidence, with quote-tweet structures making the escalation of frames empirically visible. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends the narrative-identity lens to radicalization itself, showing "redpilling" is typically gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion, legitimated through participants' self-image as rational evaluators of "evidence." [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] finds the same identity-work dynamic structuring "Great Reset" conspiracy communities across six platforms, arguing symbolic boundary work—not platform affordances or content—sustains conspiracy culture. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows how the very term "fake news" is itself weaponized symmetrically by left and right outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy and deviance. Against this narrative-turn sits harder-nosed work on differential exposure and belief effects: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] find electoral misinformation belief in Brazil directly erodes political tolerance and trust in election results, with messaging apps as a key indirect pathway, while [[Lieu2025-nl]] shows climate misinformation content-category (not rhetorical fallacy) drives both perceived veracity and political polarization.

## Reach, diffusion, and the architecture of exposure

A more infrastructural set of papers asks not what content says but how far and through what channels it actually travels. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] establish that misinformation diffusion is concentrated and platform-dependent, a point sharpened considerably by [[Dahlke2026-sl34]]'s "embedded referrer" reanalysis, which shows platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is routinely underestimated 1.5–15x by direct-referral methods—with sharply partisan-asymmetric effects (Twitter for Republicans, YouTube for Democrats)—while also showing platform bans would disproportionately cut high-quality news too. This directly informs [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s finding that Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented it. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] intervenes experimentally at the exposure-architecture level, showing that simply hiding elite-authored posts reduces low-credibility sharing and misinformation susceptibility without moving affective polarization—suggesting exposure structure, not attitude change, is the more tractable lever. Gaisbauer2025-br and [[Eady2025-vm]] contribute methodological infrastructure for this whole diffusion literature: multi-level "political cartography" and joint elite-public ideology scaling via shared URLs, the latter showing polarized politicians dominate the visible news-sharing environment and that reduced electoral competition predicts more extreme sharing. [[Gerard2025-br]] extends network reconstruction cross-platform, identifying a tiny population of "bridge users" who seed roughly 70% of narratives migrating between Truth Social and X. [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] examine targeting and visibility from the supply side directly: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads and disproportionate radical-right media visibility in the 2024 EU elections, respectively, both suggesting structural (not merely algorithmic) amplification of illiberal content.

## Platform-specific dynamics: TikTok, YouTube, and the "second generation"

A distinct cluster interrogates how platform architecture itself, rather than partisan behavior, reshapes polarization dynamics. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] jointly theorize a generational shift from "networked" to "clustered" or "parasocial" publics—item-centric, algorithmically inferred groupings replacing peer-to-peer interpersonal ties—with consequences for depersonalization and fragmentation that other papers in this note empirically instantiate. [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows how this clustered, recommendation-driven architecture sustains an "ambient ideology" (the Tate-space) that outpaces YouTube's own moderation. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extend this to far-right visual/memetic strategy on Instagram and TikTok respectively, both emphasizing platform-tailored affective softening over overt hostility. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates the doom narrative, finding pro-immigration content actually dominates Canadian TikTok, with ideologically opposed users deploying the platform's affordances in strikingly similar ways—a "shared vernacular" finding that echoes [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s comment-convergence result. [[Rothut2026-or]] shows Telegram operating as a distinct mainstreaming conduit, where protest movements like Querdenken become structurally embedded in far-right networks via shared anti-elite framing.

## LLMs as new actors in the polarization ecosystem

Emerging alongside is a cluster examining generative AI's dual role as diagnostic tool and persuasive/inferential agent. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text via subtle cultural cues, raising the same targeting risks documented for platform ads. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrate AI systems out-persuading even expert human debaters and canvassers across real elections, driven by information throughput rather than rapport—though [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows this persuasive power is contingent on perceived neutrality, collapsing by ~25% when users are told the model is biased against their party. [[Dubey2026-bl]] finds "balanced" chatbots are, counterintuitively, trusted *more* by high-conspiracy-belief users. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] synthesizes this frontier into policy terms, proposing LLM-enabled "bridging systems" and collective dialogue as depolarization infrastructure—an aspirational counterpoint to the largely diagnostic and cautionary tenor of the rest of this section.

## Coordination, bots, and authoritarian contexts

A final strand extends polarization dynamics beyond liberal democracies and beyond human actors alone. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[Zhao2025-ny]] examine social bots as agenda-builders and hashtag hijackers, finding bots most effective at shifting attribute (sentiment) salience toward negativity rather than shaping the press. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to authoritarian "connective counteraction," showing pro-Navalny and pro-government Russian Twitter mobilization exhibit distinct centralization/modularity signatures, with government-aligned astroturfing operating through more clustered, team-based coordination. Karlsnes2026-hd closes the loop reflexively: media narratives *about* election interference, independent of whether interference actually works, themselves increase affective polarization when the reported beneficiary is the opposing party—suggesting alarmist discourse about disinformation can itself become a polarizing vector, a fitting final complication for a literature that, as [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] remind us, must constantly interrogate its own conceptual and methodological premises.
