# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior: from detection to doctrine

This body of work traces a decade-long arc from the discovery of a specific behavioral signature—near-synchronous link sharing—to a general theory of manufactured attention, and finally to speculative concern about what happens when the coordinating agents are no longer human. It is anchored by a signature methodological lineage running through [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], the CooRnet tool it introduced, and its descendants, but it now radiates into election forensics, platform governance, authoritarian information control, and the encyclopedic systematization of "coordination" as a concept in its own right.

### The founding move: from content to coordination

[[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] is the load-bearing paper of this collection. Its central argument—that disinformation research should stop chasing bad content or bad actors and instead detect *coordinated collective action*—reframes the entire field around behavioral, content-agnostic signals. This ecological reframing is operationalized and hardened across a series of applied studies: [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] extends CLSB/CooRnet to COVID-19 fact-checking workflows, showing the method surfaces problematic content faster than manual fact-checking and documenting tactics like link laundering and first-comment link hiding; [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] converts the static-list problem into an iterative monitoring workflow for the 2022 Italian election, revealing how quickly known coordinated networks mutate and spawn new assets; and [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and [[Righetti2025-slf9]] apply the same lens outward—to Alternative Influence Networks amplifying pandemic pseudoscience, and to the transnational anti-gender organization CitizenGO, respectively—demonstrating that CLSB-style coordination is not confined to disinformation "farms" but structures mainstream advocacy and media ecosystems as well.

The toolkit itself keeps evolving. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet generalizes coordination detection to any uniquely identifiable shared object across platforms and modalities, explicitly positioning itself against the platform-specificity of CooRnet and prior tools. [[Yang2025-iv]] pushes further by abandoning timing altogether, exploiting statistical regularities in sharing *speed and frequency* to evade the trivial manipulability of timestamp-based detection. This methodological refinement continues in [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]], both of which argue that coordination is inherently multimodal and temporal—that no single action-type (retweets, hashtags, URLs) suffices, and that naive flattening of multi-layer networks discards structurally important signal. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends detection to real-time bot coordination on Bluesky, while [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the harder problem of adapting text-centric coordination methods to TikTok's video-first ecosystem, and [[Minici2024-tf]] proposes a graph foundation model (IOHunter) explicitly aimed at cross-campaign generalization—signaling a shift from bespoke detection to transferable, foundation-model-based infrastructure.

### From signature to typology: what does coordination *mean*?

A second cluster interrogates the meaning, not just the detection, of coordination signatures. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] is pivotal here: it shows that identical CooRnet-style coordination signatures can belong to influence operations, legitimate media outlets, political activists, or gambling ad networks alike, and traces how Meta's own definition of CIB has narrowed from "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors," letting much manufactured attention persist unlabeled. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] answers this ambiguity by proposing "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category defined by the convergence of deceptive intent, affordance exploitation, and coordination—deliberately decoupling the concept from misinformation per se, and diagnosing a "Zombie Internet" condition in which synthetic and authentic engagement become indistinguishable. [[Thiele2025-ol]] takes this conceptual work furthest, critiquing "coordinated inauthentic behavior" itself as analytically inadequate (it conflates inauthenticity with intent, and assumes real-name platforms) and proposing a rational-choice attribution model and an eight-type typology of campaigns based on scale, elaborateness, and disguise. [[Song2025-yh]] and [[Graham2026-fb]] push from opposite directions toward the same conclusion—that coordination and even "botting" are not inherently malicious: CLSB can carry credible public-health messaging as readily as anti-vaccine narratives, and Reddit's mushroom-emoji "botting" is better understood as culturally negotiated participatory performance than as an authenticity violation. Together these papers mark a maturation of the field away from treating coordination signatures as self-evidently sinister toward treating authenticity as a contested, contextual, and gradated construct—a shift also visible in [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]]'s account of Serbia's SNS party, whose networks are notable precisely for how convincingly they *avoid* looking coordinated.

### Does coordination matter? Causal skepticism and scale

A countercurrent within the topic asks how much coordinated activity actually shapes outcomes. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formalizes this skepticism graph-theoretically, showing that real coordinated accounts in UK election cascades achieve influence far below optimal or even random-matched benchmarks—suggesting CIB is less pivotal to diffusion than commonly assumed. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion dataset paper [[noauthor_undated-bm]] deliver the most consequential empirical corrective at scale: analyzing Meta's 49 "deceptive network" takedowns around the 2020 US election, they show that reach is wildly concentrated in a few networks, that most exposure comes from *reshares by unaffiliated accounts* rather than direct network posting, and that associations between exposure and downstream political outcomes evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. This exposure-based, causally cautious approach stands in productive tension with [[Kim2026-wg]], which uses direct ad-exposure tracking to find genuine, targeted turnout-suppression effects concentrated in geo-racially targeted subpopulations—illustrating that null average effects can mask real harms in heterogeneous subgroups. [[Pante2025-pq]] extends this skeptical, benchmark-driven posture to claims of inter-state IO collaboration, arguing prior findings lack adequate control comparisons.

### Coordination in the wild: platforms, regimes, and movements

A large empirical wing applies coordination detection to specific political and platform contexts, mapping how the phenomenon adapts to infrastructure and regime type. On authoritarian and semi-authoritarian terrain, [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] traces pro-government Telegram coordination in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] examine dual-use coordination during Chinese COVID protests and Russian pro-/anti-Navalny mobilization, the latter extending Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to "connective counteraction"; [[Kim2026-br]] offers a two-decade, explainable-ML analysis of state-linked trolling in Korean news comments, finding condemnation rhetoric—not overt promotion—drives engagement. On electoral and platform-specific terrain, [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification in Romanian EP elections; [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] and [[Simeone2025-vo]] examine German advertising coordination and the network "ripple effects" of Twitter's Arizona-audit deplatforming, respectively, with the latter notably finding CIB was *not* the operative mechanism—human disengagement was. [[Kansaon2025-id]] extends detection into WhatsApp's closed ecosystem in Brazil, linking coordinated messaging to real-world Supreme Court attacks and January 8th mobilization. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] and Bastos2025-ol turn to the supply side of coordination itself, using OSINT CVs and computer vision respectively to reconstruct the IRA's organizational labor structure and the visual self-presentation of troll-farm personas. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations in the Philippines as political brokerage, and [[Gerard2025-br]] models cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X through "bridge users," offering a discourse-centered alternative to interaction-based coordination graphs.

### Mainstreaming, radicalization, and the far right's media ecology

Adjacent to coordination proper is a cluster tracing how coordinated or quasi-coordinated online ecosystems mainstream radical content. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" via Querdenken's Telegram entanglement with far-right networks; [[Askanius2026-de]] and [[Copland2025-em]] map alternative influence networks—Swedish far-right YouTubers and Sky News Australia, respectively—showing how guest appearances, hyperlinking, and dual national/international content strategies hybridize commercial influencer culture with propaganda; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] extends connective action theory to intra-party factionalism via the CRT case; [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses collective-action framing and Granger causality to link Proud Boys' online rhetoric to offline violence; and [[Karo2026-dn]] documents adversarial creative evasion tactics ("everyday extremism") among jihadist TikTok communities, paralleling the evasion literature elsewhere in this set (link laundering, first-comment hiding, misspelled keywords).

### Narrative, source, and the limits of domain-based misinformation frames

A methodologically distinct group critiques source- and content-based misinformation metrics from within: [[Goel2025-iq]] shows mainstream outlets' true content is routinely repurposed into misleading narratives via co-sharing with fake news, evading domain-blocklist detection; [[Prochaska2025-ef]] argues individual-post analysis misses the collaborative "deep story" work of election-fraud rumoring; [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as strategic narrative substitution; [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces a coordinated, non-bot-driven contrarian network amplifying credentialed anti-consensus scientists in COVID discourse and its downstream effect on news coverage; and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows misinformation's *source* (influencer vs. regular user) independently drives toxic audience reactions. These papers collectively insist that coordination, source, and narrative context—not just verifiable falsity—are what make information "problematic."

### The AI horizon: from human coordination to synthetic swarms

The collection closes on a speculative and generative-AI-inflected frontier. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents an empirically observed structural break: coordinated gambling-promotion networks on Facebook exploded exponentially after ChatGPT's launch, with generative AI intensifying rather than replacing established persuasion drivers. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates this frontier directly, showing that networked LLM agents spontaneously reproduce IO-like coordination—network density, narrative homogenization, synchronized amplification—merely from mutual awareness of teammates, without explicit deliberation. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a policy register, warning that LLM-agentic "malicious AI swarms" represent a qualitative leap beyond IRA-style botnets, capable of manufacturing consensus and poisoning training data at scale, and proposing layered governance responses. [[Graham2025-gp]] closes the loop conceptually, arguing (via #IStandWithPutin) that propaganda's contemporary danger lies in exploiting rather than evading the platform infrastructures built for truth—a fitting capstone thesis for a research program whose central discovery was that inauthenticity is best found not in what is said, but in the coordinated structure of how it is said together.
