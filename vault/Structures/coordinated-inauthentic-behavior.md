# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The methodological core: from CLSB to a generalized coordination science

The structure's spine is the CooRnet/CLSB research program itself. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establishes the founding move: rather than chasing content veracity or bad actors, treat *coordinated collective action*—accounts sharing the same URLs near-simultaneously and repeatedly—as the diagnostic signal of manipulation, empirically grounding Facebook's own "coordinated inauthentic behavior" category in prior scholarship on connective action and cloaked authenticity. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] operationalizes this for real-time fact-checking during COVID-19, showing CLSB-detected content is problematic at a higher rate than routine fact-checking baselines, while cataloguing the evasive tactics (link laundering, first-comment links, religious-group hijacking) that coordinated actors develop in response to detection. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] closes the loop methodologically, turning static lists into a continuously updating workflow and demonstrating, across three case studies from the 2022 Italian election, that content-agnostic, actor/behavior-focused detection surfaces political astroturfing, click-economy operations, and religious proselytism networks alike.

This lineage is then generalized and challenged by a wave of successor tools. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet reframes coordination as a minimal, platform-agnostic abstraction—repeated near-synchronous sharing of any uniquely identifiable object—explicitly responding to CooRnet's platform-specificity and to API volatility, and validates against the South Korean NIS dataset. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] interrogates a different limitation: coordination is inherently multimodal, and naive flattening of retweet/reply/mention/hashtag layers discards structurally important actors; multiplex community detection is proposed as the more balanced operationalization. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] pushes further by adding temporal decay kernels to multiplex representations, showing time-awareness plus modality-preservation together outperform single-signal baselines across 26 benchmark datasets. [[Yang2025-iv]] attacks the timing-dependence problem from another angle, exploiting statistical regularities in sharing speed/frequency that are harder to game than raw synchronicity. Meanwhile [[Minici2024-tf]]'s IOHunter and [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE push toward foundation-model and discourse-embedding approaches that generalize across campaigns and even across platforms, with Gerard's "bridge users" reframing cross-platform influence as structural narrative alignment rather than direct interaction. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] and [[Luceri2025-tr]] extend detection to new terrain—Bluesky's decentralized architecture and TikTok's video-first ecosystem—arguing existing text-centric methods do not transfer cleanly.

## Conceptual reckonings: what does "coordinated" and "inauthentic" actually mean?

A parallel strand interrogates the categories themselves rather than the detection machinery. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows that identical coordination *signatures* can belong to influence operations, legitimate media outlets, or grassroots meme communities alike—coordination detection cannot by itself distinguish inauthentic from authentic behavior, and Meta's own definition of CIB has narrowed over time from broad "false amplifiers" to only "adversarial threat actors," leaving much manufactured attention on-platform. [[Thiele2025-ol]] goes further, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and builds a rational-choice attribution typology (scale, elaborateness, disguise) to infer hidden principals from observable campaign features. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a still broader category unifying propaganda, gambling promotion, and hijacked-group fraud under shared structural mechanisms—affordance exploitation, coordination, deceptive framing—regardless of motive, coining the "Zombie Internet" condition where human and automated participation become indistinguishable. [[Song2025-yh]] complicates the moral valence further still, showing CLSB is "ideologically agnostic": pro-vaccine coordination looks structurally similar to anti-vaccine coordination, differing mainly in source credibility and national political culture (UK safety-skepticism vs. US freedom-framing).

## A skeptical counter-current: does coordination actually matter?

Running against the field's founding assumption that coordinated activity is consequential, several 2025–26 papers ask whether CIB actually moves outcomes. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models influence in retweet cascades and finds real coordinated accounts achieve far less influence than optimal or even random placement would predict—CIB may be structurally marginal rather than pivotal. [[Simeone2025-vo]] finds that the disruptive "ripple effects" of the Twitter Arizona-audit deplatforming were *not* attributable to bot-like CIB (only 10 accounts met coordination thresholds); real human disengagement, not coordination collapse, drove the effect. [[Pante2025-pq]] revisits claimed inter-state collaboration between state-backed operations and finds it does not survive rigorous, control-benchmarked reanalysis. And the large [[Appel2026-qr]]/[[noauthor_undated-bm]] FIES studies—analyzing Meta's own 49 deceptive networks around the 2020 US election—show that while reach was substantial (37M Facebook users), it was concentrated in a handful of networks, driven mostly by *non-network* reshares, and that correlations with downstream political outcomes evaporate once pre-exposure user characteristics are controlled. Together these papers push the program toward a more measured, exposure- and causality-conscious empirical standard than early prevalence counts encouraged.

## Empirical domains I: elections and state-aligned networks worldwide

The CLSB toolkit and its relatives have been deployed across an expanding roster of electoral contexts: Italy ([[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]), Germany ([[Righetti2025-sl2a]], [[Slack1783507532-sl10]]), the US 2020 election ([[Appel2026-qr]], [[noauthor_undated-bm]]), the 2019 UK general election ([[Mannocci2025-ig]], [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]), Brazil's WhatsApp ecosystem ([[Kansaon2025-id]]), Romania's 2024 European Parliament campaign ([[Oprea2025-lf]]), and Serbia's SNS-linked "mimetic" networks that evade detection precisely by *not* resembling centralized bot farms ([[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]]). State-aligned amplification is examined structurally in Russia and Belarus ([[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]), in Russian Twitter's protest/counter-protest dynamic around Navalny ([[Kulichkina2025-sl09]]), and in China's 2022 COVID protests ([[Kulichkina2026-zk]]), while [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the IRA's organizational life (via leaked employee CVs) grounds the whole tradition in the labor-market realities of an actual troll factory, revealing recruitment tied to Crimea rather than the 2016 US election and a workforce indistinguishable from an ordinary PR agency.

## Empirical domains II: health, science communication, and ideological mainstreaming

A second empirical cluster tracks how coordination interacts with contested knowledge and ideology. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]]'s covid-skeptic network mapping in Italy and [[Song2025-yh]]'s UK/US vaccine-stance comparison show coordination operating on both sides of health controversies, while [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces a distinctly *contrarian* coordinated retweet network amplifying credentialed anti-consensus scientists, with news media typically following rather than leading superspreader activity. Coordination also appears as an infrastructure for ideological mainstreaming: [[Rothut2026-or]] documents how Germany's Querdenken protest movement became structurally embedded in far-right/conspiracist Telegram networks; [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a cohesive Swedish far-right YouTube "alternative influence network" fusing influencer monetization with propaganda; and [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows CitizenGO running a centrally coordinated multilingual network that manufactures the appearance of transnational grassroots consensus around anti-gender politics—all three cases extending CIB-adjacent methods from narrowly electoral contexts into the slower-moving question of discursive normalization.

## Frontier: automation, generative AI, and the coordination-as-labor problem

The most recent papers gesture toward where the phenomenon is heading. [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] examine bots as active discourse-shapers—hijacking hashtags and functioning as "agenda-builders" against campaign messaging, particularly through negative-sentiment amplification. [[Orlando2025-ul]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]] look forward rather than backward: the former shows that networked LLM agents spontaneously reproduce IO coordination signatures (synchronized amplification, narrative convergence) once given mere awareness of teammates, without any explicit directive; the latter extrapolates this into a policy-forum warning about "malicious AI swarms" that could fabricate consensus at a scale and adaptivity beyond anything the IRA-era botnets achieved. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] supplies an empirical bridge between these speculative futures and the CLSB tradition's present: coordinated Facebook gambling networks show an exponential, structurally-dated jump in posting volume after ChatGPT's launch, with generative AI intensifying—rather than replacing—the persuasion drivers CLSB-style research has long catalogued, and exploiting the same paid/organic regulatory asymmetries that [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] identify as the structural soft spot of platform governance. Read together, the corpus traces an arc from a founded, validated detection method, through generalization and self-critique, into an open question about whether the next generation of coordination will even require human coordinators at all.
