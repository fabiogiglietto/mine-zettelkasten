# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The methodological core: from CLSB to generalized coordination detection

The foundation of this strand is the CooRnet/CLSB tradition originating with [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframed disinformation research away from content-veracity and bad-actor detection toward an "ecological," action-based focus on coordinated collective behavior. The key methodological move—detecting accounts that share identical URLs within abnormally short time windows—became a durable signature, refined across [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] (the CooRnet tool and its A-B-C iterative workflow) and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] (a continuously-updating workflow that keeps pace with actors who adapt, get suspended, or rebrand). [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] extended this into a fact-checker-facing tool (MINE-FACTS), demonstrating that CLSB surfaces a higher hit-rate of problematic content than routine manual fact-checking. This lineage culminates methodologically in [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet, which generalizes coordination detection to any uniquely identifiable shared object across platforms and modalities, explicitly responding to the brittleness of platform-specific, API-dependent tools.

Parallel to this applied lineage, a more theoretical/network-science strand has matured the concept of coordination itself. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] established that coordination exists on a continuum and is orthogonal to automation—a foundational corrective against conflating bots with coordination. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] push this further by tackling multimodality and temporality, showing that no single co-action signal (retweets, hashtags, URLs) is sufficient and that flattening multiplex layers loses structurally important communities. [[Yang2025-iv]] targets a specific evasion vector—manipulable timing signals—by exploiting statistical regularities in sharing speed and frequency instead. This technical maturation is capped by two recent survey/synthesis efforts: [[Mannocci2026-kc]], which proposes a unifying definition of coordinated online behavior around actors, synergic actions, and intent, arguing authenticity and harmfulness are orthogonal axes; and [[Thiele2025-ol]], which pivots the field toward the harder "attribution problem," building a rational-choice typology linking campaign scale, elaborateness, and disguise to a principal's resources and stakes. Newer entrants—graph foundation models ([[Minici2024-tf]]), cross-platform discourse embeddings ([[Gerard2025-br]]), and real-time bot detection on emerging platforms like Bluesky ([[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]])—extend detection to new architectures and platforms, while [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the specific challenge of adapting text-centric coordination detection to TikTok's video-first ecosystem.

## Does coordination matter? The influence-quantification turn

A crucial recent counter-current interrogates whether detected coordination is actually consequential. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] formalize post-hoc influence evaluation on cascade trees, finding that real coordinated accounts in the 2019 UK election dataset achieved influence far below optimal or even random-baseline expectations—suggesting CIB may be less pivotal than commonly assumed due to resource scarcity and poor strategic placement. This skepticism echoes older survey-based work: [[Keller2019-nk]]'s foundational South Korean NIS case found that even a well-resourced, human-staffed astroturfing campaign had only modest measurable impact, while [[Eady2023-xg]] showed IRA exposure in 2016 was dwarfed by domestic media exposure and bore no relationship to attitude change. [[Appel2026-qr]]'s large-scale Meta-collaboration study of 49 deceptive networks reinforces this: most reach came from organic reshares by non-network accounts, and correlations with downstream attitudes vanished after controlling for pre-exposure characteristics. Together these papers form a sober empirical counterweight to alarmist framings, insisting that detecting coordination is not the same as demonstrating causal influence—a distinction [[Simeone2025-vo]] complicates from the opposite direction by showing that deplatforming small numbers of *central* (not necessarily coordinated) accounts can produce disproportionate network-wide "ripple effects," independent of CIB.

## Case studies: platforms, actors, and national contexts

A large cluster of case studies stress-tests and extends the CLSB paradigm across platforms, issues, and regimes. On Facebook, [[Song2025-yh]] shows CLSB is ideologically agnostic, serving both anti-vaccine misinformation and credible institutional health communication depending on national context (UK safety-skepticism vs. US freedom-framing). [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] develops a "manufactured attention" typology spanning influence operations, media groups, political activists, and ad networks, arguing Meta's definition of CIB has narrowed from broad "false amplifiers" to only "adversarial threat actors," leaving much coordinated activity unaddressed. This concern about definitional narrowness and platform governance gaps is the direct throughline into the topic's flagship case studies: the "Zombie Internet" analysis in [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]], which proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category transcending false-content, tracing shared structural mechanisms across a pro-Putin propaganda network, gambling promotion, and poorly-moderated adult-content groups—all exploiting engagement-driven ranking and inconsistent moderation. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] deepens the gambling case specifically, documenting how generative AI produced a 13,000%+ post-volume surge post-ChatGPT and intensified visual persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, cultural localization) within Meta's paid/organic regulatory grey zone.

Beyond Meta platforms, the VERA-AI monitoring infrastructure surfaces in [[Rogers2026-cy]], which frames "post-truth spaces" as one of five categories of problematic information (alongside AINs, fake news engagement spaces, CIB, and participatory propaganda), demonstrated via Moldovan Facebook discourse ahead of the 2024 elections. National/regional case studies proliferate: [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] on pro-government Telegram coordination in Russia/Belarus; [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] on Russian/Chinese protest-repression dynamics, extending Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to "connective counteraction"; [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapting coordination detection to WhatsApp's closed ecosystem in Brazil, linking coordinated messaging to real-world mobilization around January 8, 2023; [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] on Serbia's SNS party, arguing its mimicry-based CIB evades detection longer than centralized bot farms; [[Kim2026-br]] on two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments; [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizing Philippine influence operations as political brokerage; [[Pante2025-pq]] critically re-examining claims of inter-state IO collaboration with more rigorous controls; and [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] and Bastos2025-ol providing organizational/visual forensics on the IRA and Twitter Moderation Research Consortium troll farms respectively. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] extends the framework to transnational movement infrastructure, showing CitizenGO's anti-gender network relies on centrally coordinated multilingual accounts alongside organic amplification. Historical-election studies—[[Slack1783507532-sl10]] on German 2021 advertising/coordination, [[Oprea2025-lf]] on Romanian 2024 hyperactive users—round out the electoral applications, while [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and its companion Italian-context studies trace how Alternative Influence Networks and legacy-media remediation amplified COVID-19 problematic information.

## Adjacent phenomena: extremism, automation, and the AI horizon

Several papers push outward from strict coordination detection into adjacent questions of platform vernacular, automation ambiguity, and AI-driven futures. [[Starbird2019-qv]] remains foundational here, reframing disinformation as *participatory collaborative work* rather than bot/troll dichotomies—a framing echoed in [[Graham2026-fb]]'s Reddit "botting" case, which dissolves the human/bot binary entirely, showing authenticity is culturally negotiated rather than technically fixed. Extremism case studies—[[Bailard2024-pj]] on Proud Boys framing-to-violence dynamics, [[Rothut2026-or]] on Querdenken's protest-facilitated mainstreaming, [[Karo2026-dn]] on jihadist TikTok evasion tactics, [[Rieder2026-pp]] on ambient Tate-space circulation, [[Askanius2026-de]] on Swedish far-right YouTube hybridization, [[Copland2025-em]] on Sky News Australia's network-propaganda strategy, [[Graham2025-gp]] on #IStandWithPutin infrastructure exploitation, [[Zhao2025-ny]] on hashtag hijacking—collectively show how coordination-adjacent phenomena (algorithmic amplification, vernacular camouflage, cross-platform circulation) often escape formal coordination signatures while still shaping discourse. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] bridges back to methodology by showing coordinated retweet networks amplifying contrarian science communicators are *not* bot-driven, reinforcing the coordination/automation orthogonality thesis. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extends agenda-building theory to treat bots as a genuine fourth communicator class. Finally, a forward-looking cluster—[[Schroeder2026-im]] on "malicious AI swarms," [[Orlando2025-ul]]'s generative-agent simulations showing LLM agents spontaneously reproduce IO coordination signatures, and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] on campaign persistence via event-appropriation—signals where this strand is heading: toward a coordination landscape increasingly populated by autonomous, adaptive, and synthetic actors that may outpace both detection methodology and platform governance frameworks built for an earlier, more human era of manipulation.
