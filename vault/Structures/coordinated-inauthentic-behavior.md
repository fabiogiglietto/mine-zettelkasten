# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## From Coordinated Link Sharing to a General Theory of Online Coordination

The methodological core of this strand begins with a simple empirical observation: disinformation researchers were too focused on content and known bad actors, and not enough on *behavior*. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] inaugurates "coordinated link sharing behavior" (CLSB) as an ecological alternative to veracity- and actor-centered approaches, showing that networks of pages, groups and profiles sharing the same URLs near-simultaneously are reliably associated with problematic domains during the 2018–19 Italian elections. This single methodological move — operationalizing Camille François's Actors-Behaviors-Content framework as an iterative detection cycle — becomes the DNA of an entire research program. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] formalizes the resulting tool, CooRnet, and [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] (MINE-FACTS) and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extend it into a live monitoring workflow for fact-checkers, demonstrating that continuously updated, actor-agnostic lists outperform static blocklists and can surface ideologically, financially, and even religiously motivated coordination (the Church of Almighty God case). [[Marino2023-9137f448]] then applies CLSB-derived actor lists to trace how an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster and legacy journalists jointly remediate covid-skeptic narratives, showing the method's payoff for understanding hybrid media dynamics, not just detection per se. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] uses the same coordination-detection logic to map CitizenGO's centrally administered, multilingual anti-gender network, treating coordinated link-sharing as evidence against purely "grassroots" self-presentation. The companion essay [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] steps back to theorize why big data and networked collective action structurally invite this kind of fuzzy-boundary manipulation in contemporary political communication.

This lineage is not static. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] (CooRTweet) generalizes the underlying definition — repeated, near-synchronous sharing of any uniquely identifiable object — to make the detection logic platform- and modality-agnostic, validated against the ground-truth NIS dataset of [[Keller2019-nk]]. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] push the CooRnet-descended workflow (via the VERA-AI project) into new empirical terrain: synthetic, AI-generated gambling-ad networks and a typology of "Deceptive Information Operations" that deliberately decouples manipulation from falsity, arguing that affordance exploitation and coordination — not misinformation per se — are the unifying structural signature across propaganda, gambling, and poorly moderated adult-content networks. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and [[Rogers2026-cy]] extend this same VERA-AI lineage toward conceptual housekeeping: the former shows that CIB-style coordination signatures cannot by themselves distinguish influence operations from legitimate media amplification, charting how Meta's own definition of CIB has narrowed over time to exclude much of what the tool still detects; the latter proposes "post-truth spaces" as a companion category for fact-checkers, situated alongside CIB, alternative influence networks, and participatory propaganda in a taxonomy of problematic information ecosystems.

## Competing and Complementary Detection Architectures

Parallel to this CooRnet lineage, a network-science tradition develops partly independently. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] reframes coordination as a continuum rather than a binary classification and demonstrates — crucially — that coordination and automation are orthogonal, a finding echoed throughout the corpus (e.g., [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] finds coordinated COVID-science amplifiers are *not* more bot-like than non-coordinated accounts). This orthogonality becomes a recurring theoretical anchor: coordination is a structural property, inauthenticity a separate normative one. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] systematizes the resulting methodological choices — monomodal versus multiplex, flattened versus layered — showing a fundamental trade-off between integration strength and detection inclusiveness, a problem that [[Iannucci2025-eg]] addresses with a time-aware multiplex collaboration model validated against 26 labeled datasets. [[Minici2024-tf]] pushes detection toward foundation-model generalization (IOHunter), combining language and graph representations to transfer across campaigns with minimal labeled data, while [[Gerard2025-br]] (CANE/t-CANE) reconceives cross-platform influence altogether, modeling users via latent narrative-cluster participation rather than interaction graphs, and identifying a tiny population of "bridge users" who seed most Truth Social–to–X narrative migration. [[Yang2025-iv]] attacks a specific vulnerability of the whole family of methods — their dependence on manipulable timing signals — by exploiting statistical regularities in sharing speed and frequency instead. The survey [[Mannocci2026-kc]] synthesizes this entire fractured landscape, proposing a unifying definition built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, and insisting that authenticity and harmfulness are orthogonal dimensions, so that coordination research should not collapse into a subfield of "bad actor" detection.

This methodological pluralism is matched by platform-specific adaptation. [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the video-first architecture of TikTok, where text-centric detection logics fail outright; [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist actors' own adversarial creativity on that platform (audio camouflage, emoji codes, bait-and-switch) in ways that mirror the detection-evasion arms race theorized elsewhere. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] moves detection onto Bluesky, an emergent decentralized platform, while Bastos2025-ya offers a retrospective on what is lost — methodologically and infrastructurally — now that Twitter's open APIs, which made so much of this research possible, no longer exist, a concern [[Bruns2019-nr]] had already diagnosed as the "APIcalypse," arguing that post-Cambridge-Analytica API lockdowns function less as privacy protection than as a mechanism for frustrating independent accountability research.

## Does Coordination Matter? Questioning Impact and Scale

A skeptical counter-current runs through the corpus, asking whether detected coordination is actually consequential. [[Grinberg2019-ua]]'s foundational finding — that 2016 fake-news engagement was extremely concentrated among a tiny, already-committed fringe, with mainstream media still dominating most people's exposure — anticipates a pattern that recurs throughout: [[Appel2026-qr]]'s analysis of 49 Meta-identified deceptive networks finds reach is similarly concentrated (three networks account for ~80% of exposure), that most exposure is indirect (via reshares by non-network accounts), and that associations with downstream attitudinal outcomes largely vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] formalize this skepticism graph-theoretically, showing that observed coordinated accounts in UK election retweet cascades exert influence far below optimal or even greedy-strategic benchmarks — closer to random placement than to deliberate strategic positioning — suggesting CIB may be less pivotal to diffusion than prevailing narratives assume. [[Keller2019-nk]], despite documenting an extraordinarily well-resourced, human-staffed South Korean NIS astroturfing operation with pervasive coordination signatures, likewise finds only modest measurable impact on Twitter discourse, framing this through a principal-agent lens: shirking, satisficing, and direct supervision needs leave detectable traces precisely because coordination is costly to manage.

Against this, [[Starbird2019-qv]] insists that the "bots and trolls" framing is itself the problem: disinformation campaigns are participatory *work*, built around a plausible "rational core" that organic crowds — unwitting agents, not just paid operatives — help sustain, making the orchestrated/organic boundary empirically blurry by design. [[Thiele2025-ol]] takes this critique further at the conceptual level, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" as a category conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and is poorly suited to attributing campaigns to their covert principals; the paper proposes a rational-choice typology linking scale, elaborateness, and disguise to a principal's resources and stakes. [[Graham2026-fb]]'s study of Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon radicalizes this line of thought entirely, showing that what looked like a bot invasion was in fact a human-driven, culturally embedded participatory performance — authenticity, the paper argues, is negotiated by communities rather than detectable as a technical property, a direct challenge to the premise that coordination signatures imply deception.

## Case Studies: Elections, Authoritarianism, and Platform Ecosystems

A large empirical cluster applies and stress-tests these methods across national contexts. In electoral settings: [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] on the 2021 German elections; [[Simeone2025-vo]], showing that Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced network-wide "ripple effects" exceeding mechanical node removal — and notably finding CIB was *not* the driver of the Arizona Election Review cluster's mobilization, implicating authentic human participation instead; [[Oprea2025-lf]], documenting hyperactive-user amplification on Romanian party pages during the 2024 European Parliament elections; [[Kansaon2025-id]], extending coordination detection into WhatsApp's closed ecosystem to trace Brazilian political mobilization culminating in the January 8 attacks; and [[Pante2025-pq]], which reassesses prior claims of inter-state collaboration using more rigorous control datasets. Authoritarian and quasi-authoritarian contexts receive sustained attention: [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] both examine Russian/Chinese digital repression, the latter extending Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to "connective counteraction," finding pro-Navalny networks centralized around key brokers while pro-government astroturfing is more modular and preemptive; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] maps coordinated amplification among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus; [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues Serbia's SNS deploys a subtler, grassroots-mimicking form of digital authoritarianism that evades standard detection longer than centralized bot farms; and [[Kim2026-br]] applies explainable ML to two decades of Korean troll comments, finding that moral condemnation — not direct praise of foreign narratives — is the dominant and most effective rhetorical strategy.

Platform- and actor-specific studies further diversify the picture: [[Song2025-yh]] shows CLSB is ideologically agnostic, serving both anti-vaccine misinformation and credible public-health messaging depending on national context; [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's two-pronged strategy of domestic Facebook content versus internationally viral YouTube conspiracism; [[Askanius2026-de]] maps a hybridized, commercially monetized Swedish far-right YouTube network; [[Knupfer2025-vt]] theorizes a "logic of connective faction" linking intra-party radicalization (via CRT) to hyper-partisan media ecosystems; [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses collective-action framing theory and Granger causality to connect Proud Boys' online rhetoric to offline violence; and [[Rothut2026-or]] documents how the Querdenken protest movement served as a conduit mainstreaming German far-right and conspiracist networks. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a strategic opinion-contest tactic involving bots and narrative substitution, while [[Slack1783507463-sl24]] cautions that superficially similar firestorms (a pro-Vietnam attack on China's Facebook "digital embassy") can mask entirely distinct sociological dynamics — coordinated political protest rather than marketing crisis.

## The Organizational and Economic Substrate of Manipulation

A smaller but important cluster insists that information operations be understood organizationally, not just behaviorally. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] (Poliakoff2026-fa) uses OSINT on leaked employee CVs to reconstruct the Internet Research Agency as a media-labor-market actor — young, inexperienced, Russian-educated workers embedded in a five-level hierarchy rather than ideologically driven operatives — complicating the "troll factory as alien machine" image. [[Gaw2025-ru]] extends this institutional lens to the 2022 Philippine elections, reconceiving influence operations as a form of political brokerage mediating between political clients and digital platforms. Bastos2025-ol applies computer vision to the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium dataset to ask whether troll farms exhibit identifiable visual signatures by country of origin, adding a new attributional modality to text- and network-based approaches.

## Theorizing Harm, Intent, and the Infrastructure of Truth

Several papers work at a more conceptual register, asking what, exactly, makes coordination normatively troubling. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as a structural feature of engagement-driven platform business models rather than an aberration, tracing how platforms shifted from non-intervention to CIB-style behavioral moderation during COVID and the 2020 election before retreating under political backlash. [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Tuters2026-vo]] both develop infrastructural accounts of propaganda: the former shows how #IStandWithPutin exploits, rather than evades, the very platform infrastructures built to support credible information; the latter traces the "conspiricization" of WEF language around the Great Reset, arguing that platform grammars produce conspiratorial alignment through patterned adjacency before any coherent narrative exists — a "high-tech paranoid style" in which correlation is read as elite coordination. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] complements this with the concept of "networked exposure," arguing that even ordinary, true content becomes politically weaponized once embedded in partisan circulation networks. [[Gaber2022-bk]] examines a cognate phenomenon in elite political communication — "strategic lying" as an intensification of PR spin in which fact-checking rebuttals paradoxically amplify the original falsehood. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] interrogate measurement itself: the former shows mainstream news is routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives, a phenomenon invisible to domain-list approaches; the latter finds that even a crowdsourced, cross-partisan system (Community Notes) flags Republican posts far more than Democratic ones, undermining claims that fact-checker bias explains partisan asymmetries in misinformation sanctioning.

## Adjacent Harms and the Generative-AI Horizon

Finally, the strand opens onto emergent concerns at its frontier. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that the *source* of misinformation (influencer vs. ordinary user) — not just coordination or content — structurally shapes toxic audience reactions, adding a marketing-communication inflection to the broader coordination literature. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extends agenda-building theory to treat bots as a genuine fourth category of political communicator. Most consequentially, a cluster of 2025–26 papers confronts the collision between this entire detection apparatus and generative AI. [[Orlando2025-ul]] simulates LLM agents in information-operation roles, finding that mere mutual awareness among synthetic agents — without explicit deliberation — produces coordination nearly as dense and synchronized as fully orchestrated campaigns, a troubling proof of concept for autonomous astroturfing. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a policy forum on "malicious AI swarms," arguing that LLM-driven multi-agent systems constitute a qualitative escalation beyond prior botnets, capable of manufacturing synthetic consensus and poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"). [[Crichton2026-kn]] documents a parallel empirical pipeline already in use by far-right propagandists, who harvest extremist imagery and regenerate it through commercial image models that sanitize explicit markers while preserving underlying messaging. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], more briefly, observes that manipulation campaigns persist by adaptively appropriating emerging events — a structural resilience that the generative-AI turn threatens to industrialize. Together, these final papers mark the point where the CooRnet-descended tradition of detecting coordination in *historical* platform data confronts a future in which the coordinating agents may no longer be human at all, closing the loop on the strand's founding insight: that disinformation research must track *behavior and structure*, not just content, because the actors producing that content are becoming ever harder to see.
