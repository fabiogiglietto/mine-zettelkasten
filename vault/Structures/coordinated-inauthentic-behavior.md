# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The CooRnet lineage: from link-sharing to a research program

The topic's methodological backbone is the Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) approach first operationalized by [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframed disinformation research away from content-veracity or bad-actor classification and toward detecting *collective action*: networks of pages, groups, and profiles sharing identical URLs in tight, repeated, near-synchronous bursts. That paper's Italian election case established the now-canonical findings that coordinated networks disproportionately amplify problematic domains and that "politicalness" inversely correlates with source diversity — a pattern echoed across nearly every downstream CLSB study in this collection. The CooRnet package this work spawned becomes the connective tissue of the corpus: [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] extends it into a content-agnostic, near-real-time fact-checking triage tool for COVID-19 misinformation, tracing an Italian covid-skeptic cluster's genealogy back into pre-existing League/M5S coordinated networks and cataloguing evasion tactics (link-laundering, first-comment link-hiding, image-macro cross-posting) that recur, almost identically, in later Facebook studies. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] operationalizes the natural next problem — that static lists of coordinated accounts decay — with an iterative monitoring workflow applied to the 2022 Italian snap election, surfacing hundreds of new accounts and three structurally distinct case studies (a hyperpartisan M5S echo chamber, a religious-page clickbait economy, and a covert Church of Almighty God recruitment network) that together demonstrate CLSB's genericity across motivations. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and [[Song2025-yh]] apply the same lineage to substantive questions: the former shows how an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster and legacy-media remediation jointly propel Italian COVID-19 problematic content, the latter compares UK/US anti- and pro-vaccine CLSB networks to argue that coordination is ideologically agnostic, serving both misinformation and credible public-health messaging depending on who wields it. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] push the CLSB detection pipeline into new, non-electoral commercial and hybrid domains — AI-generated gambling promotion exploiting the ad/organic regulatory gap, and a broader "Deceptive Information Operations" typology spanning pro-Putin propaganda, gambling, and poorly moderated adult-content groups — arguing that a shared structural logic (affordance exploitation plus simulated coordination) unites operations that differ wildly in motive and content veracity. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] closes an important reflexive loop: applying CooRnet-style detection broadly, it finds that "manufactured attention" signatures are structurally indistinguishable between influence operations and ordinary media outlets, and traces how Meta's own definition of CIB has narrowed from "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors," letting much coordinated activity remain on-platform by definitional fiat. [[Rogers2026-cy]] extends this critical, taxonomic project by situating post-truth spaces alongside CIB, alternative influence networks, and participatory propaganda as one node in a broader ecology of problematic-information structures, useful to fact-checkers as leads rather than verdicts.

## Generalizing, stress-testing, and re-engineering detection

A second cluster interrogates and extends the CooRnet paradigm's technical assumptions. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]'s CooRTweet generalizes coordination detection to any uniquely identifiable shared object across platforms and modalities, validated against the South Korean NIS dataset, explicitly responding to the platform- and content-type constraints of CooRnet and the Coordination Network Toolkit. [[Mannocci2025-ig]] and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] both push on multimodality: the former systematically compares five ways of integrating retweet, reply, mention, hashtag, and URL co-action signals, finding an inescapable trade-off between integration strength and detection inclusiveness, with multiplex community detection as the best-balanced compromise; the latter formalizes a temporally-weighted multiplex model that outperforms single-modality and time-agnostic baselines across 26 benchmark datasets, arguing that both temporal proximity and repetition are jointly necessary signatures of deliberate coordination. [[Yang2025-iv]] attacks the timing-manipulability problem directly, proposing statistical regularities in sharing speed and frequency as more evasion-resistant signals than raw timestamps. [[Minici2024-tf]] moves detection into the foundation-model era with IOHunter, combining language models and graph neural networks to generalize across heterogeneous, even out-of-distribution, state-sponsored campaigns. Platform-specific adaptations proliferate: [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the video-first architecture of TikTok, arguing text-centric coordination detection does not transfer; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] pushes toward real-time bot-coordination detection on the decentralized platform Bluesky; [[Gerard2025-br]] proposes Cluster Affiliation Network Embedding (CANE/t-CANE) as a platform-agnostic alternative to interaction-based graphs, identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed roughly 70% of narratives migrating between Truth Social and X during the 2024 US election. Two papers turn reflexively skeptical: [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models the theoretical influence ceiling of coordinated nodes in UK 2019 election retweet cascades and finds observed coordinated accounts achieve influence far below optimal or even random-matched benchmarks — a sobering counterpoint to detection-triumphalist narratives — while [[Pante2025-pq]] reassesses prior claims of inter-state CIB collaboration on X/Twitter and finds they weaken substantially once modern multi-signal methods and proper control datasets are applied. [[Thiele2025-ol]] supplies the conceptual scaffolding these empirical efforts often lack: a rational-choice "attribution problem" framework and eight-type typology for inferring covert campaign principals from observable scale, elaborateness, and disguise, explicitly critiquing Meta's own CIB concept as conflating inauthenticity with intent.

## Elections as the proving ground

Electoral contexts are where CIB/CLSB methodology meets its highest-stakes applications, and this corpus spans a remarkable geographic range. In the US, [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] leverage unprecedented Meta-provided exposure data on 49 "deceptive online networks" during the 2020 election, showing reach is extremely concentrated, driven mostly by reshares from unaffiliated accounts, and that once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled, associations with downstream political outcomes largely evaporate — a methodologically cautious counterweight to alarmist influence-operation narratives. [[Kim2026-wg]] complicates this caution by documenting geo-racially targeted voter-suppression advertising (including IRA-attributed ads) in 2016 and linking exposure to verified turnout declines, arguing average-treatment-effect designs mask real harms concentrated in targeted subpopulations. Brazil is a recurring site: [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] pioneers volunteer browser-plugin auditing of irregular electoral ads in 2018, while [[Kansaon2025-id]] extends coordination detection into WhatsApp's closed ecosystem, tracing how coordinated accounts disseminated misinformation and helped mobilize real-world post-election protests, including the January 8 events. Europe contributes [[Oprea2025-lf]] (hyperactive-user amplification on Romanian party pages during 2024 EP elections), [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] (German 2021 federal election advertising and coordination), and the CooRTweet validation case in [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] on the same election. [[Simeone2025-vo]] examines a US deplatforming intervention (Arizona Election Review accounts) and — notably — finds coordinated inauthentic behavior was *not* the driver of that network's mobilization, with only ten accounts meeting coordination thresholds, suggesting network-structural interventions can matter independent of CIB per se. Beyond established democracies, [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS party mimicking authentic grassroots support in ways that evade standard bot-farm detection, and [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceptualizes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage — an intermediary economic infrastructure rather than an isolated propaganda phenomenon — based on interviews with IO practitioners themselves.

## State-backed operations and authoritarian information ecosystems

A distinct cluster examines coordination as an instrument of state power. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] offers rare organizational grounding via 350 leaked CVs, recasting the St Petersburg IRA as a labor-market actor embedded in Russia's media economy — recruited around the Crimea annexation, not primarily the 2016 US election — complicating simplistic "troll farm" narratives. Bastos2025-ol pursues attribution through an entirely different signal: visual self-presentation in profile imagery across the Twitter Moderation Research Consortium's terabyte-scale removed-operations dataset. [[Kim2026-br]] applies two decades of longitudinal, explainable ML to Korean news comments, finding suspected state-linked trolls rely on morally condemning rhetoric rather than overt praise — a subtler manipulation logic than typical astroturfing models assume. Telegram and Twitter figure prominently in post-Soviet contexts: [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] maps pro-government Telegram coordination in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] contrasts centralized pro-Navalny mobilization with more modular, brokered pro-government counter-mobilization, extending Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to "connective counteraction"; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] detects large-scale coordinated activity surrounding China's 2022 COVID-19 protests, framing social media as dual-use infrastructure for both mobilization and repression. [[Graham2025-gp]] rounds out this cluster with a case study of #IStandWithPutin, arguing propaganda succeeds not by evading platforms' truth-verification infrastructures but by parasitically exploiting them.

## Movements, mainstreaming, and the far right

Several papers trace how coordinated online activity intersects with offline extremist mobilization and ideological mainstreaming. [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses Granger causality on 500,000 Proud Boys Telegram messages to show diagnostic and motivational collective-action framing — not explicit calls-to-action — predict subsequent offline violence, in a reciprocal online-offline cycle. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how Germany's Querdenken movement became structurally entangled with far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks, with anti-elite framing (not explicit ideology) as the connective bridge. [[Askanius2026-de]] documents a parallel hybridization on Swedish far-right YouTube, where influencer microcelebrity practices fuse with political propaganda and coordinated cross-promotion within a self-named "Tube Family." [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows CitizenGO's anti-gender transnational network relies on centrally coordinated multilingual accounts posing as grassroots advocacy, deliberately imitating platforms like Change.org for legitimacy. [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a node of transnational "network propaganda," with distinct domestic (Facebook) and international (YouTube) strategies. [[Karo2026-dn]] closes this thematic arc with an ethnographic account of jihadist "everyday extremism" on TikTok, where evasion tactics (audio camouflage, emoji codes, bait-and-switch) mirror adversarial creativity documented elsewhere in far-right and anti-vaccine communities — underscoring that platform-vernacular evasion is ideology-agnostic.

## Automation, bots, and the LLM frontier

A forward-looking cluster asks how generative AI reshapes coordination itself. [[Schroeder2026-im]] offers a policy-forum argument that LLM-driven multi-agent "swarms" constitute a qualitative escalation beyond botnet-era manipulation, threatening democracy via manufactured consensus and training-data poisoning. [[Orlando2025-ul]] operationalizes this concern empirically, using generative agent-based modeling to show that mere *awareness* of teammates among LLM agents — without explicit deliberation — produces coordination nearly as strong as full collective decision-making, a troubling sufficiency result for platform governance. [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] examine more conventional bot roles: hashtag hijacking as a strategic attention-capture tactic, and social bots as "fourth" agenda-builders that most effectively shift negative sentiment in the 2022 Ohio midterms. [[Graham2026-fb]] provides a valuable counter-note, showing that Reddit's "Anus_Fungi" phenomenon — widely perceived as bot invasion — was mostly human-driven imitation, arguing the human/bot binary itself is analytically unstable and that authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical fact — a claim that resonates with [[Rogers2025-sl8f]]'s finding that coordination signatures alone cannot distinguish inauthentic from authentic behavior.

## Adjacent information ecosystems and open questions

A final set of papers extends coordination-adjacent methodology into broader misinformation ecology. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces how a small, non-automated but coordinated contrarian retweet network amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts in COVID-19 science communication, with news coverage typically *following* rather than leading superspreader activity. [[Goel2025-iq]] shows that domain-level misinformation metrics miss a larger phenomenon — mainstream articles co-shared with fake news to support misleading narratives — a finding that complements CLSB's behavioral, rather than source-based, orientation. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] gestures toward the temporal adaptability of manipulation campaigns, arguing they persist by appropriating emerging events rather than remaining static — a claim that threads through nearly every case study in this collection, from Giglietto's evolving Italian covid-skeptic clusters to CitizenGO's decade-long mainstreaming project. Together, these papers suggest that the CooRnet/CLSB tradition, having matured into a robust and diversifying detection methodology, is now pressing outward on two fronts simultaneously: toward more rigorous, generalizable, and reflexively skeptical measurement (is coordination truly consequential, and for whom?), and toward an expanding empirical frontier — video platforms, encrypted messaging, decentralized networks, and now autonomous AI agents — where the very boundary between authentic and inauthentic collective action is increasingly contested terrain.
