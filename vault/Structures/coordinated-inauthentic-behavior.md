# Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior

## The CooRnet lineage: from link-sharing to a general theory of coordination

This line of work begins with a methodological wager: that disinformation research had over-invested in classifying content as true or false and in blacklisting bad actors, and under-invested in detecting the *collective action* that makes problematic content travel. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] makes this case explicitly, introducing coordinated link sharing behavior (CLSB) as a way of finding networks of pages, groups and profiles that post identical URLs in suspiciously tight time windows, and showing that such networks disproportionately carry problematic domains. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies the broader diagnosis—blurred organizational boundaries, opaque micro-targeting, and an attention economy that rewards amplification—that motivates treating coordination, rather than content or actor identity, as the primary unit of analysis. The CooRnet tool that operationalizes CLSB is formally presented in [[Slack1783507719-slc1]], which folds detection into François's Actors-Behaviors-Content cycle, and is stress-tested in applied settings by [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] (the MINE-FACTS fact-checking pipeline for Italian COVID-19 discourse) and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which turns the static list of known coordinated accounts into a continuously updated monitoring workflow—directly anticipating the always-on logic of the VERA-AI Alert system. [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] completes this methodological arc by generalizing CLSB into CooRTweet, an R package that abstracts coordination down to "repeated near-synchronous sharing of any uniquely identifiable object," making the method platform- and modality-agnostic at a moment when API volatility was making platform-specific tools obsolete.

## What is coordination, and when is it a problem?

A parallel strand interrogates the concept itself. [[Starbird2019-qv]] reframes disinformation as *collaborative work*, arguing that bots-and-trolls framings obscure the participatory, often-authentic crowds that sustain information operations—a claim [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] operationalizes by showing coordination and automation are empirically orthogonal and that coordination exists on a continuum rather than a binary. This conceptual unease culminates in two recent systematizing efforts: [[Mannocci2026-kc]], a survey that proposes actors–actions–intent as the invariant core of coordination and insists that authenticity and harm are independent axes (coordination can be authentic-but-harmful or inauthentic-but-harmless), and [[Thiele2025-ol]], which attacks the platform-derived "coordinated inauthentic behavior" label for conflating inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposes a rational-choice typology oriented toward attribution rather than detection. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] applies CLSB-style detection to show empirically that identical coordination *signatures* can belong to influence operations, legitimate media outlets, gambling ad networks, or grassroots meme communities alike—vindicating the view that coordination detection is necessary but not sufficient, and that Meta's own definition of CIB has quietly narrowed to exclude much of what it once covered. [[Graham2026-fb]] pushes this further still, using Reddit's "botting" culture to argue that the human/bot and authentic/inauthentic binaries are themselves culturally negotiated rather than technical facts.

## Beyond CLSB: temporal, multiplex, and foundation-model detection

A dense methodological frontier has grown up around improving on single-signal, time-window detection. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that coordination is inherently multimodal and temporal: the former introduces exponentially time-decayed, multiplex collaboration networks that beat single-modality baselines across 26 benchmark datasets, while the latter systematically compares five ways of integrating modalities and finds multiplex community detection the best-balanced option against naive "flattening." [[Yang2025-iv]] attacks the timing-manipulability problem directly, proposing speed-and-frequency regularities as more evasion-resistant signals for Facebook link-sharing. [[Minici2024-tf]] moves toward graph foundation models (IOHunter), fusing language and graph-neural signals to generalize across six state-sponsored campaigns, while [[Gerard2025-br]] reconceives cross-platform influence structurally, via shared narrative-cluster embeddings (CANE/t-CANE) rather than interaction traces, identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed most Truth Social–to–X narrative migration. [[Luceri2025-tr]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extend detection into under-studied terrain—TikTok's video-first ecosystem and Bluesky's decentralized, real-time setting—signaling the field's awareness that text/retweet-centric tools do not automatically transfer to new platforms.

## Does coordination actually move the needle?

A skeptical counter-current, largely independent of the CooRnet tradition but essential to calibrating its claims, asks whether detected coordination translates into meaningful influence. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and its companion [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] formalize influence as a graph-theoretic optimization problem and find that real coordinated accounts in UK election cascades achieve far less influence than optimal or even randomly-placed benchmarks would predict—coordination detected is not coordination that matters. This chimes with the individual-level survey evidence of [[Eady2023-xg]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]], both showing that exposure to Russian IRA/fake-news content in 2016 was extraordinarily concentrated among already-committed partisans and dwarfed by exposure to mainstream sources, with no measurable attitudinal effect; [[Appel2026-qr]] extends this skepticism to 2020, showing that most exposure to deceptive networks came via organic reshares rather than direct network activity, and that naive correlational associations with downstream beliefs vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Pante2025-pq]] turns the same scrutiny on claims of inter-state IO collaboration, arguing prior findings do not survive modern multi-signal detection with proper control datasets. [[Guess2021-ym]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] round out this current with large-scale Facebook descriptive work showing low-credibility content is a small, though non-trivial and ideologically skewed, share of the overall news ecosystem. Together these papers function as a check on over-claiming: coordination is detectable, but its causal weight in shaping belief and behavior remains an open, often disappointing, empirical question—one the CLSB tradition must keep answering rather than assuming.

## vera.ai WP4 in the field: applied investigations toward VERA-AI Alert

The WP4 case studies show CLSB/CooRnet-style detection maturing into a diagnostic instrument for diverse harms, each pushing the typology that will feed into the always-on VERA-AI Alert system. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] tracks how generative AI has intensified and industrialized coordinated gambling promotion on Facebook, documenting a post-ChatGPT explosion in visual persuasion content and locating the vulnerability in the regulatory gap between paid and organic content. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes across three structurally similar but motivationally distinct Facebook operations (pro-Putin propaganda, gambling promotion, hijacked groups) under the "Deceptive Information Operations" / "Zombie Internet" framework, arguing that affordance-exploitation and simulated coordination—not falsity—are the unifying diagnostic. [[Rogers2026-cy]] develops a complementary cartography of "post-truth spaces" for fact-checkers, applied to Moldovan Facebook ahead of the 2024 elections, showing that the most influential nodes are often "borderline" bridges rather than the post-truth clusters themselves. Earlier applied work in the same lineage—[[Marino2023-9137f448]] on Italian "Alternative Influence Networks" remediating anti-vaccine talking points through legacy media, [[Righetti2025-slf9]] on CitizenGO's centrally coordinated transnational anti-gender network, [[Song2025-yh]] comparing pro- and anti-vaccine CLSB in the UK and US, and the more compact [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] and [[Slack1783507463-sl24]]—demonstrates the method's range, from political advertising audits to reinterpreting "online firestorms" as coordinated political protest rather than brand crises.

## Comparative political contexts: astroturfing and digital authoritarianism

A large cluster of papers applies coordination detection (often explicitly building on or paralleling CooRnet) across authoritarian and electoral contexts worldwide, establishing the ground-truth cases against which newer methods are validated. [[Keller2019-nk]] remains foundational here, using leaked South Korean NIS account lists to show that coordination signatures—not bot-like individual traits—best distinguish astroturf campaigns, a dataset later re-validated by [[Righetti2025-sl2a]]. [[Kim2026-br]] extends this to two decades of Naver News comments, finding condemnation rhetoric, not overt boosterism, is the dominant and most effective troll strategy. Russian and post-Soviet contexts recur throughout: [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] models pro-Navalny mobilization and pro-government counter-coordination as a dynamic, reactive cycle; [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] maps pro-government Telegram amplification in Russia and Belarus; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] applies coordination detection to the 2022 China COVID protests, showing dual-use infrastructure for both mobilization and repression. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues Serbia's SNS party exemplifies a newer, harder-to-detect, decentralized mimicry of grassroots support that eludes standard detection. Elsewhere, [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts rapid-spread-network detection to Brazilian WhatsApp, linking coordinated messaging to real-world mobilization around January 8; [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes Philippine influence operations as political brokerage rather than discrete campaigns; [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that deplatforming the Arizona "audit" community produced network-wide ripple effects not attributable to CIB; [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Askanius2026-de]] trace how Sky News Australia and Swedish far-right YouTubers hybridize coordinated amplification with commercial influencer logics; and the organizational ethnographies of [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] (the St Petersburg IRA's labor-market structure) and Bastos2025-ol (visual self-presentation across state-linked troll farms) push the field toward understanding the human and institutional infrastructure behind coordination, not just its network traces.

## Platform governance, transparency, and the limits of enforcement

[[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Oprea2025-lf]] examine the regulatory and platform-policy environment coordination detection is meant to inform: the former audits YouTube's inconsistent application of authoritative-source labeling during the 2024 EU elections, the latter documents hyperactive-user amplification on Romanian party pages that Meta's own Community Standards nominally prohibit but does not enforce. Both reinforce [[Thiele2025-ol]]'s and [[Rogers2025-sl8f]]'s point that detection capability has outpaced governance capacity.

## Emerging frontiers: generative AI and agentic coordination

Two forward-looking papers anticipate the next phase of the phenomenon this whole topic area has been built to detect. [[Orlando2025-ul]] shows, via generative agent-based modeling, that LLM agents spontaneously reproduce the coordination signatures (density, narrative homogeneity, synchronized amplification) that CLSB-style tools are designed to catch—even under mere "teammate awareness" rather than explicit top-down control. [[Schroeder2026-im]] extrapolates this into a policy warning about "malicious AI swarms" capable of persistent, adaptive, cross-platform manufactured consensus, arguing that detection-based defenses alone cannot keep pace and that provenance infrastructure and incentive-based governance are needed—directly motivating the real-time monitoring ambitions of systems like VERA-AI Alert.

## Adjacent currents: framing, mainstreaming, and the wider misinformation ecosystem

A final set of papers sits at the edges of the coordination literature proper but supplies essential context for interpreting what coordinated activity *does*. Work on collective-action framing and mainstreaming—[[Bailard2024-pj]] on Proud Boys online-offline cycles, [[Rothut2026-or]] on how Querdenken protest communication routed sympathizers toward far-right networks, [[Tuters2026-vo]]'s account of the Great Reset as infrastructural "conspiricization"—shows that coordination is often only one ingredient in broader discursive mainstreaming processes. [[Karo2026-dn]] documents adversarial creativity (audio camouflage, emoji codes) that coordination detectors must contend with on video platforms. Science-communication and narrative-diffusion studies—[[Efstratiou2026-ij]] on contrarian coordination amplifying anti-consensus COVID experts, [[Goel2025-iq]] on mainstream articles repurposed into misleading narratives, [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame account of election-fraud rumoring, [[Zhao2025-ny]] on hashtag hijacking, [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] on bots as agenda-builders, and [[Graham2025-gp]] on propaganda exploiting the "infrastructure of truth"—collectively show why content-only or actor-only approaches keep failing, reinforcing from the outside the behavioral, coordination-centered methodology this entire research program was built to establish. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]], on the adaptive persistence of manipulation campaigns across emerging events, closes the loop back to the central argument: coordination is not a static signature to be listed once, but a moving target that monitoring infrastructure—from CooRnet to CooRTweet to VERA-AI Alert—must track continuously.
