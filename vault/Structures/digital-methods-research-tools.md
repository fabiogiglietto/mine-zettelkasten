# Digital Research Methods & Tools

## From CooRnet to CooRTweet: formalizing coordinated behavior detection

The anchor of this topic is a lineage of open-source tools built to operationalize a slippery concept: coordination. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] introduces CooRnet, the R-based implementation of Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) detection, embedding it within Francois's Actors-Behaviors-Content framework as an iterative cycle in which harmful content seeds detection of coordinated actors, whose subsequent output seeds the next round. Applied to COVID-19 disinformation in Italy, CooRnet demonstrates both the payoff and the platform-specificity of this approach — it was built for Facebook's link-sharing ecology and its CrowdTangle-dependent data supply.

[[Righetti2025-sl2a]] represents the methodological maturation of this lineage: CooRTweet generalizes CLSB into a platform- and modality-agnostic definition — repeated near-synchronous sharing of any uniquely identifiable object by a stable set of accounts — explicitly positioning itself against CooRnet's and other tools' constraints to single platforms or content types. Validated against the 86-million-tweet South Korean NIS dataset, CooRTweet recovers 85–92% of known coordinated accounts while introducing flexible tagging rather than rigid thresholds, an explicit acknowledgment that coordination is a matter of degree rather than a binary label. This move toward abstraction is itself a response to platform volatility: a generalized architecture is more likely to survive the next API shutdown than a tool wedded to one platform's affordances.

## Extending and stress-testing the CLSB paradigm

Around this core, a cluster of papers extends, critiques, and formalizes coordination detection as a research program in its own right. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] offers the field's most systematic reconciliation of fragmented platform and academic definitions, proposing actors–actions–intent as the constitutive components and authenticity/harmfulness/orchestration/time-variance as orthogonal dimensions — a conceptual scaffold that retroactively contextualizes CooRnet and CooRTweet as particular operationalizations of a much broader design space. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] push on the same design space from the network-science side, showing that temporal weighting and multiplex (rather than flattened) representations of multiple coordination modalities materially improve detection robustness and reveal trade-offs between integration strength and inclusiveness — concerns directly relevant to how tools like CooRTweet might be extended to multi-modal cases.

Other papers test the CLSB/CIB paradigm against evasion and against platforms it wasn't built for. [[Yang2025-iv]] proposes detecting Facebook coordination via statistical regularities in sharing speed/frequency rather than timing alone, explicitly responding to the manipulability of timing-based signals that CooRnet-style tools rely on. [[Luceri2025-tr]] confronts the video-first architecture of TikTok, arguing that text-centric coordination methods do not transfer cleanly. Bastos2025-ol adds a visual dimension entirely absent from link- or text-based coordination detection, probing whether troll-farm profile imagery carries attributable signatures. [[Pante2025-pq]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] apply these detection frameworks to substantive cases — inter-state information operations and the 2022 China COVID protests — with the former explicitly using rigor and control datasets to interrogate whether earlier claims of coordination survive more careful methodology, a useful corrective note for a field prone to overreading synchronicity as conspiracy. [[Bruns2025-fz]] steps back from the coordination-detection paradigm altogether to propose "practice mapping" via vector embeddings as an alternative to the "hairball" problem of conventional multimodal network visualization, suggesting the methodological conversation extends beyond coordination per se into how multimodal social media interaction is represented at all.

## The post-API crisis as the condition of possibility for these tools

The urgency and design choices behind CooRnet and CooRTweet cannot be separated from the collapsing data-access infrastructure that this whole topic sits atop. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya document, from complementary angles, the scale of scholarship enabled by Twitter's APIs and the abrupt decline following the 2023 pricing changes. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this history across platforms — from "Wild West" openness through academic cooperation to today's pay-to-play and DSA-mediated regimes — arguing that access is permanently contingent on platform decisions rather than a stable research commons. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting landscape of APIs and tools directly in the DSA era, the regulatory backdrop against which tools like CooRTweet's platform-agnostic design becomes a pragmatic necessity rather than mere elegance.

Several papers document the concrete costs of relying on platform-provided data infrastructures even where access nominally persists. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows how an ostensibly neutral anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset introduces a platform-wide algorithmic artifact that can overwhelm the substantive signals researchers seek — a cautionary tale directly relevant to any CLSB-style analysis built on CrowdTangle-derived data. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] extend this critique to the "clean room" successor regimes (Meta Content Library, TDM Studio), arguing that these environments actively reshape what can be seen, asked, and concluded, disadvantaging non-programming and Majority World researchers alike. [[Rieder2025-ju]] performs an analogous audit of YouTube's search API, finding a severe temporal decay in retrievability that undermines the very possibility of historical, DSA-relevant research. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds constructively to this same "post-API" scarcity by building TubeStats/TokStats, open dashboards providing genuinely random samples of YouTube and TikTok content as an alternative to popularity-biased opportunistic samples — itself a tool-building intervention in the spirit of CooRnet/CooRTweet, aimed at denominator and representativeness problems rather than coordination per se.

## Tools and workflows beyond coordination

Adjacent to the CLSB lineage, the topic gathers a wider set of open methodological contributions addressing other steps in the digital-methods pipeline. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] develop human-in-the-loop and VLLM-based pipelines for, respectively, qualitative interview coding and connotative image clustering, both foregrounding interpretability as a design requirement rather than an afterthought. [[Fan2025-ut]] proposes linear concept erasure as a lightweight, broadly applicable preprocessing step for deconfounding text embeddings across sources and languages — a plug-in improvement relevant to any embedding-based workflow, including the semantic clustering underlying tools like CooRTweet's content-matching. [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extend the toolkit into fine-tuned LLMs for event classification and paired-comparison scaling of open-ended survey text, both explicitly pitched at accessibility for resource-constrained researchers. [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Fan2026-af]] push methodology toward underused data dimensions — visual-first discourse analysis and hyper-longitudinal user-sequence mining — arguing that dominant text-centric and cross-sectional conventions systematically misrepresent their objects of study. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] scrutinize the validity of two workhorse collection techniques, web scraping and Facebook ad-based recruitment, showing that seemingly innocuous methodological choices (ex-situ collection, ad-targeting granularity) introduce systematic, not merely random, distortions. [[Balluff2026-ev]] illustrates one such workflow (named-entity co-occurrence networks) applied substantively to alternative-media comparison, showing how the broader toolkit developed elsewhere in this cluster gets deployed on a concrete empirical question.

## The costs and stakes of building open infrastructure

Finally, two papers frame the human and legal costs of sustaining this open-methods enterprise. [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling effects, legal exposure, and emotional toll experienced by researchers — including social computing scholars doing exactly the kind of scraping- and API-dependent work this topic depends on — under overbroad computer-crime statutes, arguing that legal risk has intensified for social-media research since 2020. [[Ahuja2025-ku]], though centered on dark-pattern regulation rather than research tooling per se, models a parallel "law-to-design" translation exercise relevant to how researchers and regulators alike must operationalize legal categories (autonomy, deception, manipulation) into auditable technical criteria — a reasoning move structurally similar to what CooRnet and CooRTweet perform in translating "coordination" into computable definitions. Together, these papers make clear that developing and disseminating tools like CooRnet and CooRTweet is not merely a technical achievement but an ongoing act of infrastructural and even legal risk-bearing, undertaken in a landscape where platforms, regulators, and researchers are in continuous, unstable negotiation over what can be measured, seen, and known.
