# Computational & LLM-Assisted Content Analysis

## The core tension: scale versus validity

Nearly every paper filed here orbits a single methodological problem: large language models and embeddings make it possible to code political and media content at a scale no human team could match, but this scale is purchased against uncertain validity. [[Balluff2026-if]] states the trade-off most bluntly, arguing that the field's uptake of LLMs for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulation has outrun critical reflection on reproducibility, environmental cost, and corporate dependency, and calling for a "least resource-intensive method" ethic. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys the same terrain from the technical side, organizing LLM-based annotation and synthesis around generation, assessment, and downstream use. Between these two poles sits most of the topic's empirical work: attempts to show, task by task, where LLM annotation holds up and where it breaks down.

## Validating LLMs as annotators and coders

A cluster of studies treats LLM-based coding as a measurement problem requiring its own validation logic. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] documents a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline for Italian political Facebook content—fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, LLM-generated cluster labels—and argues that each stage needs bespoke validation because expert (not crowdsourced) coders are now required to catch subtleties LLMs may miss. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] extends this empirically, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large outperforms an Italian BERT model (UmBERTo) for clustering political news, with GPT-4o-mini serving as a cheap, fine-tunable quality-rater. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Ober2026-vd]] show hybrid human–LLM workflows working productively outside English: GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment coding on a decade of Norwegian Facebook posts, and a topic-modeling-plus-LLM-labeling pipeline that refines a human codebook on US teacher interviews. [[Meher2025-qb]] pushes further into fine-tuning, adapting Llama 3.1 via QLoRA for conflict-event classification and showing that domain adaptation on consumer hardware can dramatically outperform zero-shot prompting, especially for rare categories.

Several papers interrogate *where* LLM annotation fails. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that demographic "bias" in LLM annotation is dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (human label entropy)—a finding that reframes fairness debates around annotation tasks rather than models. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows LLMs falling well short of expert human interpretation of implicature and presupposition in Italian political speech, with distractor design and prompting strategy materially affecting measured competence. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] demonstrates an analogous ceiling for fact-checking: standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly on PolitiFact claims unless given curated retrieval context, and search-enabled models show a citation skew toward left-leaning "credible" sources. Together these papers suggest a recurring pattern: LLMs are not unreliable in the aggregate, but their reliability is highly conditional on task framing, context provision, and the intrinsic ambiguity of the item being coded.

## Positioning, scaling, and inference from text

A related strand asks whether LLMs can go beyond classification to *measurement*—placing actors or texts on latent scales. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to locate texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses reproduces established scaling benchmarks across languages. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent traits, addressing the classic trade-off between rich but unwieldy open-ended data and constrained closed-ended items. [[Lee2026-je]] pushes the inference logic toward a genuinely novel and unsettling capability: LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, which raises privacy and micro-targeting concerns that echo the Cambridge Analytica moment but with a more powerful inferential engine. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends the logic of automated evaluation one level further, benchmarking whether AI coding agents can reproduce published social-science findings outright—finding surprisingly high (if agent-dependent) success, alongside worrying evidence of sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting, a direct methodological warning for anyone using LLMs to check or "validate" prior work.

## Coordination detection: a parallel computational tradition

Running alongside the annotation literature is a distinct but methodologically kindred tradition: detecting coordinated inauthentic behavior (CIB) in networked interaction data rather than in text content per se. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] offers the synthesizing move here, proposing a unifying definition of coordination (actors, synergic actions, intent) and a shared framework of authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, and time-variance to reconcile fragmented platform and academic definitions. Several papers refine detection machinery: [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] both argue that single-modality, time-agnostic coordination detection is inadequate, proposing multiplex and time-aware models that trade off integration strength against inclusiveness; [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] operationalizes a minimal, platform-agnostic definition of coordination in the CooRTweet R package, validated against the Keller et al. NIS dataset; [[Minici2024-tf]] proposes a graph foundation model (IOHunter) combining language and structural signals for cross-campaign generalization.

Other papers use these tools to interrogate, rather than merely refine, claims about coordination's causal importance. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] models the counterfactual influence coordinated accounts *could* have exerted in UK election retweet cascades and finds real coordinated accounts' placement resembles random assignment—suggesting CIB may be less pivotal than assumed. [[Pante2025-pq]] similarly re-examines claims of inter-state coordination on Twitter/X, arguing that without modern multi-signal detection and proper control datasets, prior findings of state collaboration may not hold up. Substantive applications of coordination detection include [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] on dual-use Twitter activity during China's COVID protests, [[Kim2026-br]] on two decades of suspected troll activity in Korean news comments (finding moral condemnation, not overt foreign praise, as the dominant and most effective rhetorical strategy), and Bastos2025-ol, which extends coordination analysis into the visual register by profiling troll-farm account imagery.

## Multimodality: beyond text-only pipelines

A growing subset insists that text-centric computational content analysis misses how political communication actually operates on contemporary platforms. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] shows GPT-4o substantially outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, Google Cloud Vision) for face recognition and person-counting in German campaign Instagram content, effectively lowering the technical barrier to visual political communication research. [[Arminio2025-tw]] makes a parallel case for image clustering, arguing that vision-language models capture *connotative* (culturally embedded) meaning that CNN-based pipelines miss, while also producing human-interpretable cluster labels via TF-IDF—directly addressing the interpretability gap that critics of black-box neural clustering raise. [[Arora2025-tx]] extends framing analysis itself into the multimodal register, arguing that fixed, text-only frame sets under-describe how outlets use images and text jointly. [[Gardam2025-er]] and [[Bruns2025-fz]] gesture toward similar arguments—visual-first methodology for climate-denial discourse on Instagram, and embedding-based "practice mapping" as an alternative to hairball network visualizations for multimodal interaction data—though both remain closer to programmatic statements than fully realized empirical demonstrations.

## Narrative and framing at scale

Beyond classification and coordination, several papers push computational methods toward capturing higher-order rhetorical structure: not just what topic a text is about, but what story it tells and who it casts in which role. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas's Actantial Model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" in Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse, finding that comments substantially soften surface-level narrative divergence between partisan video audiences even as deeper narrative motifs preserve partisan differences—a genuinely novel decomposition of polarization into content-level and reception-level components. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles the estimand problem explicitly, arguing that "narrative similarity" is conflated in existing literature with lexical overlap, topic similarity, and semantic similarity, and proposing an LLM-based claim-and-subject distillation pipeline validated against exact text reuse, topic modeling, and Relatio—demonstrating that estimator choice has real substantive consequences (its bioweapons-narrative case study finds mainstream-Russian narrative overlap invisible to n-gram methods). [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] gesture toward the same unsupervised, non-predefined-category ambition for polarized discourse framing more generally. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this narrative turn from the misinformation side, showing that source-level (domain-blocklist) misinformation metrics miss a larger phenomenon: true mainstream content repurposed to support misleading narratives, detected via co-sharing patterns with known fake news.

## Misinformation, fact-checking, and epistemics of AI-generated content

A dense cluster addresses misinformation measurement and correction, often via computational content analysis. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data with BERT-classified browsing to trace contagion and mitigation pathways in COVID-19 conspiracy belief formation. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as anomalous supply-demand imbalances across social media and search data, linking void periods to reduced credible content and higher misinformation prevalence. [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] extend computational content analysis to the affective register of misinformation and fact-checking, respectively—news values in flagged Facebook content across 24 countries, and emotionality as a fact-checking strategy. [[Dubey2026-bl]] and [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shift from measurement to intervention, testing "balanced news chatbots" and scaled Instagram prebunking ads as tools against conspiracy belief, while [[Kotz2026-lk]] demonstrates that conversational AI dialogue (not static messaging) shifts beliefs and policy support across climate, vaccination, and inequality domains, especially among skeptics—an important complement to the largely classificatory work elsewhere in this set, showing LLMs as *intervention* rather than *measurement* tools.

At the epistemological edge, [[Dierickx2026-tw]] and [[Triedman2025-uy]] confront what "fact" even means once generative AI enters the information ecosystem. Dierickx proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category for AI outputs that are plausible but not evidentially anchored, while Triedman's empirical audit of Grokipedia against Wikipedia shows concretely what this looks like in practice: heavy derivation from Wikipedia text combined with systematically greater reliance on unreliable and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial and political topics—an empirical demonstration of exactly the epistemic risks Dierickx theorizes.

## Data infrastructure and measurement artifacts

A recurring cautionary strand shows that computational content analysis is only as good as the data pipelines feeding it. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] demonstrates that web-scraping methodology itself—not just time delay—introduces massive, non-randomly distributed distortion in measures of online news consumption, undermining a large swath of web-tracking research that relies on ex-situ scraping. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] finds a platform-level artifact (Meta's 100-share anonymization threshold interacting with feed-ranking changes) that produces spuriously similar cross-country trends in the widely used URL Shares Dataset. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a parallel confound at the embedding level, showing that linear concept erasure (LEACE) can remove source/language confounds from text embeddings, dramatically improving cross-lingual and cross-source clustering and retrieval without degrading out-of-domain performance. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] extends the infrastructure critique to survey sampling, cataloguing systematic trade-offs between representativeness and response validity across nine opt-in online panels. [[Fan2026-af]] proposes a complementary reorientation: treating digital trace data as temporally structured "user-sequences" rather than aggregating away the fine-grained timing information that most measurement pipelines discard. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] rounds out this cluster by characterizing diffusion and reach patterns for Facebook misinformation during the 2020 US election at genuinely unprecedented scale—itself only possible because of the data-access arrangements the other papers scrutinize.

## Reflexive and normative accounts of computational analysis

A final set of papers steps back to ask what all this scale is *for*, and who should control it. [[Matias2025-px]] argues that public and lived-experience involvement is not a political concession but a way to materially improve the scientific rigor of AI evaluation, citing cases where community-driven re-analysis surfaced flaws invisible to purely technical audits. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends this civic-participatory lens to digital public squares broadly, surveying collective dialogue systems, bridging algorithms, and community moderation as LLM-enabled correctives to engagement-optimized platforms. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] offers a more skeptical theoretical counterpoint, arguing via Luhmann and conversation analysis that chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding—a caution against overreading LLM "comprehension" of political discourse that resonates with the annotation-validity findings above. [[Ng2026-og]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] gesture toward where this trajectory leads: formal social-theoretic frameworks for governing agentic AI populations, and automated scientific peer review itself, both suggesting that the methodological questions raised throughout this topic—reliability, validity, human oversight—will only intensify as LLMs move from annotating content to acting as autonomous agents and evaluators in their own right. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] provides a grounded empirical counterpoint to this speculative horizon, showing that audiences cannot reliably distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, with disclosure producing only short-lived curiosity rather than durable trust shifts—a reminder that the stakes of LLM-assisted content production extend well beyond the research pipeline into the media ecosystem being studied.

## Adjacent contexts: polarization, toxicity, and platform literacy

A smaller set of papers uses these computational tools to revisit long-standing communication-theory constructs. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] reframes echo chambers and filter bubbles as communicative feedback loops, arguing that reinforcing dynamics need not produce runaway radicalization. [[Marino2026-slef]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] apply LLM-based actor classification and entity-network methods to hyperpartisan Brazilian and German alternative media respectively, both complicating the assumption of static, impermeable partisan enclaves. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]]—linking validated dark-personality questionnaires to Reddit text—and [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]'s review of algorithm literacy round out the topic's periphery, showing how computational content-analysis methods are increasingly brought to bear on psychological and literacy questions that were previously the preserve of survey-based communication research.
