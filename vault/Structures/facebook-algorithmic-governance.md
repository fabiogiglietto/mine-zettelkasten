# Facebook Algorithmic Governance and Visibility

## The arc of inquiry

This body of work traces a shift from Facebook as a background "black box" whose algorithmic effects could only be inferred through user-level experiments, toward Facebook as a legible, auditable governance system whose specific policy interventions can be dated, measured, and contested—often using the very transparency infrastructure (Meta Content Library, URL Shares Dataset, CrowdTangle) that researchers spent a decade demanding. The throughline connecting them is the question of *visibility*: who gets seen, whose reach is amplified or suppressed, and whether these outcomes reflect user choice, network structure, or deliberate platform governance.

## From null effects to detectable interventions

The foundational empirical debate was set by the 2020 Meta-academic collaboration: [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Nyhan2023-gb]] used massive field experiments (chronological feeds, reshare removal, like-minded source reduction) during the US election and found strikingly consistent null results on polarization and attitudes, even as they documented large shifts in content composition. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] had earlier established the template—friend networks are the dominant filter, algorithmic ranking a secondary, modest one—while [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicated this picture by showing segregation compounds at each stage of the "funnel of engagement" and is sharply asymmetric, concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem that also houses most fact-checked misinformation.

This null-effects consensus has been increasingly challenged by work showing that algorithmic effects are detectable once researchers look for *policy-level* interventions rather than diffuse individual-level attitude change. [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates that Facebook's News Feed, far from being opaque, produces measurable, lagged engagement signatures tied to documented ranking changes—explicitly arguing against the "black box" framing and for systemic DSA-enabled auditing. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this logic across 2017–2022, showing that the relationship between shares and views is itself non-stationary, with partisan content penalized and quality-adherent content rewarded in ways that spike precisely around known governance moments like the 2020 "break the glass" measures—direct evidence that Facebook actively curates rather than passively transmits.

## The political content reduction policy as a case study

[[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the pivot point this topic is organized around: using the Meta Content Library, it identifies structural breakpoints showing that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' reach by up to 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and only partially reversed after the 2025 policy change—while extremist accounts evaded the penalty by posting more frequently. This paper exemplifies the methodological turn the whole cluster is moving toward: rather than asking "does the algorithm polarize users," it asks "what did this specific, dateable governance decision do to specific political actors," treating policy change as a natural experiment. It directly extends [[McNally2025-dn]]'s claim that algorithmic interventions leave empirically recoverable traces, and its finding of a transparency deficit (the policy's absence from DSA compliance reporting) echoes [[Rogers2026-zz]]'s broader argument about Meta's strategic redefinition of moderation categories to narrow accountability, and [[Rogers2025-sl8f]]'s observation that Meta's definitions of "inauthentic" behavior have progressively contracted to exclude much of what once counted as manipulation.

[[Votta2025-xz]] complements this by showing that even advertising reach and cost—ostensibly governed by advertiser targeting choices—are substantially shaped by opaque ad-delivery algorithms, reinforcing that visibility outcomes across both organic and paid Facebook content are products of platform-side calibration rather than neutral market mechanisms.

## Reduction as a governance category

Conceptually, this entire cluster can be read through [[Gillespie2022-jx]]'s argument that "reduction"—demoting without removing—constitutes a form of content moderation that has been kept deliberately invisible, outside the frame of transparency reports and public debate. The Italian MP study is arguably the most concrete empirical demonstration of exactly this dynamic: a reduction policy applied broadly to "political content," unannounced in its actual timing, producing asymmetric effects (penalizing accountable mainstream politicians while extremist accounts adapt around it)—precisely the "troubling implications for transparency, accountability, and social equity" Gillespie anticipates. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research adds a complementary micro-mechanism: the platform's own affective design features (which reactions are weighted, how anger and controversy are rewarded) interact with reduction policies to shape which political content survives the funnel.

## Misinformation, trust, and differential visibility

A parallel strand examines content *quality* rather than content *type* as a visibility determinant. [[Allcott2019-gn]] and [[Guess2021-ym]] chart the declining relative prevalence of link-based misinformation on Facebook after 2016, but [[Yang2023-cg]] shows this is an artifact of method: image-based misinformation, ignored by link-based studies, remains rampant and starkly partisan-skewed. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the US-centric Guess et al. design to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares around election years even as Views remain comparatively stable—suggesting platform-level dampening consistent with the active-curation thesis of [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]]. [[Rogers2026-zz]] ties these threads together historically, tracing Meta's terminological evolution from "fake news" to "borderline content" and showing that whatever the label, the content that still performs well remains overwhelmingly right-wing—a direct echo of [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]'s asymmetric segregation finding and a challenge to Meta's "content-agnostic" self-description.

## Platforms beyond Facebook: comparative algorithmic governance

Several papers extend these questions to other platforms, offering comparative leverage on whether Facebook's dynamics are platform-specific or symptomatic of algorithmic curation generally. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Gauthier2026-iq]] find that X's algorithm confers visibility advantages tied to agitation, owner-proximity, and conservative-activist following, with Gauthier's persistence mechanism (follows induced by the algorithm outlast the algorithm itself) offering a possible resolution to the puzzle of why Meta's 2020 null results differ from X's detectable effects. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] show that X's recommender and Community Notes systems respectively encode latent ideological structure and systematically undermoderate polarizing content—structural parallels to the asymmetric segregation Facebook studies document. [[Brown2026-br]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] examine YouTube, separately disentangling algorithmic from user-driven effects (finding rabbit holes but not radicalization) and documenting how deplatforming fails against distributed, algorithmically-amplified "ambient" content ecosystems—a caution against assuming policy interventions like Meta's reduction policy straightforwardly suppress targeted content, since compensatory posting-volume strategies (as seen among Italian extremist accounts) can reconstitute reach regardless of per-post penalties. [[Jurg2025-ur]] applies a DSA-era audit lens to YouTube's moderation of EU election content, finding the same opacity and inconsistency in platform communication that [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] identifies for Meta. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes TikTok's "clustered publics" as a successor social form to Facebook's "networked publics," suggesting the entire visibility regime Facebook-era research has mapped may be giving way to a qualitatively different, more opaque curatorial logic. [[Brady2026-ln]] offers a constructive counterpoint, testing whether redesigned algorithms (not just removal of algorithmic curation) can actively mitigate the norm-distorting effects that engagement-based ranking produces, pointing toward prosocial algorithm design as a research and policy horizon.

## User experience, folk theories, and the politics of "gaming"

A final strand grounds these structural findings in lived user experience. [[Bucher2017-qh]] shows that users' "algorithmic imaginary"—their affective, often inaccurate theories of how Facebook's algorithm works—itself feeds back into platform behavior, while [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] surveys the fragmented literature on algorithm literacy this generates. [[Petre2019-zh]] and [[Cotter2019-js]] examine how platforms discursively police the boundary between "legitimate" visibility strategies and "gaming," a framing directly relevant to understanding how Italian extremist accounts' volume-compensation strategy in [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] might itself become subject to future moderation narratives. [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] and [[Wan2026-ai]] bring this full circle to elected officials: Brazilian parliamentarians perceive (correctly, per engagement data) that antagonistic content is rewarded and legislative substance punished, a finding that resonates directly with the Italian case's core concern—how platform-level visibility governance reshapes the incentives and public presence of democratic representatives themselves.
