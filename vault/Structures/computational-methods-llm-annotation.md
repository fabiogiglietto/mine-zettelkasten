# Computational Methods and LLM-Assisted Annotation

## From Embeddings to Annotation: A Shared Methodological Frontier

The papers gathered here trace an arc from the earliest, most basic computational task — turning text into a numerical representation that can be clustered or compared — through increasingly ambitious uses of large language models as classifiers, annotators, and even measurement instruments in communication research. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] anchors this arc empirically: a head-to-head comparison of OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for clustering political news shared on Facebook shows that general-purpose LLM embeddings outperform specialized BERT variants even in a non-English setting, extending findings previously reported for English corpora. [[Fan2025-ut]] complements this by diagnosing *why* naive embedding-based similarity analyses go wrong—pretrained embeddings encode confounders like source or language—and shows that a simple linear correction (LEACE) can be applied as a near-costless preprocessing step across ten embedding models, dramatically improving clustering and retrieval without harming out-of-distribution performance. Together they suggest that the choice and treatment of embeddings, not just the choice of downstream algorithm, is a first-order methodological decision that political-communication researchers have often left unexamined. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the embedding logic to the visual domain, arguing that CNN-based image clustering captures only denotative content and proposing a VLLM-to-text-to-embedding pipeline that recovers *connotative* meaning (e.g., grouping wind turbines and solar panels as "renewable energy") while remaining interpretable via TF-IDF summaries—an argument echoed methodologically by [[Bruns2025-fz]], which proposes "practice mapping" via vector embeddings of network actions as a way past the interpretive limits of conventional multimodal network visualization.

## Building LLM-in-the-Loop Pipelines

A second cluster of papers moves from embeddings to full annotation pipelines that insert LLMs at one or more stages of classification. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] is the most explicit methodological statement of this design, documenting a three-stage pipeline—fine-tuned political classifier, LLM-generated embeddings for clustering, and LLM-generated cluster labels—applied to Italian election data, and using this as a case for why validating "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipelines requires bespoke protocols rather than reusing validation practices built for single fine-tuned models. This companion piece to [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] shows the embedding comparison feeding directly into a working substantive pipeline, with the same finding (text-embedding-3-large's superiority) operationalized at scale, and it foregrounds a problem that recurs throughout the topic: LLM-generated clusters and labels vary in granularity in ways that resist a single evaluation standard, and expert (not crowdsourced) coders are needed once the model already outperforms low-skill annotators. [[Larsson2026-ro]] offers a parallel demonstration in a different context—GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification of a decade of Norwegian Facebook posts, validated against human coders—showing that hybrid LLM/human content analysis can support longitudinal, non-English political communication research. [[Ober2026-vd]] pushes the "human-in-the-loop" logic into qualitative analysis, integrating topic modeling (for transparency), LLM prompt engineering (for scalable labeling), and iterative human codebook refinement, explicitly arguing that topic modeling's mathematical grounding should anchor LLM-assisted labeling rather than the reverse. [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Bailard2024-pj]] show two different routes to reliable classification without relying on prompting alone: fine-tuning an open-source LLM (Llama 3.1 via QLoRA) for conflict-event classification, and fine-tuning DeBERTa for collective-action-frame classification of extremist Telegram discourse, respectively—both demonstrating that domain-specific fine-tuning can yield robust, prompt-invariant classifiers well-suited to large corpora. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends this in the multimodal-visual space, finding that a general-purpose multimodal LLM (GPT-4o) outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person counting in campaign imagery, echoing the embeddings' finding that general LLMs increasingly outcompete specialized models even outside language.

## Reliability, Disagreement, and the Limits of LLM Judgment

A crucial through-line concerns whether these pipelines can be trusted, and under what conditions they break down. [[Brown2025-jk]] directly interrogates the "whose labels?" question, finding that LLM-human disagreement in subjective annotation tasks is overwhelmingly explained by *item difficulty* (human label entropy) rather than by systematic demographic bias, and that when biases do appear they are dataset-specific rather than model-specific—an important corrective to fears that LLM annotation silently encodes majority-group perspectives. [[Paci2025-ag]] complicates optimism about LLM judgment from another angle, showing that even capable models like GPT-4o-mini fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, with chain-of-thought prompting offering only modest gains—a reminder that fluency in surface classification does not guarantee pragmatic competence on the subtler inferential tasks central to political discourse analysis. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] identifies an analogous ceiling in fact-checking: standard and even reasoning-enabled LLMs perform poorly at verifying political claims from internal knowledge or generic web search, and only a curated retrieval system dramatically closes the gap—while also revealing that search-enabled models exhibit a left-leaning citation skew, adding a normative wrinkle to claims of LLM objectivity. [[Balluff2026-if]] synthesizes these concerns into an explicit critique of "unreflective" LLM adoption across text analysis, synthetic data generation, and simulation, arguing that reproducibility (given opaque corporate updates), environmental cost, and demographic bias in synthetic outputs are systematically underweighted relative to convenience, and that smaller, open, task-appropriate models often perform comparably at far lower cost. [[Tan2024-vl]] situates all of this within a broader taxonomy, surveying the rapidly growing literature on LLM-based data annotation and synthesis and identifying quality assurance and ethics as the field's persistent open problems—concerns that [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends into the domain of reproducibility itself, showing that frontier AI coding agents can reproduce a striking share of published social-science findings, but also that they are vulnerable to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting, echoing the annotation-reliability concerns raised elsewhere in the topic in a new methodological register.

## LLMs as Measurement Instruments

Beyond classification, several papers treat LLMs as instruments for scaling or positioning texts along latent dimensions—a more ambitious methodological move than annotation per se. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes simply asking an instruction-tuned LLM to place a text on an ideological dimension and averaging many such responses, reporting strong correlations with established benchmarks across languages and text types. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] pursues a related goal for open-ended survey responses, pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to generate continuous latent trait scales that improve on both closed-ended items and prior LLM-coding approaches. [[Lee2026-je]] pushes measurement into ethically fraught territory, showing that LLMs can reliably infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse by exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, outperforming supervised baselines and raising acute privacy concerns. [[Elfes2026-jb]] and [[Waight2025-al]] both use LLMs to operationalize concepts—actantial narrative roles and cross-lingual narrative similarity, respectively—that were previously intractable at scale; the latter's validation exercise is particularly notable for showing that fine-tuning an LLM annotator on boundary cases substantially improves precision, and for demonstrating that estimator choice (exact text reuse versus LLM-based narrative comparison) has substantive downstream consequences for what phenomena become visible at all. [[Lai2024-to]] rounds out this cluster with a non-LLM approach to a similar measurement problem—estimating YouTube video ideology from Reddit sharing patterns via correspondence analysis—illustrating that the broader challenge of scaling political content is being tackled through both LLM-based and network-based routes. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends the instrumental use of LLMs furthest, treating conversational AI itself as an intervention that shifts beliefs and policy support, with systematic textual analysis of the model's adaptive persuasive strategies functioning as a form of LLM-assisted discourse annotation.

## Adjacent Computational Pipelines: Coordination, Networks, and Infrastructure

A wider set of papers extends the topic's core concerns—validated, scalable computational pipelines—into coordinated-behavior detection and platform-data infrastructure, domains that share the reliability and validation challenges central to LLM annotation even where LLMs are not always the primary tool. [[Iannucci2025-eg]], [[Minici2024-tf]], [[Righetti2025-sl2a]], [[Luceri2025-tr]], and [[Pante2025-pq]] collectively advance and stress-test methods for detecting coordinated inauthentic behavior—via time-aware multiplex networks, graph foundation models, a generalized platform-agnostic R package, and adaptations to video-first platforms—while [[Pante2025-pq]] explicitly demonstrates how weak validation (absent control datasets) can produce overstated claims of inter-state coordination, a cautionary parallel to the annotation-reliability debates above. [[Kim2026-br]] and Bastos2025-ol apply explainable machine learning and computer vision, respectively, to characterize troll behavior through rhetorical and visual signatures, while [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] cautions that computationally derived textual proxies for personality traits show limited convergence with validated psychometric measures—another instance of the gap between surface linguistic signal and the underlying construct it is meant to index. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] push automated framing analysis beyond predefined categories and single modalities. Finally, a set of infrastructure-oriented papers—[[Stagnaro2025-pz]], [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]], [[Ulloa2024-jm]], [[Fan2026-af]], [[Zheng2026-bi]], and [[Brown2026-br]]—interrogate the upstream data-collection and sampling pipelines (opt-in panels, Facebook-ad recruitment, web scraping, digital trace sequencing, platform-wide random sampling, algorithmic audits) on which any downstream LLM-assisted or embedding-based analysis ultimately depends, underscoring that the "reliability" problem foregrounded by LLM annotation research is really one instance of a broader challenge running through every stage of computational communication research—collection, representation, classification, and interpretation alike. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] closes the loop by turning this same validation logic reflexively on the scientific process itself, using an agentic AI pipeline to review manuscripts for logical and empirical flaws—suggesting that the tension between automation's efficiency and its need for careful, task-specific validation, so central to LLM-assisted annotation, is poised to become a general feature of computational research infrastructure well beyond communication studies.
