# Health Misinformation Networks

## Coordination as an Organizing Logic

A methodological throughline connects five of these papers: coordination — not content — is the unit of analysis. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the paradigm most explicitly, using the CooRnet package to detect Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) among Italian Facebook actors as a content-agnostic signal that can surface problematic information faster than manual fact-checking. Its genealogy of an Italian covid-skeptic network — emerging from pre-existing political clusters, adapting tactics like "link laundering" and first-comment link placement to evade moderation — supplies the empirical substrate for [[Marino2023-9137f448]], which zooms into the same dataset to trace how an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers, amplified through remediation by legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers, achieves disproportionate share-driven engagement. Together these two Italian studies model coordination as a layered phenomenon: macro-level actor networks, meso-level thematic clusters, and micro-level tactical repertoires for evading platform enforcement.

[[Song2025-yh]] extends the CLSB lens cross-nationally, comparing UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse and complicating the assumption that coordination is inherently nefarious: pro-vaccine communities coordinate too, circulating credible institutional content (NHS, CDC), while anti-vaccine coordination diverges by political culture — UK networks emphasizing vaccine safety and denser, more engaged structures, US networks emphasizing individual freedom within looser but larger networks. This comparative finding anticipates the broader collection's implicit argument that "misinformation network" is not a fixed structural signature but a socio-politically contingent one. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the coordination concept onto Twitter/X and into science communication specifically, showing that a small, non-automated but highly coordinated contrarian cluster disproportionately amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts, and that mainstream news coverage of scientific papers tends to *follow* rather than precede superspreader activity — making coordinated platform actors an upstream driver of the broader information ecosystem rather than a downstream artifact of it.

## From Coordination to Consequence

Where the coordination-detection papers focus on structure and tactics, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Adam2026-tz]] push toward causal consequence. Bollenbacher's SIRVA epidemic model links geolocated antivaccine tweets to county-level vaccination shortfalls, cases, and deaths in the US, offering a rare causally-grounded bridge between online speech and offline epidemic outcomes — a lower-bound estimate that nonetheless demonstrates the tractability of connecting network-level exposure to population health. Adam's German/Swiss panel-plus-web-tracking study operates at the individual level instead of the county level, showing that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and political predisposition: alternative media produce contagion effects, mainstream debunking produces mitigation effects on average, but populists selectively engage with the former and experience backfire/counterarguing when exposed to the latter. Read alongside the coordination papers, Adam's finding that even mainstream outlets circulated more conspiracy-supporting than -opposing content underscores why coordinated amplification (as in Efstratiou and Giglietto) can gain purchase even in ostensibly high-trust information environments.

## Demand, Supply, and the Individual Consumer

Two papers reframe the ecosystem in market terms. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as anomalous demand-supply imbalances across six European countries' social media and search data, showing empirically that voids correlate with reduced high-credibility content and increased misinformation share — providing a mechanistic, event-triggered account (vaccine rollouts, EMA authorizations) of *when* misinformation is most likely to fill gaps that coordinated networks like those in Giglietto and Marino can then exploit. [[Lyons2026-ca]] shifts scale again, from ecosystem to individual: passive web-tracking of US adults shows low-credibility health exposure is rare in aggregate but starkly concentrated among older adults, poor health-discerners, and consumers of dubious political news, arriving via referral chains from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media referral — a finding that complicates any assumption that coordinated network amplification (the mechanism foregrounded elsewhere in this set) is the primary exposure pathway for ordinary users, at least outside acute crisis moments.

## Convergences and Open Tensions

Across the set, national comparison recurs as a structuring device — Germany/Switzerland (Adam), UK/US (Song), Italy (Giglietto, Marino), six European countries (Scalco), the US alone (Bollenbacher, Lyons) — consistently showing that political culture and media trust architecture shape both the content and structure of misinformation circulation, even when the underlying coordination mechanics (CLSB, superspreader amplification, void-filling) are portable across contexts. A productive tension remains between structural/network accounts (Giglietto, Marino, Efstratiou, Song) that locate the driver of misinformation in orchestrated amplification, and individual/psychological accounts (Adam, Lyons) that locate it in selective exposure and predisposition; Bollenbacher and Scalco each attempt, in different registers, to close the gap between these levels by linking network-level signals to population outcomes. Taken together, the papers trace an arc from detecting coordination, to explaining its cultural variation, to measuring its downstream epidemiological cost, to questioning how much of everyday exposure it actually explains.
