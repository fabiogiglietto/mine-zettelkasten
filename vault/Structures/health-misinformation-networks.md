# Health Misinformation Networks

## Mapping the Architecture of Problematic Health Information

The papers gathered here trace a single argument in several registers: that health misinformation rarely spreads through diffuse, organic contagion but through identifiable *structures* — coordinated actor networks, alternative influence hierarchies, and gatekeeping arrangements — that can be mapped, measured, and in some cases causally linked to offline harm. The Italian case anchors much of this work empirically, but the corpus repeatedly widens the lens to Germany, the UK, the US and Brazil, building toward a comparative infrastructure for understanding how these dynamics recur (and diverge) across political and media systems.

### Coordinated Networks as the Basic Unit of Analysis

The methodological backbone of the topic is Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB) analysis. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the paradigm for Italy, showing how a covid-skeptic Facebook network emerged as an offshoot of pre-existing League/Five Star political clusters and evolved tactics — link laundering, first-comment link-hiding, exploitation of religious groups — specifically to evade platform moderation. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this dataset to trace an "Alternative Influence Network," identifying an Intellectual Dark Web cluster of high-engagement, low-volume influencers who are amplified less by their own posting than by legacy journalists and right-leaning outlets recycling their statements — a remediation logic that ties the coordinated network back into the hybrid media system. [[Song2025-yh]] internationalizes the CLSB approach, comparing UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse and showing that coordination is not inherently malicious: anti-vaccine networks in the UK were denser and safety-focused, while in the US pro-vaccine institutional coordination (NHS, CDC) was comparably dense, revealing that the *structure* of coordination is agnostic but its *content alignment* is shaped by national political culture. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the same logic to Twitter/X, finding an almost entirely contrarian coordinated retweet network that concentrates amplification on a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts — not the most popular accounts overall — and that news coverage typically follows rather than precedes this superspreader activity, closing a pathway from coordinated platform actors to mainstream agenda-setting. [[Rothut2026-or]] shows how the same connective logic operates off mainstream platforms altogether: on Telegram, the German Querdenken protest movement was structurally embedded — via a small set of brokerage-position far-right channels — in transnational conspiracist and extremist milieus, demonstrating that protest infrastructures, not just fact-checking-resistant pages, can function as mainstreaming conduits.

### Gatekeeping, Aggregation, and the Persistence of Voids

A second thread interrogates the gatekeepers and infrastructural conditions that shape what health information becomes visible in the first place. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examines Google News Italia as an algorithmic gatekeeper for health content circulating on Facebook, finding that emotionally charged "popular" topics (celebrity illness, healthcare malfunction) generate disproportionate engagement relative to scientific or institutional health news — a structural bias toward virality over technical accuracy that creates fertile ground for the coordinated actors described above. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] identifies an analogous but elite-level gatekeeping asymmetry in the UK press, where newspapers' political orientation predicts which side of the (mainstream, not fringe) scientific debate over COVID-19 restrictions gets cited — showing that polarization in "who counts as an expert" can occur even among credentialed scientists, without any conspiratorial content per se. [[Scalco2026-bd]] supplies a measurement innovation that unites these gatekeeping concerns with the network studies above: by operationalizing "information voids" as anomalous demand-supply imbalances across Facebook, Twitter, Wikipedia and Google Trends, it shows empirically that voids around vaccine milestones (EMA authorizations, AstraZeneca suspension) are precisely when high-credibility content share drops and misinformation prevalence rises — giving the coordinated-network findings a demand-side mechanism: voids create the vacuum that alternative influence structures fill.

### From Exposure to Belief to Behavior

A third cluster asks what such exposure actually does to audiences. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is not simply "contagion" from alternative media but a joint product of predispositions (populism, political mistrust) and selective media diets, including a backfire/counterarguing pathway when populists do encounter mainstream debunking. [[Lyons2026-ca]] narrows the aperture to health content specifically, finding that low-credibility exposure is rare in aggregate but heavily concentrated among older adults with poorer discernment and stronger conspiracist worldviews, and — importantly — that this exposure is driven by habitual referral chains between low-credibility sites rather than by social media or search, complicating platform-centric theories of exposure. [[Lieu2025-nl]], working in the climate domain but methodologically resonant, shows that *content category* (e.g., attacks on proposed solutions) rather than the logical fallacy employed drives perceived veracity and polarization — a reminder that misinformation's persuasive power often lies in framing choices rather than in the falsifiable claim itself, a lesson directly applicable to the "remediation" and reframing tactics documented in [[Marino2023-9137f448]] and [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]]. At the behavioral end, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] offers the corpus's most direct causal claim, using an epidemic-compartmental model to estimate that antivaccine Twitter exposure caused thousands of vaccine refusals and measurable excess cases and deaths in the US — translating network-level circulation into public-health outcomes.

### Beyond Feeds: Messaging Apps and Democratic Stakes

A final arc extends the topic from health misinformation proper into its political analogue, testing whether the same structural logics (coordination, gatekeeping, alternative influence) generalize to electoral misinformation, particularly via encrypted messaging. [[Ventura2025-sw]] uses a WhatsApp deactivation experiment in Brazil to show that private messaging platforms — largely invisible to the feed-based analyses dominant elsewhere in this corpus — shape misinformation exposure and political belief in ways distinct from public platforms. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] develop this further with panel data from the 2022 Brazilian election, showing that belief in electoral misinformation erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, that messaging-app news use indirectly fuels these effects via misinformation belief, and that mainstream news consumption acts as a stabilizing counterweight even among "losing" partisans. Together with [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]], which demonstrates a Facebook-ad-based method for recruiting and surveying hard-to-reach conspiracist populations in Italy, this final cluster signals where the topic is headed methodologically: toward experimentally and causally grounded designs capable of testing, across contexts as different as Italy, Germany, the UK, the US and Brazil, whether coordinated and alternative influence structures are merely correlates of misinformation belief or genuine engines of democratic and public-health harm.
