# Health Misinformation and Problematic Content Networks

## Mapping Coordinated Networks: The CooRnet Lineage

A core strand of this topic traces the development and application of a single methodological lineage for surfacing coordinated inauthentic behavior on Facebook. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] lays out the foundational tool, CooRnet, which operationalizes Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) as a content-agnostic signal and embeds it in an iterative Actors-Behaviors-Content cycle. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] takes this infrastructure into the field as the MINE-FACTS project, showing that CLSB-based detection can outpace manual fact-checking — surfacing problematic content at roughly 40% hit-rate versus ~28% for routine checking — while cataloguing the evasive tactics (link laundering, first-comment link placement, religious-group infiltration) that Italian covid-skeptic networks used to survive platform enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this same Italian dataset forward in time to ask *who* gets amplified once coordination is established, identifying an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster whose share-heavy engagement profile and symbiotic relationship with legacy journalists reveals a remediation pathway from TV and newspaper soundbites into conspiracy circulation. Read together, these three papers trace a single research program moving from tool-building, to field deployment, to content-level interpretation of what the coordinated networks actually spread — and they anchor the topic's recurring claim that coordination is a structural, behavioral signature distinct from bot automation or any single bad actor.

[[Song2025-yh]] broadens the CLSB lens comparatively, showing that coordinated link sharing is not inherently malicious: in the UK and US, anti-vaccine and pro-vaccine communities both coordinate, but with starkly different content profiles (UK anti-vaccine CLSB emphasizing safety skepticism, US anti-vaccine CLSB emphasizing individual freedom) and markedly different proportions of verified versus "unknown" source material. This reframes CLSB as a tactic shaped by national political culture rather than a marker of ill intent per se, complicating the more unambiguously adversarial framing of the Italian case studies. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], working earlier and adjacently, examines Google News Italia as an algorithmic gatekeeper for health information, finding that engagement is driven less by source credibility than by topical "emotionality" (celebrity health stories vastly out-engage technical science reporting) — a finding that anticipates the remediation and amplification dynamics later documented in the AIN and CLSB studies.

## Alternative Influence, Gatekeeping, and Science Communication Pathways

Beyond the CooRnet lineage, two papers zoom out to map the broader information ecosystem in which coordinated clusters sit. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] performs an English-language, platform-wide analogue to the Italian AIN work: it finds a nearly entirely contrarian coordinated retweet network on Twitter that artificially elevates a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts, and demonstrates that news coverage of COVID science tends to *follow* rather than precede superspreader activity — with high-trust outlets tracking conformist superspreaders and low-trust outlets tracking contrarian ones. This directly parallels Marino's finding that legacy media remain central nodes for circulating alternative-influence narratives, suggesting a cross-national, cross-platform pattern whereby coordinated/alternative actors seed content that mainstream media subsequently ratifies or launders. [[Scalco2026-bd]] contributes a complementary supply-side diagnostic: rather than studying who spreads content, it measures when reliable supply fails to meet demand, defining "information voids" via anomaly detection across Facebook, Twitter, GDELT, Wikipedia, and Google Trends. Its finding that voids are empirically associated with lower-credibility content and longer persistence than periods of overabundance offers a structural, event-triggered explanation for *why* windows open for the kind of coordinated and alternative-influence amplification documented elsewhere in this set — information voids plausibly being the conditions under which CLSB networks and AIN clusters gain traction.

## From Exposure to Harm: Causal and Behavioral Consequences

A second cluster asks whether documented exposure to problematic content actually changes health behavior and outcomes, and the two papers here reach partly complementary, partly tension-producing conclusions. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] builds a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets and estimates a causal chain from Twitter exposure to vaccine refusal to excess cases and deaths — a modest but non-zero lower-bound effect (~14,000 refusals, ~545 cases, ~8 deaths). [[Allen2024-av]], working on Facebook with a much larger-scale, exposure-weighted causal design, arrives at a more unsettling reframing: fact-check-flagged misinformation is more persuasive *per view* but reaches so few people that its aggregate impact is dwarfed — by a factor of 46 — by unflagged, "vaccine-skeptical" content from mainstream outlets that is technically accurate but implies vaccines are harmful. Taken together, these two papers suggest that the predominant focus on coordinated/flagged misinformation networks (the subject of the CooRnet-lineage papers) may understate the public-health stakes of ordinary, credentialed media content — a point that resonates with Efstratiou's finding that mainstream outlets actively circulate contrarian-aligned material, and with Adam's finding (below) that even quality mainstream media circulated substantial conspiracy-supporting content during the pandemic.

## Who Is Exposed, and Why: Individual-Level Dynamics

A third strand turns from networks and content to audiences. [[Adam2026-tz]] uses panel-plus-web-tracking data in Germany/Switzerland to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and predisposition: populists selectively engage with conspiracy-supporting alternative media and selectively avoid — or counterargue against — mainstream debunking, producing both contagion and (occasionally backfiring) mitigation effects. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends the exposure-tracking paradigm to health content specifically in the US, finding that low-credibility health exposure is rare in the aggregate but highly concentrated among older adults, poor health-information discerners, and consumers of dubious political news — and crucially, that this exposure arrives via referral from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media, implying habitual rather than incidental consumption. Both papers converge on a key structural insight echoed throughout this topic: exposure to problematic content is not evenly distributed but concentrated among small, identifiable, cross-domain subpopulations, whether defined by political predisposition (Adam) or demographic/behavioral profile (Lyons). [[Lieu2025-nl]] complicates the content side of this picture experimentally, showing that it is the *substance* of climate misinformation (attacks on proposed solutions) rather than its logical form (the specific fallacy used) that drives perceived veracity and political polarization — a reminder, transferable to health misinformation, that taxonomizing argumentative structure may matter less than targeting specific high-resonance claim categories.

## Beyond Fact-Checking: Correction and Intervention Strategies

The topic closes with two papers that push past detection and toward remedy, implicitly questioning whether the claim-by-claim fact-checking apparatus that motivates tools like CooRnet is adequate to the problem it targets. [[Ramos2026-qo]], drawn from Maldita.es's response to the 2024 Valencia floods, argues that fact-checking alone cannot neutralize disinformation *narratives* that evolve and regenerate even after factual correction, proposing narrative monitoring and argument-checking (targeting the rhetorical scaffolding — false authority, post hoc reasoning — rather than individual claims) as necessary complements. This directly echoes concerns raised by Giglietto and Slack about the latency and brittleness of claim-based correction relative to adaptive coordinated networks. [[Costello2024-bg]], by contrast, offers evidence that deep, personalized evidentiary engagement — via LLM dialogue — can durably shift even entrenched conspiracy beliefs, challenging motivational accounts of belief resistance and suggesting that the apparent failure of debunking documented elsewhere in this topic (e.g., the backfire/counterarguing effect in Adam2026-tz) may reflect insufficient personalization and depth rather than an intractable property of conspiracist cognition.

## Synthesis

Across these fourteen papers, a layered picture emerges: structural tools (CooRnet and its descendants) expose the mechanics of coordination; comparative and cross-platform studies (Efstratiou, Song, Scalco) situate that coordination within broader media and supply-demand ecosystems; causal-impact studies (Bollenbacher, Allen) push back against assuming that coordinated or flagged content is where the public-health harm actually concentrates; audience-level studies (Adam, Lyons, Lieu) locate susceptibility in specific predispositions and demographics rather than the population at large; and the closing pair (Ramos, Costello) debate what kind of correction — narrative-level or dialogic — might actually work. The empirical center of gravity is heavily Italian, UK/US, and German-Swiss, built substantially on the Facebook CLSB methodology; the topic's framing nod to country-specific studies in India and Nigeria is not yet represented among the filed papers, marking a clear direction for the structure note's future expansion into Global South network studies.
