# Health Misinformation Networks

## Coordinated Networks and the Architecture of Circulation

The empirical core of this topic is a family of studies that treat health misinformation not as isolated false claims but as the product of coordinated network structures. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the methodological baseline: using the CooRnet package to detect Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB), the MINE-FACTS project shows that Italian covid-skeptic clusters emerged as offshoots of pre-existing political coordination (League, Five Star Movement accounts) and adapted tactics—link laundering, first-comment link placement, infiltration of religious groups—specifically to evade platform enforcement. Crucially, the paper demonstrates that a content-agnostic, behavioral signal can surface problematic content at a rate nearly 40% higher than routine third-party fact-checking, making the case for network-level detection as a genuine *speed-up* mechanism for fact-checkers. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this same Italian covid-skeptic dataset upward, from link-sharing behavior to the influencer layer: an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of public figures generates disproportionate share-driven engagement, and—critically—legacy media and mainstream journalists remain the central nodes through which this content is remediated and legitimated, revealing a hybrid-media logic in which "alternative" narratives are laundered through recognizably mainstream channels rather than existing in a separate ecosystem. [[Song2025-yh]] broadens the comparative lens to the UK and US, showing that CLSB is not inherently a vector of malice: pro-vaccine coalitions coordinate just as densely as anti-vaccine ones, but with divergent credibility profiles (largely institutional sources like NHS/CDC versus high shares of "unknown" or unverifiable content) and divergent national framings—UK anti-vaccine discourse foregrounds safety, US anti-vaccine discourse foregrounds individual liberty. [[Rothut2026-or]] pushes the network argument into an adjacent register, showing how the German Querdenken protest movement functioned as a meso-level conduit—via shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology—linking moderate pandemic skeptics into a Telegram network whose structural center was occupied not by protest organizers but by established far-right brokers, illustrating how protest infrastructures can mainstream fringe communities over time.

## Gatekeepers, Voids, and Who Gets Exposed

A second cluster interrogates the supply side and the demographic distribution of exposure rather than the coordination mechanics themselves. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] reframes Google News Italia as a classical gatekeeper whose algorithmic curation shapes which health topics circulate on Facebook, but shows that user engagement decouples from source credibility: emotionally charged, "popular" categories (celebrity health stories, malfunction scandals) drive comments and shares far more than scientific or systemic health topics, regardless of outlet identity—a finding that anticipates the topical-affect dynamics later explored in fact-checking research. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes a related but distinct phenomenon—the "information void," where demand for reliable content outpaces supply—showing empirically that these voids, triggered by real-world events like vaccine authorizations, are associated with reduced high-credibility content and increased misinformation, and that voids persist longer than periods of overabundance, implying an asymmetric vulnerability window that gatekeeping interventions must specifically target. [[Lyons2026-ca]] complicates both papers' implicit assumption of broad-based exposure: passive tracking data show that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in the aggregate but heavily concentrated in a small population (older adults, poor discerners, dubious-political-news consumers), and—strikingly—arrives via referral chains between low-credibility sites themselves rather than through search or social media, suggesting that gatekeeping interventions focused on major platforms may miss the habitual, cross-domain pathways that actually sustain exposure among the most vulnerable.

## From Media Exposure to Belief to Behavior

A third strand traces the causal chain from exposure through belief to real-world consequence. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is not simply "supply-driven": it results from a marriage of media exposure and predispositions, in which populist and distrustful individuals selectively engage with conspiracy-supportive alternative media and selectively avoid mainstream debunking—and when they do encounter debunking, a counterarguing/backfire pathway can actually reinforce their prior beliefs, complicating naïve corrective-messaging assumptions found elsewhere in this topic. [[Marwick2025-vx]] offers a qualitative counterpoint to this exposure-effects model at the extremist end of the spectrum: analyzing "redpilling" narratives, the paper argues that the popular "exposure equals belief" framework misdescribes how disinformation actually functions in far-right recruitment, which is instead a gradual socialization process in which pseudo-evidence is marshaled to sustain a self-image of rational inquiry—a finding that resonates with [[Rothut2026-or]]'s emphasis on gradual, discursive mainstreaming over sudden conversion. At the most consequential end of the causal chain, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] attempts to close the gap between correlational misinformation research and epidemiological outcomes: fitting a novel SIRVA compartmental model to county-level data, the authors estimate that antivaccine Twitter content caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and at least 8 additional COVID-19 deaths, offering one of the few attempts in this literature to quantify the offline mortality cost of online health misinformation with a causal (rather than merely associative) design.

## Fact-Checking Acceleration, Mutation, and Its Limits

The topic's namesake concern—fact-checking acceleration—receives sustained attention as both a technical and institutional problem. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] already frames CLSB detection explicitly as a speed-up tool for time-constrained fact-checkers, but three further papers interrogate what happens *after* verification and whether verification is even the right unit of intervention. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] directly confronts the political framing of fact-checking as censorship in the wake of Meta's 2025 decision to end its Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, showing empirically that content removal was always rare (~30% of verified cases) and that fact-checkers themselves overwhelmingly favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion—while expressing sharp skepticism that Community Notes can substitute for professional verification, a skepticism grounded in concerns about coordinated manipulation that echo the CLSB dynamics documented by [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Song2025-yh]]. [[Xue2025-bp]] identifies a subtler tension within the fact-checking genre itself: fact-checks are not the dispassionate, purely informational artifacts they claim to be, but frequently deploy emotional language that shapes engagement and sentiment toward fact-checked targets—raising the question of whether corrective communication's efficacy depends on the same affective mechanisms that drive misinformation's spread in [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]. [[Ramos2026-qo]], reflecting on the 2024 Valencia floods, argues most directly that claim-level fact-checking is structurally insufficient against narratives that mutate and regenerate (the Bonaire parking-structure conspiracy escalated across multiple claim variants even after official refutation), proposing narrative monitoring and argument-checking as necessary complements—an argument that dovetails with [[Scalco2026-bd]]'s finding that voids and misinformation persist asymmetrically over time, and with [[Adam2026-tz]]'s finding that debunking alone can backfire among predisposed audiences.

## Source Effects, Toxicity, and the Wider Misinformation Ecology

A final set of papers broadens the frame beyond COVID-19 to test whether these dynamics generalize across issues and institutional contexts. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that misinformation's *source*—social media influencer versus regular user—is itself a driver of toxic audience response independent of content, with influencer-driven misinformation on sociopolitical (though not health) topics producing markedly more hostile, echo-chamber-like reactions, extending the topic's concern with amplification mechanisms from networks of accounts (as in [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Marino2023-9137f448]]) to networks of affect. [[Lieu2025-nl]], working in the climate domain, offers a useful methodological parallel to [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]]'s topic-over-source finding: it is the *content category* of misinformation (attacks on climate solutions), not the underlying rhetorical fallacy, that drives perceived veracity and political polarization—suggesting that health misinformation researchers might similarly prioritize content taxonomies over generic "fallacy" corrections. Finally, [[Rossini2026-mj]] extends the topic's implicit promise of cross-national comparison beyond the European and North American cases that dominate this collection, showing in the Brazilian 2022 election context how elite-driven disinformation transmitted via WhatsApp erodes institutional trust while mainstream news consumption stabilizes it—even among supporters of the disinformation-spreading incumbent. Read alongside [[Song2025-yh]]'s UK/US comparison and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s Latin American fact-checker interviews, this piece gestures toward the Global South comparative agenda the topic aspires to (echoing the field's stated interest in India and Nigeria) even where dedicated studies of those specific national contexts are not yet represented in this note's corpus—marking a clear direction for future filing.
