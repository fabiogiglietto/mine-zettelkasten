# Survey Measurement Validity and Comparability

## The comprehension gap underlying measurement validity

At the root of this cluster sits a deceptively simple provocation from [[Hinck2026-yj]]: survey researchers routinely assume respondents interpret questions as intended, yet almost never produce evidence for that assumption. This is less a single finding than a methodological conscience for the rest of the papers filed here. Every subsequent concern—about sample composition, about dataset comparability, about what a "trust" item actually captures—can be read as a special case of the comprehension problem: if we cannot show that respondents understand a question the way we intend, then downstream statistics about representativeness, change over time, or cross-national difference are built on an unexamined foundation.

## Sample composition, recruitment, and quality checks

A first line of inquiry asks whether online samples deliver the respondents—and the response behavior—that researchers assume they are getting. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] is the anchor study here, showing that nine opt-in online panels cannot be treated as interchangeable: panels that use demographic quotas (Lucid, Forthright) are more representative on non-demographic measures too, but at the cost of lower response validity, partly explained by heavier mobile-phone use. Critically, the paper shows that representativeness and validity are not simply traded off by fiat—two trivial front-end attention checks substantially raise validity without eroding representativeness—but that heterogeneous treatment effects, as opposed to main effects, are detectable only in the more representative samples, meaning validity fixes alone cannot substitute for compositional quality. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] extends the representativeness question to recruitment itself, using Facebook's ad infrastructure (Pixel tracking, custom-audience exclusion) to reach a niche, hard-to-survey population (Italian conspiracy believers) efficiently and cheaply—but finds that this digitally-targeted sample does not clearly differ in conspiracy endorsement from a general-population CAWI benchmark, leaving open whether platform "interest" targeting captures the attitudinal construct researchers assume it does. The large-scale surveys in [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] illustrate the stakes of getting this right at national scale: both rely on probability-based, nationally representative designs to track AI attitudes over time and across demographic subgroups, and both depend on the kind of quota/weighting infrastructure whose limits Stagnaro's opt-in comparisons expose. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] pushes sample-composition questions into new territory: an ~81,000-respondent survey of Claude.ai users is, by construction, a non-probability, highly selected sample of AI power-users, and the paper's inferences about displacement and productivity hinge on LLM-based classification of open-ended self-reports rather than traditional demographic quotas—raising the comprehension and classification-validity questions that the rest of this cluster is built around, in a context where "representativeness" means something quite different from a general-population panel.

## Beyond closed questions: scaling open-ended and AI-assisted measurement

A second thread addresses how researchers measure latent constructs at all, given that closed-ended items constrain depth while open-ended coding is costly. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes combining LLMs with paired-comparison methods to scale free-text responses into continuous latent measures, directly answering the tradeoff Hinck implicitly diagnoses: open text may better reveal how respondents actually understood a question, but only if it can be processed validly at scale. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s use of Claude-powered classifiers to infer occupation and displacement concern from open-ended survey text is a live application of exactly this approach, with the same unresolved question of whether model-inferred codes track respondents' intended meaning. [[Messing2026-sl63]] supplies the methodological infrastructure needed to evaluate such pipelines rigorously: by extending total-survey-error logic into a "Total Evaluation Error" framework for LLM-as-judge and annotation pipelines, it shows that naive standard errors ignore judge- and prompt-level variance, that this hidden error does not shrink with more data, and that uncorrected pipelines are vulnerable to a form of benchmark-gaming analogous to p-hacking. Read alongside DiGiuseppe and the Anthropic report, this paper reframes "does the respondent understand the question" as a problem that now recurs one level up the pipeline—"does the LLM judge/classifier understand the response"—with the same evidentiary burden Hinck demands for human comprehension.

## Cross-national and longitudinal comparability of trust measures

A third thread interrogates whether trust measures mean the same thing across countries, time, and datasets. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] is the centerpiece: pooling WVS/EVS and Reuters Digital News Report data across 115 countries and four decades, it documents a real but non-universal decline in trust in news, shows the decline predates the internet, and—crucially for comparability—demonstrates that baseline trust levels diverge sharply depending on whether a survey asks about "confidence in the press" or "trust in news," and that between-country and between-dataset variance dwarfs the temporal trend itself. This is a direct empirical demonstration of the comparability risks Hinck warns about in the abstract. [[Schemer2026-mh|Schemer2026-mh]] operationalizes the same worry for a different construct, running a specification-curve analysis across 504 ways of measuring media partisan slant and polarization and finding that methodological choices—audience-based versus content-based slant scores, bipolar versus disaggregated polarization measures—substantially change coefficient magnitude and can even mask directional asymmetries, even though they rarely flip the sign of the relationship. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit at the level of a widely used measurement instrument itself (NewsGuard), showing that continuous trustworthiness scores are comparatively stable since 2022 but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications can distort findings dramatically when single high-profile sources cross threshold, and that political-orientation labels are unevenly available across countries—a direct caution about treating a "validated" measurement tool as equally valid everywhere it is applied. [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] approaches comparability from the respondent's side rather than the instrument's: interviewing users in Brazil, India, the UK, and the US about what they consider "news" at all, it finds a shared "core" definition alongside a diverging "expanded" definition, implying that even a seemingly stable construct like "news" is not uniformly understood across cultural and national contexts—exactly the kind of comprehension evidence Hinck argues is missing from most survey practice.

## Case studies: measuring trust in an AI-mediated information environment

A final set of papers can be read as applied laboratories for these measurement concerns, converging on trust as a construct that is unusually slippery to operationalize. [[Dubey2026-bl]]'s chatbot-trust studies literally encounter the comprehension problem mid-analysis: a negatively worded trust item cross-loads onto a separate "perceived risk" factor and has to be dropped to preserve model fit, a small but telling instance of wording effects distorting a latent trust measure. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] both show that "trust in AI-produced news" fragments depending on how the disclosure is framed—generic AI-generated/human-written labels versus task-specific disclosures—with Mattis explicitly arguing that coarse labels used in prior survey work obscure real variation in audience response by journalistic task. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a further distinction, finding that trust in AI-generated search results and trust in AI-produced news are not interchangeable constructs within the same representative sample, reinforcing the broader lesson that "trust" cannot be measured as a single undifferentiated item without losing meaningful structure. Finally, [[Ulloa2024-jm]], though focused on behavioral trace data rather than self-report, rounds out the cluster's logic: it shows that even "objective" digital measures of news consumption are substantially distorted by the measurement environment itself (in-situ versus ex-situ scraping), and that these distortions are non-random across content categories—a non-survey analogue of the same message running through every paper here: measurement choices are never neutral, and validity and comparability must be demonstrated, not assumed.
