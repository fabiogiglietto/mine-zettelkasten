# Survey Measurement Validity and Comparability

## The comprehension problem at the root

Any claim built on survey data rests on an assumption that is rarely tested: that respondents interpret a question the way researchers intend. [[Hinck2026-yj]] makes this gap explicit, arguing that survey methodology has no routine practice for demonstrating respondent comprehension and that this is a validity problem hiding in plain sight. This concern frames much of what follows in this cluster: before asking whether samples are representative or whether trust items travel across countries and decades, one has to ask whether "the" question being asked is stable across respondents at all. Several papers here implicitly test this assumption under pressure — by generating open-ended text that must be interpreted (whether by humans or models), or by observing that identical wording produces wildly different substantive conclusions depending on who answers and how.

## Who is answering, and how well

A second cluster interrogates not question wording but sample composition and response quality. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] is the most systematic treatment, benchmarking nine opt-in online panels and showing a genuine trade-off: platforms with demographic quotas look more representative but show lower response validity (more inattentiveness, more mobile-driven noise), while "professional" high-validity panels skew away from population benchmarks. Critically, the paper shows that representativeness and attentiveness are *not* substitutable — post-stratification weights can restore main effects even in bad samples but cannot recover heterogeneous treatment effects, meaning the sample you choose constrains which findings are even detectable. This has direct implications for the wave of applied survey experiments collected elsewhere in this set — [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy, [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] on AI persuasion, [[Dubey2026-bl]] on chatbot trust, [[Hove2026-sl49]] on AI-image detection, [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] on AI-disclosure effects — all of which depend on panel quality assumptions that [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] shows cannot be taken for granted. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] approaches the same representativeness problem from the recruitment side, using Facebook's ad infrastructure to reach a niche, stigmatized population (conspiracy believers) more cheaply than panel vendors, but finding that even a carefully instrumented procedure (Pixel tracking, conversion optimization, deduplication) cannot guarantee that the people reached are substantively distinctive from a general-population benchmark — echoing Stagnaro et al.'s point that efficient recruitment and valid measurement are separate achievements. Large descriptive surveys such as [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] illustrate the stakes: cross-national and over-time claims about AI attitudes depend entirely on consistent sampling frames and item wording being maintained release after release, which is precisely what is hardest to verify from the outside.

## Trust measures across time, countries, and datasets

The comparability problem sharpens when the object of measurement is "trust" itself, a notoriously elastic construct. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] is the anchor text here, combining WVS/EVS and Reuters DNR data across 115 countries and four decades to show a real but non-universal decline in trust in news — while explicitly flagging that different datasets ask different questions ("confidence in the press" vs. "trust in news") and that baseline levels diverge by instrument in ways that can be mistaken for substantive cross-national difference. This is a direct empirical demonstration of the longitudinal/cross-national comparability hazard that [[Hinck2026-yj]] raises in the abstract. [[Schemer2026-mh]] provides a parallel demonstration within a single country and time point: using specification-curve analysis on 504 ways of operationalizing media partisanship and polarization, the authors show that measurement choices (audience-based vs. content-based slant scores, bipolar vs. disaggregated polarization indices) substantially change effect magnitudes and significance even when they rarely flip the sign — a cautionary case study in how much "researcher degrees of freedom" in operationalizing a trust-adjacent construct can shape a field's apparent consensus. [[Luhring2025-od]] extends this logic to an increasingly popular *input* to trust- and misinformation-related measurement: the NewsGuard database. The audit shows the ratings themselves are reasonably stable since 2022, but binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classification can reverse substantive conclusions when single sources cross a threshold, and that coverage differences across snapshots and countries — not real rating change — drive most apparent cross-time or cross-country divergence. Together these three papers suggest a general diagnostic: differences in reported trust or partisanship across studies are often artifacts of instrument, dataset vintage, or operationalization rather than of underlying attitudes.

## New instruments, new validity questions

A final strand examines tools proposed to work around these problems, and shows they import validity questions of their own. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes LLM-paired comparisons to scale open-ended responses into continuous latent measures, directly responding to the closed-ended/open-ended trade-off that underlies much of the comprehension problem. [[Brown2025-jk]] and the Anthropic survey report [[UnknownUnknown-db]] show both the promise and the hazard of this move: LLMs can annotate subjective responses or infer respondent attributes (occupation, concerns) from free text at scale, but agreement with intended labels is overwhelmingly driven by item difficulty (label entropy) rather than by model or prompt choice, and large shares of respondents remain unclassifiable even with inference. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] offers a complementary caution from the digital-trace side: behavioral measures assumed to be more "objective" than self-report (scraped browsing content) are themselves subject to systematic, non-random measurement error driven by collection environment, undermining any simple substitution of trace data for self-report as a validity check.

## A field testing itself on trust in AI

Read together, the recent wave of AI-trust and AI-attitude surveys in this collection — [[Suk2026-ai]]'s call for a multilevel trust framework, [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] on trust in AI search vs. AI news, [[Mattis2026-gu]] and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] on disclosure effects, [[Dubey2026-bl]] on chatbot acceptance, and the persuasion experiments of [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], and [[Voelkel2026-lc]] — constitute a live test case for everything argued above. Each introduces new trust items, new disclosure wordings, and new sample recruitment strategies without a shared measurement baseline, reproducing exactly the proliferation of ad hoc operationalizations that [[Schemer2026-mh]] and [[Fletcher2026-lv]] show can quietly drive divergent findings. The methodological lessons of this topic — demand evidence of comprehension, scrutinize sample composition rather than assuming quotas equal representativeness, and treat question wording and dataset choice as substantive variables rather than background noise — apply with particular urgency to this fast-moving subfield, where "trust in AI" risks becoming as contested and incommensurable a measure as "trust in news" already is.
