# Survey Measurement Validity and Comparability

## The unexamined assumption: do respondents understand what we ask?

The most basic challenge in this literature is also the most neglected: survey researchers routinely assume respondents interpret questions as intended, without ever testing that assumption. [[Hinck2026-yj]] names this gap directly, arguing that comprehension evidence should be a standard deliverable of survey design rather than an afterthought. This concern reverberates through several empirical papers in the set that were not designed as methods papers but that stumble onto comprehension problems anyway. [[Allcott2017-yz]], for instance, includes placebo (never-circulated) fake-news headlines in its post-election survey and finds that respondents "recall" and "believe" these fabricated items at almost the same rate as real fake stories—direct evidence that self-reports of exposure are contaminated by something other than accurate recall of a well-understood stimulus. Similarly, [[Berlinski2023-rj]]'s finding that fact-check tweets fail to restore "confidence in elections" while leaving "support for democracy" untouched suggests respondents treat these as cleanly separable constructs, which is itself an empirical claim about comprehension that the study is able to probe only because it varies the stimulus experimentally. These cases illustrate why Hinck's call matters: without designed-in comprehension checks, it is hard to know whether null or surprising results reflect substantive processes or interpretive slippage.

## Sample composition, quality control, and representativeness in opt-in online research

A second cluster addresses who ends up answering surveys at all, and what quality-control choices do to representativeness. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] is the anchor study here, offering a systematic nine-platform comparison and showing that representativeness and response validity trade off against each other, that demographic quotas buy representativeness at the cost of attentiveness, and—crucially—that simple attention checks can be added without sacrificing representativeness, while post-stratification weights fix main effects but not heterogeneous treatment effects. This last point has direct implications for every substantive study in this topic that relies on subgroup analysis from non-probability panels. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] pushes on the recruitment side rather than the weighting side, testing whether Facebook's ad platform can replace panel-based CAWI for reaching niche, stigmatized populations (conspiracy believers); the ambiguous result—efficient recruitment but inconclusive evidence that interest-based targeting captures genuinely more extreme respondents—echoes Stagnaro et al.'s warning that sample composition and construct validity are entangled in ways simple cost/conversion metrics cannot resolve. [[Oswald2025-km]] extends the representativeness problem beyond the survey instrument itself, arguing that the visible "tip of the iceberg" on social media—dominated by a hyperactive minority—distorts both citizens' and researchers' inferences about public opinion, a structural analogue to the opt-in sample problem: in both cases, easily observed respondents (whether survey-takers or posters) are systematically unlike the silent majority they are taken to represent. [[Guess2019-ym]] offers a partial corrective by linking panel survey data directly to respondents' actual Facebook sharing behavior, demonstrating how combining self-report with behavioral trace data can validate (or overturn) demographic assumptions—here, revealing that age, not general shareiness, drives fake-news sharing, a finding invisible to designs relying on self-reported behavior alone.

## Trust measures across contexts: wording, datasets, and the comparability problem

The topic's third strand concerns whether "trust" measured in one place, time, or instrument means the same thing elsewhere. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] is the central contribution, combining WVS/EVS and Reuters DNR data across 115 countries and four decades to show that the apparent decline in trust in news is real but that baseline levels and even direction of trend diverge sharply by dataset and by country's media-freedom context—differences the authors trace partly to literal question wording ("the press" vs. "news") rather than to underlying attitudes. This directly operationalizes the topic's concern with cross-national and longitudinal comparability and should be read alongside [[Schemer2026-mh]], which demonstrates via specification-curve analysis that methodological choices in operationalizing *both* independent variables (media partisanship scores) and dependent variables (affective vs. belief polarization) swing coefficient magnitudes by nearly sevenfold, even though sign rarely flips—a cautionary complement to Fletcher et al.'s cross-national findings, showing the same fragility within a single country and election cycle. [[Luhring2025-od]] examines a related measurement infrastructure question: NewsGuard's source-trustworthiness ratings, widely used as an input to political communication research, are shown to be reasonably stable as continuous scores but highly distortive when dichotomized, and systematically biased by uneven country coverage—another instance in which an instrument's apparent neutrality masks construct-validity problems that ripple into downstream substantive claims.

A adjacent wave of papers extends the trust-measurement question to generative AI, effectively asking whether existing survey constructs of media/institutional trust travel to a new object. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework explicitly borrowing from the mass-media and social-media trust literatures, implicitly conceding that trust in AI cannot simply be bolted onto legacy survey items without reconsideration. The empirical AI-trust studies in this set—[[Fattorini2026-bo]] (Italy), [[Gottfried2026-ww]] (US/Pew), [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]]—each use differently worded instruments (self-assessed "informedness," task-specific AI disclosure conjoints, quality ratings, chatbot trust scales) to probe related but non-identical constructs, and together they illustrate the comparability problem in miniature: Gottfried's blunt societal-impact items, Mattis's task-specific disclosure framing, and Gilardi's credibility/readability/expertise battery are all plausible operationalizations of "trust in AI-produced content," yet their divergent designs make direct cross-study comparison hazardous in exactly the way Fletcher et al. and Schemer et al. warn against for trust-in-news measures more generally.

## New instruments for measurement: LLMs as annotators and scalers

A final strand considers whether large language models can serve as measurement tools themselves, raising fresh validity questions. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] proposes pairing LLMs with paired-comparison methods to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent traits, directly addressing the classic trade-off between closed-ended items (constrained but comparable) and open-ended coding (rich but unscalable)—a methodological bridge relevant to every trust-measurement debate above. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates the validity of LLMs as annotators more skeptically, showing that apparent demographic "bias" in LLM-human agreement is dataset-specific and dwarfed by item difficulty (human disagreement itself), a useful corrective against overinterpreting small demographic effects as evidence of systematic unfairness. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] shows the applied stakes: Anthropic's survey of 81,000 Claude users relies on LLM classifiers to infer occupation and sentiment from open text, compounding standard self-report concerns (comprehension, representativeness of a self-selected user base) with new uncertainty about classifier-inferred constructs. Finally, [[Ulloa2024-jm]] reframes the comparability problem for behavioral-trace measurement rather than self-report: it shows that ex-situ web scraping—often used to validate or substitute for survey-based measures of news exposure—introduces its own large, non-random distortions, a reminder that the turn toward "objective" digital-trace measures does not escape the validity and comparability problems documented throughout this topic, but merely relocates them.
