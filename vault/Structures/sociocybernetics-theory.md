# Sociocybernetics and Social Systems Theory

## From Cybernetic Foundations to Observing Media Systems

The thread running through this topic is a commitment to treating social and media phenomena as *systems*—self-organizing, observer-dependent, irreducible to the intentions of any single actor—rather than as simple chains of cause and effect. This is the inheritance of sociocybernetics (RC51) filtered through Luhmann's systems theory and second-order cybernetics: the recognition that what counts as "information," "agency," or "truth" is always relative to an observing system, and that complex social wholes exhibit emergent dynamics not derivable from their parts. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] makes this foundation explicit, drawing directly on von Foerster and Bateson to argue that "fake news" cannot be understood through the intent of a content creator alone. Information is "a difference that makes a difference" only for a particular observer; what matters is the chain of judgments by which propagators—not just injectors—decide to pass something along. The paper's micro/meso/macro model, in which global cascades of misinformation are emergent and autonomous even though composed of heterogeneous individual acts, is paradigmatic sociocybernetic reasoning: it refuses to locate the phenomenon at any single level and instead asks how levels couple.

## Macro-Structural Theories of the Network Society

At the broadest scale, [[Castells2009-sle8]] supplies the macro-sociological backbone for this body of work: the claim that digital networking technologies have produced a qualitatively new social morphology, the "network society," organized around a space of flows and timeless time that reconfigure economy, culture, and power. Castells' insistence that inherited industrial-era categories cannot grasp this new structure resonates with the systems-theoretic premise that genuinely new forms of social differentiation require new observational vocabularies—a premise [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] operationalizes at the infrastructural level, arguing that Internet architecture itself must be redesigned around social-capital properties (trust, reciprocity, reach) rather than purely technical metrics, since the "prosumer" blurs the consumer/producer distinction constitutive of older network models. Both papers treat technological and social structure as co-constitutive, a stance that anticipates the "mutual shaping" sociotechnical lens later deployed by [[Starbird2019-qv]].

## Feedback, Complexity, and the Limits of Metaphor

A second cluster interrogates popular metaphors used to describe digital-media dynamics and finds them wanting precisely because they flatten systemic complexity into linear or single-mechanism stories. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] replaces "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles" with a typology of communicative feedback loops, explicitly invoking Ashby and cybernetic equilibrium-seeking to explain why reinforcing dynamics do not inevitably produce runaway radicalization—negative feedback (users correcting over-personalized recommendations) coexists with positive feedback (fringe groups escalating for attention), and system-level behavior cannot be inferred from any single loop. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] performs an analogous operation on misinformation discourse, showing how the "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" metaphors each capture only one level of a four-level complex adaptive system (micro, meso-social, meso-platform, macro) and that interventions calibrated to one level can backfire at another. Both papers share with [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] a refusal of single-cause, single-level explanation and a call for multilevel, iterative, portfolio-style modes of observation—the sociocybernetic method applied to policy design.

## Reconfiguring the Observed System: Post-Social Media

[[Tornberg2026-lc]] extends this systems-observational stance to the object itself, arguing that the very system these frameworks were built to observe—"social media" as a social-graph-based circulation structure—is dissolving into three divergent post-social formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, AI-mediated communication). This is less a rejection of the earlier frameworks than a demand that the boundaries of the system under observation be redrawn, a move fully consonant with systems theory's emphasis on system/environment differentiation as historically contingent rather than fixed. Read alongside [[Starbird2019-qv]], which shows how strategic information operations exploit the participatory, loosely coordinated texture of online crowds—blurring orchestrated and organic action in ways that resist clean "bot vs. authentic user" categorization—the post-social turn appears as a continuation of a long-standing difficulty: the social system under study keeps outrunning the analytic categories built to observe it.

## Extending Social Systems Theory to Machine Agency

The most recent papers push social systems theory to its emerging frontier: artificial agents as participants in, and possibly constituents of, social systems. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes the demarcation problem directly in social-theoretic terms, arguing that agency is not an intrinsic property of a system but a socially granted and monitored status—a relational, practice-based account that refuses to settle the AI-agency question by inspecting internal mechanisms alone. [[Ng2026-og]] operationalizes a cognate intuition formally, proposing that multi-agent AI systems be modeled with social theory as a "structural prior," via a triple of information-exchange, influence-dynamics, and network-interaction functions, and empirically demonstrating phenomena—strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability—that are recognizably systems-theoretic (emergence, structural coupling, instability of equilibrium) rather than artifacts of individual-agent design. Together these two papers mark the point at which sociocybernetics, long used to observe human social and media systems, is being explicitly repurposed to govern and evaluate machine-populated social systems—closing the loop back to the foundational insight, voiced first in [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], that agency, truth, and information are never properties of isolated units but always artifacts of an observing, interacting system.
