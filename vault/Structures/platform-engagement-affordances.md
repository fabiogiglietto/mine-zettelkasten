# Platform Engagement and Affordances

## Theorizing Platform Affordances: From Networked to Clustered Publics

The conceptual backbone of this literature begins with boyd's foundational account of social network sites as "networked publics" [[Slack1783508184-sld3]], whose affordances—persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability—generate the now-canonical dynamics of invisible audiences and context collapse. Marwick and boyd extend this into the phenomenology of use, showing how Twitter users manage context collapse by conjuring an "imagined audience" and performing strategic, branded authenticity [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. Two decades on, Gerbaudo argues that this entire paradigm has been superseded: TikTok's recommender-driven "For You" feed replaces people-centric networked publics with item-centric "clustered publics," assembled from implicit behavioral signals rather than explicit ties, producing depersonalization, opacity, and subcultural fragmentation [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]. Helmond's retrospective on "platformization" complements this shift at the infrastructural level, arguing that the retirement of Facebook's Like button marks the end of the open-web social-graph paradigm and the beginning of an AI-centered phase in which platforms no longer need explicit behavioral signals at all [[Helmond2026-ll]]. Kakavand's multi-platform study of German far-right networks operationalizes affordance theory empirically, showing that connectivity, replicability, and scalability are relational outcomes co-produced by platform design and user practice rather than fixed properties—actors do not reproduce identical networks across Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram but adapt to each [[Kakavand2026-kt]]. Chadwick's older "hybrid media system" framework anticipates this relational, non-deterministic stance, insisting that newer media logics layer onto rather than replace older ones, and that power remains unevenly distributed despite expanded participation [[Chadwick2015-ii]]. Manovich's analysis of generative AI as a creative "medium that thinks" extends the affordance question into AI-native production, arguing that probabilistic generation constitutes a structural trade-off between variability and control that will reshape what gets made and circulated on platforms [[Manovich2026-ih]].

## Emotional Affordances and Reaction Mechanics

A second cluster examines how lightweight emotional signals—reactions, not just likes—structure visibility. Anwar's systematic review of 64 studies on Facebook's Reactions feature establishes the baseline finding that anger is a disproportionate engagement driver in political contexts, while lifestyle content skews positive, and that reactions function as "paralinguistic digital affordances" enabling nuanced, low-effort emotional expression [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]. Kalsnes and colleagues test this pattern comparatively across Scandinavian elections, finding that populist parties are especially adept at eliciting "Angry" reactions and that anger (unlike "Love") robustly predicts sharing—evidence that affective affordances are not neutral but asymmetrically reward confrontational political styles [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]. Xue's study of fact-checking posts extends the emotion-engagement nexus into the corrective genre itself, showing that even ostensibly objective fact-checks lean on emotionality to drive engagement, troubling fact-checking's normative self-image [[Xue2025-bp]].

## Algorithmic Curation and Political Effects: The Field-Experiment Turn

The largest and most methodologically ambitious cluster traces a decade-long argument about whether algorithmic curation—as opposed to network composition and individual choice—causally shapes political attitudes. Bakshy et al.'s foundational Facebook study finds that individual clicking, not the News Feed algorithm, is the primary filter against cross-cutting content [[Bakshy2015-rn]], a conclusion echoed by Flaxman et al.'s web-browsing analysis showing direct visits to mainstream outlets dwarf social/search-driven segregation [[Flaxman2016-lm]] and by Messing and Westwood's experiments showing social endorsements swamp partisan source cues in news selection [[Messing2014-jc]]. This "modest effects" consensus is substantially reinforced by the 2023 Meta collaborative studies: Guess et al. find that switching Facebook/Instagram to reverse-chronological feeds reshapes exposure and engagement but not downstream polarization or knowledge [[Guess2023-ur]]; a companion study finds removing reshares cuts exposure to political news and untrustworthy sources yet barely moves attitudes [[Guess2023-ai]]; Nyhan et al. find reducing like-minded exposure by a third changes feed composition without denting polarization [[Nyhan2023-gb]]; and Gonzalez-Bailon et al. document that segregation is nonetheless real, asymmetric, and concentrated in a homogeneously conservative "corner" of the ecosystem where most misinformation circulates [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]. Brown's YouTube audit similarly disentangles supply from demand, finding only mild, user-driven echo chambers, strong algorithmically-induced "rabbit holes," and no radicalization pathway—though a platform-wide conservative tilt [[Brown2026-br]].

Against this null-effects baseline, a newer wave of X/Twitter-focused work reopens the question by varying *what kind* of algorithmic exposure is tested. Gauthier et al.'s field experiment on X's "For You" feed finds a genuine, asymmetric causal effect: switching the algorithm on shifts attitudes rightward and the effect persists even after switching it off, because algorithmic exposure changes which accounts users follow [[Gauthier2026-iq]]—a mechanism that helps explain why Meta's studies found nulls while X's do not. Efstratiou et al. complement this with an audit of account-level visibility during the Musk transition, showing apparent right-wing algorithmic favoritism is largely explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk himself rather than ideology per se [[Efstratiou2025-gs]]. Bouchaud et al. push the mechanism one level deeper, reverse-engineering X's embedding space to show the recommender inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' Left-Right ideology [[Bouchaud2026-lr]]. Brady et al.'s Registered Report on Bluesky closes the loop normatively, showing engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distort perceived social norms (not just attitudes directly), while a custom "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment [[Brady2026-ln]]. Jaidka et al.'s elite-blocking experiment offers a complementary structural intervention, showing that merely hiding political elites' posts—not political content generally—reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing [[Jaidka2026-sl34]]. Wan's study of political efficacy and Google Discover clicks adds an individual-differences lens to this algorithmic-exposure literature, asking how psychological dispositions condition selection behavior within fully algorithmic feeds [[Wan2026-ai]].

## Virality, Diffusion, and the Limits of the "Viral" Narrative

A parallel strand interrogates virality itself as a diffusion phenomenon. Vosoughi et al.'s landmark Twitter analysis finds falsehood spreads farther, faster, and deeper than truth, driven by human novelty-seeking rather than bots [[Vosoughi2018-at]], while Del Vicario et al. model Facebook cascades through homogeneity and polarization within echo chambers, arguing algorithmic fixes alone cannot address a fundamentally social-psychological dynamic [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]]. Gonzalez-Bailon et al.'s billion-reshare analysis of the 2020 election reinforces that mass virality is rare and unevenly distributed, with misinformation disproportionately represented among the most-viral content [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]. Mosleh et al. and Dahlke et al. both complicate single-platform generalizations: the former shows partisan/low-quality news engagement diverges across seven platforms [[Mosleh2024-op]], while the latter's "embedded referrer" framework demonstrates that conventional direct-referral methods undercount platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure by 1.5–15×, even as platform removal would disproportionately harm high-quality news access too [[Dahlke2026-sl34]]. Green and Zhu's "curation bubbles" paper reframes the whole debate at the level of measurement, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning networked curation—not algorithmic ranking per se—manufactures apparent homogeneity [[Green2025-ap]]. Oswald and Jungherr push this further into a meta-critique: because a small minority produces most visible political content, both citizens and researchers systematically misread the "tip of the iceberg" as representative public opinion [[Oswald2025-km]]. Schulz et al. supply the mechanism behind this distortion, showing "false polarization" arises from self-selection in posting (stronger partisans post more) rather than genuine attitude divergence [[Schulz2026-ts]]. Graham's case study of #IStandWithPutin closes this section by showing how propaganda campaigns exploit, rather than evade, the very platform infrastructures—hashtags, algorithmic amplification, verification—built to support credible information [[Graham2025-gp]].

## Engineering Virality: Creators, Propaganda, and Platform Grammars

Where the previous section treats virality as something that happens, a further set of papers treats it as something increasingly *engineered*. Tang's study of Chinese "AI slop" creators shows virality being industrialized—benchmarked, tested, and reproduced via matrix-account systems, challenging what it means to be a "creator" under generative AI [[Tang2026-gu]]. Zhao et al. analyze "hashtag hijacking" as a deliberate tactic combining attention capture, narrative substitution, and bot amplification in opinion contests [[Zhao2025-ny]]. Tuters' study of the "Great Reset" conspiracy develops the most ambitious theoretical apparatus here, arguing that platform grammars generate conspiratorial alignment through patterned adjacency and modular reuse *before* coherent narratives form—an infrastructural, rather than belief-centered, account of conspiracism that extends Hofstadter's "paranoid style" into a "high-tech" register [[Tuters2026-vo]]. Kasianenko et al. extend this infrastructural lens to search engines, finding Google's AI Overviews inconsistently moderate conspiratorial query framings, sometimes legitimizing newer conspiracy theories by presenting them as "debates" [[Kasianenko2026-tn]]. Bosch and Burgess identify sound as an under-examined, platform-specific vector for TikTok disinformation about the Ukraine invasion [[Bosch2024-hj]], while Bouchafra et al. show the Sweden Democrats deliberately tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric to TikTok's memetic, youth-oriented virality mechanics even over their larger Facebook/X followings [[Bouchafra2026-ts]]. Rodarte's study of Brazilian parliamentarians documents a "democratic cost" to this logic: politicians believe (correctly, per the computational evidence) that congressional substance is penalized by engagement metrics while antagonism and news-cycle alignment are rewarded, reshaping political communication toward confrontation [[Rodarte2026-sl16]].

## Influencer Labor, Toxicity, and Audience Culture

A related cluster examines how ordinary users and creators internalize and perform these engagement logics. Volpe's ethnography of Italian second-hand-fashion micro-influencers reframes "authentic" care-based content as a survival strategy under platform production demands, intertwining ethics and self-branding [[Volpe2026-um]]. Di Domenico et al. show that misinformation sourced from influencers (versus regular users) generates distinctively more frequent and homogeneous toxicity, because credibility and parasocial bonds convert into collective antagonism [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]—a finding resonant with Cabbuag's account of TikTok "dogshows" in the Philippines, where platform affordances actively amplify legal-but-harmful humor among Gen Z influencer audiences [[Cabbuag2024-me]]. Wedel's qualitative study of young Germans' "informativeness" judgments complicates institutional definitions of news, showing audiences extend journalistic norms to influencers and weigh platform-specific selection logics (self-selected on YouTube, actor-mediated on Instagram, algorithmic on TikTok) in deciding what counts as useful content [[Wedel2026-ix]].

## Governing Visibility: Moderation, Reduction, and Regulation

A final cluster turns from description to governance. Gillespie's conceptual intervention argues that "reduction"—algorithmic demotion without removal—has been systematically under-theorized relative to removal/banning, despite being platforms' dominant tool for governing virality, and that it concentrates curatorial power while evading transparency [[Gillespie2022-jx]]. Several papers operationalize this call for accountability: Ahuja et al. bridge HCI dark-patterns scholarship with the EU Digital Services Act, mapping 59 dark patterns onto three legal autonomy-violation types [[Ahuja2025-ku]]; Tonneau et al. use DSA transparency data to document stark cross-lingual disparities in human moderation allocation, with Global South languages chronically under-resourced [[Tonneau2025-bv]]; and McNally et al. show that Facebook's News Feed, far from being an unknowable "black box," exhibits detectable, lagged algorithmic effects on hard-news engagement—evidence marshaled explicitly in support of DSA-style systemic audits [[McNally2025-dn]]. Gagrcin and Masur's integrative review of 169 studies on "algorithm literacy" finds the construct itself remains conceptually fragmented, limiting cumulative knowledge about how users understand and contest these systems [[Gagrcin2024-dl]]. Mannocci et al. contribute a methodological corrective to coordination-detection research, showing single-modality or naively flattened approaches miss much coordinated inauthentic behavior that multiplex community detection captures [[Mannocci2025-ig]]. Ducci's study of Google News as health-information gatekeeper for Facebook shows engagement is driven more by topic (celebrity vs. technical) than by source credibility, illustrating how algorithmic aggregation and audience affect jointly shape visibility even outside overtly political contexts [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]].

## Platform Succession and Decline

Closing the arc, two papers examine what happens when platform affordances erode or users seek exit. Bruns traces Twitter's historical arc from prosocial, flat-network public-debate space to its "enshittification" under Musk, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) largely clone its form without resolving its underlying moderation and governance failures [[Bruns2026-yv]]. Wang et al.'s empirical study of academic Twitter-to-Mastodon migration bears this out: even a highly motivated, well-networked cohort largely failed to sustain migration, with retention driven less by internal network cohesion than by field-specific servers and diverse cross-server engagement—evidence that platform affordances for discoverability and community, not just ideology or outrage at ownership, govern whether engagement endures [[Wang2026-ub]].
