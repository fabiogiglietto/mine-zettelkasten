# Platform Data Governance & Transparency

## Structure

At the center of this cluster sits a single infrastructural fact: platforms control the instruments by which they can be studied, and every methodological or normative claim researchers make is downstream of that control. The notes trace three interlocking arcs — (1) the historical collapse and re-regulation of data access, (2) the empirical auditing of specific platform mechanisms (feeds, thresholds, moderation, ad delivery) once access is obtained, and (3) the political-economic critique of what this dependency does to knowledge production itself.

## From the "Wild West" to the walled garden, and back to law

A strong historical through-line runs from [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s founding observation that "platform" is a discursive shield allowing firms to disclaim editorial responsibility, through [[Freelon2018-ao]]'s naming of the "post-API age" after Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure, to [[Bruns2019-nr]]'s "APIcalypse" account of how the Cambridge Analytica scandal was instrumentalized to shut out critical scholarship. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this trajectory in more granular terms — laissez-faire, authentication, academic cooperation, pay-to-play — showing the same pattern (Twitter/X, Reddit, TikTok) recurring across platforms. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya eulogize the "Golden Age" of the Twitter API in bibliometric and reflective registers respectively, while [[Yang2026-tq]] supplies the broadest empirical confirmation: social-media-based social science publishing plateaued and declined exactly when API restrictions bit, with disciplines and platforms shifting unevenly (TikTok/YouTube rising, Twitter/Facebook falling).

Into this vacuum steps the EU's Digital Services Act, especially Article 40, reframed by [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] and [[Ohme2026-nv]] as a shift from "independence by permission" to a binding *right* of vetted-researcher access — though one still riddled with friction. [[Pierri2026-ib]], [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[Philipp2026-tl]] catalogue the resulting landscape of APIs, portals, and legal channels, converging on the diagnosis that price, proficiency, and permission jointly gatekeep who can actually do this research, with Global South and under-resourced researchers systematically excluded (echoed structurally in [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]]'s account of data colonialism). [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] extend this into the operational register: "clean room" environments (Meta Content Library, TDM Studio) and CrowdTangle's own API architecture don't just limit *access* but actively shape *what can be seen* — a "Seeing like an API/clean room" epistemics that privileges code-literate, quantitative, single-platform work over qualitative, longitudinal, or cross-platform designs. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows this contestation is not merely technical but discursive: "data quality" itself is a politically negotiated concept in the DSA rulemaking process, mobilized differently by academics, NGOs, and platforms (Meta notably inverting the researcher argument to claim access would be futile). [[Crosset2026-mq]] zooms out further, reading DSA-style transparency mandates as a form of "security bureaucratisation" — proceduralized governance-at-a-distance rather than direct content control.

## Auditing the machinery once you're inside

A second cluster uses whatever access remains to interrogate specific platform mechanisms, often exposing artifacts that platforms' own framing obscures. The Meta URL Shares Dataset receives the most sustained scrutiny: [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] independently show that its 100-public-share censoring threshold — not differential-privacy noise — massively distorts fake-news prevalence estimates and produces spurious cross-country/temporal convergence traceable to feed-ranking changes (MSI) rather than real-world events. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the dataset's use comparatively to Germany/France/Italy, while [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] use later iterations (the Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset) to show Facebook actively curates reach via partisan penalties and journalistic-quality rewards that fluctuate with political moments like the 2020 "break the glass" interventions — directly undercutting the "neutral conduit" framing that [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[Gillespie2022-jx]] critique at the conceptual level. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] applies the same forensic, breakpoint-detection logic to Meta's political-content-reduction policy, finding its Italian rollout preceded Meta's announced global timeline by ten months and was never disclosed in DSA compliance reports — a direct empirical demonstration of Gillespie's "reduction" thesis and of the transparency deficits [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] describe structurally.

Parallel audits target other infrastructures: [[McNally2025-dn]] shows Facebook's News Feed algorithm differentially and detectably affects hard-news engagement, arguing the "black box" metaphor is overstated and that DSA Article 40(4) could formalize such auditing. [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] stress-test YouTube's and TikTok's research APIs and find them "forgetful by design" or simply broken for systemic-risk research, while [[Jurg2025-ur]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[Karo2026-dn]], and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] use available (if imperfect) transparency data to document inconsistent labeling, moderation blind spots by language, and the persistence of extremist and manosphere content despite formal removal policies. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to the representativeness problem directly by building genuinely random-sample tools (TubeStats/TokStats) as an alternative to platform-curated APIs. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] turn the same forensic energy on X, showing its recommender system inadvertently encodes political ideology in a linear embedding direction, and that Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing (often electorally consequential) content by design — a finding [[Renault2025-uh]] complements by showing partisan asymmetries in flagging persist even under crowdsourced moderation, undercutting Musk/Zuckerberg's stated rationale for abandoning professional fact-checking (a shift [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows in practice rarely results in removal anyway).

## The political economy of studying platforms

A third strand steps back to interrogate what this entire dependency does to the research enterprise. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] quantify industry entanglement in high-profile social media science, finding roughly half of papers have undisclosed industry ties concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged scientists and editors, with topics skewed toward user-blame (misinformation sharing) over platform-scale dynamics — a bias [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames through explicit analogy to tobacco/pharma agnotology. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes this dynamic, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models that platforms manage through behavioral (not truth-based) interventions they can turn on and off depending on political backlash — precisely the retreat documented empirically by [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s three-phase periodization of election-policy rollback, and rhetorically analyzed in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]'s account of how Meta's Newsroom externalizes blame, and [[De2026-ld]]'s concept of "changecraft" as platforms' discursive management of constant policy churn. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows that even Meta's own definitions of "coordinated inauthentic behavior" have narrowed over time in ways that let manufactured-attention networks persist unlabeled — reinforcing the theme that platform self-description is not a stable analytic category.

Several essays situate the whole cluster within larger reflections on what "platform" and "social" now mean: [[Helmond2026-ll]] argues platformization is entering an AI-centered phase that makes the open-web signals (like/URL-share data) this literature relies on increasingly obsolete, while [[Boyd2026-op]] proposes retiring "social media" for "parasocial media" given the shift from reciprocal to broadcast/algorithmic engagement — a shift that helps explain why data like URL-shares or Like-button signals are vanishing as objects of study. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supplies the encompassing theoretical frame: platforms as contested infrastructures where private and public values collide, a frame [[Bechmann2026-dr]] updates for the DSA era by calling for research on platform *collective behavior as democratic infrastructure* rather than narrow causal-effects paradigms. Case studies of real-world political and epistemic stakes — [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s Brazil X-ban "sorting ratchet," [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s Austrian advertising-for-coverage scandal, [[Farkas2026-lr]]'s fact-checkers defending platform funding, [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]] on democratic backsliding and cross-platform disinformation prevalence, [[Schulte2026-df]]'s German-election multi-platform infrastructure problem, [[Votta2025-xz]] on ad-delivery algorithms, [[Ahuja2025-ku]] on dark-pattern autonomy violations under Article 25, [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] on deepfake fraud ecosystems, and [[Moran2025-qn]] on the collapsing trust-and-safety profession — collectively demonstrate why the access and auditing questions above are not merely methodological housekeeping but bear directly on democratic accountability. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] and [[Suk2026-ai]] mark the edges of the topic: the former shows Facebook's ad infrastructure repurposed as a survey-recruitment tool even as APIs closed, and the latter gestures toward the field's likely next object — trust in generative AI — suggesting this entire post-API, DSA-era struggle over transparency is a rehearsal for governing an even less observable technology to come.
