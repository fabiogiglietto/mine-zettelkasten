# Platform Governance & Research Data Access

## The closing of the open web and the "post-API age"

The foundation of this literature is a shared historical narrative: a decade-plus arc from relatively open platform data ecosystems to their systematic closure, and the halting emergence of regulated alternatives. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this history most comprehensively, tracing five eras from "API prehistory" through "academic cooperation" to today's fragmented regimes of laissez-faire, academic, walled-garden, and pay-to-play access. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya each eulogize the Twitter API specifically, with Murtfeldt's bibliometric analysis quantifying the "Golden Age" and its aftermath—a 13% publication decline in 2024—while [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] frames the same trajectory as a movement "from the Wild West to the Walled Garden," explicitly linking Twitter/X's fate to structurally similar closures at Meta (CrowdTangle) and Reddit. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review supplies the broadest empirical confirmation: the share of social science papers using social media data tripled over 2010–2024 before plateauing and declining, with Twitter/X and Facebook usage cratering precisely as API restrictions bit. Together these papers establish that data access is not a background methodological detail but a contested infrastructural condition that platforms can unilaterally revoke—what [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] calls the shift from platform "philanthropy" to platforms treating access as discretionary and reversible.

## Article 40 and the DSA as regulatory intervention

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act—and Article 40 in particular—emerges as the central regulatory hope and battleground. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] (companion pieces drawing on the same ERC-supported pilot and DG-CONNECT roundtable) collectively map what Article 40 changes in practice and what remains unresolved: misaligned incentives between platforms and researchers, resource asymmetries that disadvantage early-career and under-resourced scholars, and a "circular problem" whereby data access is needed to win grants but grants are needed to sustain data access. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the movement's most explicitly optimistic reading, arguing that platform resistance to Article 40 is itself evidence the regulation is working—targeting real asymmetries of informational power—and cautioning against retreat from binding frameworks. [[Lukito2026-nb]] complicates this picture empirically, cataloguing 72 access tools along "price, proficiency, or permission" axes and showing that DSA-mandated programs remain EU-restricted, beta-stage, and skewed toward production over consumption data. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes the conceptual agenda further, arguing the DSA era demands abandoning narrow causal-effects paradigms in favor of studying platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds a granular institutional lens, showing through discourse analysis of EU consultation feedback that "data quality" was largely absent from official DSA texts until academic and NGO stakeholders forced its partial inclusion—evidence that even seemingly technical regulatory categories are politically contested terrain.

## Auditing the instruments of compliance: APIs, clean rooms, and their failures

A substantial empirical cluster interrogates whether DSA-mandated access tools actually deliver on their transparency promise. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts a rigorous audit of YouTube's search API, revealing a "forgetful by design" temporal decay that undermines retrospective research central to DSA systemic-risk obligations; [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content moderation transparency during EU elections, documenting inconsistent "News Funding Notice" labeling and vague removal statements. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] examine the "clean room" paradigm itself—the Meta Content Library and its CrowdTangle predecessor—arguing these environments structurally privilege code-literate, well-resourced researchers while foreclosing qualitative, longitudinal, and cross-platform work; Cullen's "Seeing like an API" framework theorizes how technical design and governance rules jointly constrain what can even be asked. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive counterpoint, building TubeStats and TokStats as independent random-sampling infrastructure precisely because sanctioned APIs fail to provide representative data. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose a different failure mode: systematic underinvestment in human moderation for Global South languages, turning the DSA's own disclosure requirements into evidence of the inequities they were meant to illuminate. [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] similarly treat multi-platform research capacity as an infrastructure problem requiring standardization rather than one-off studies.

## Meta's data regimes as recurring case study

Meta's URL Shares Dataset and its successors function as a methodological through-line connecting data-quality critique to substantive findings about platform curation. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies a critical artifact—the 100-public-shares threshold—that produces spurious cross-country similarity in the dataset, a cautionary methodological finding directly relevant to any comparative work built on it, including [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]'s cross-national study of untrustworthy URL prevalence in Germany, France, and Italy. This lineage culminates in [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which uses the successor Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset to show Facebook actively dampens reach for partisan content and rewards journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate with governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures—directly challenging the "neutral conduit" framing platforms prefer. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] extends this evidentiary line to Meta's political content reduction policy, finding via the Meta Content Library that Italian parliamentarians' reach fell 72% under a policy that began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout—a transparency deficit uncovered only because DSA-enabled infrastructure made retrospective breakpoint detection possible. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] use adjacent Meta-provided datasets to probe exposure concentration and the meaning of user "false news" reports, the latter directly motivated by Meta's 2025 abandonment of professional fact-checking. Collectively these papers model a research program that is simultaneously reliant on and critical of platform-provided data.

## Industry capture, independence, and the politics of collaboration

A parallel strand interrogates the structural conditions under which platform-adjacent research is produced at all. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] document pervasive, largely undisclosed industry ties among authors, editors, and reviewers of high-profile social media research, arguing for "industrial saturation" analogous to tobacco- and pharma-industry science capture. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the same dynamic as a call for regulatory reform (DSA) paired with stronger research-on-research norms. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers the sharpest methodological indictment of the flagship Meta2020 partnership, arguing its findings lack both temporal and "poetic" validity and that reactive academic science structurally cannot keep pace with platforms—an argument that motivates [[Allen2025-ot]]'s endorsement of platform-independent experimental methods and [[Brady2026-ln]]'s registered-report field experiment on Bluesky as an alternative to permission-based collaboration. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines this dependency from fact-checkers' side, showing how European fact-checking organizations rhetorically defend platform funding while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the fallout of Meta's abandonment of that very model. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] globalizes the stakes, arguing that Global South democracies face compounded asymmetries—data colonialism plus weak regulatory infrastructure—making the DSA's EU-centric model an incomplete template.

## Moderation, algorithmic governance, and the limits of transparency

Finally, several papers examine what platform governance actually accomplishes once instantiated, providing substantive counterpoint to procedural critiques of access. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] traces convergent phases in platform election policy—protection, civic-integrity expansion, backlash—showing policy is reactive to US election cycles rather than principled. [[Crosset2026-mq]] theorizes DSA-style regulation as "security bureaucratisation," turning platforms into quasi-public infrastructures governed through auditing and reporting rather than direct content control. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows Meta's own definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has narrowed over time, allowing manufactured attention to persist under looser scrutiny, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] interrogate whether Community Notes—the post-fact-checking alternative—actually redresses partisan asymmetries or systematically undermoderates polarizing content. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] extends algorithmic audit into recommender internals, showing X's system inadvertently encodes political ideology. [[McNally2025-dn]] and Efstic Ventura2026-yc-adjacent studies (e.g., [[Efstratiou2025-gs]]) demonstrate that governance interventions leave detectable traces even without direct access, using longitudinal engagement patterns and algorithmic feed comparisons to infer platform behavior indirectly—arguably the methodological future this literature points toward when direct data access remains contested, incomplete, or revoked.
