# Platform Governance & Researcher Data Access

## The Regulatory Turn: DSA Article 40 as a New Settlement

The centre of gravity for this literature is the attempt, embodied in Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act, to convert researcher data access from a matter of platform discretion into a matter of legal right. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] together stage this as an open contest rather than a settled achievement: Ohme documents what Article 40 changes in day-to-day research practice, Pierri and co-authors draw on an 18-month ERC pilot with DG-CONNECT to catalogue misaligned incentives and resource asymmetries, and de Vreese and Tromble argue explicitly that the visible turbulence — platform stalling, legal smokescreens, narrow interpretations — should be read as evidence the regulation is finally biting entrenched power, not as proof it has failed. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] supplies the longue durée against which to read this moment, periodizing platform data access from "Wild West" APIs through a brief "Golden Age" to commercial paywalls, and positioning the DSA as a shift from data philanthropy to regulatory obligation — one still undermined by uneven implementation and a two-tier global system that leaves non-EU, early-career, and Global South researchers in a "data abyss." [[Lukito2026-nb]] operationalizes this asymmetry empirically, cataloguing 72 access tools along axes of price, proficiency, and permission, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent and access infrastructure overwhelmingly WEIRD-centric. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how contested even the DSA's technical vocabulary is: "data quality," largely absent from EU legislative text, was pushed into the Delegated Regulation's recitals only through sustained NGO and academic advocacy against platform resistance (with Meta notably inverting the argument to claim access would be futile anyway).

## The Post-API Age and the Infrastructure Lost

A second cluster documents, often elegiacally, what has actually been lost. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]]'s bibliometric "eulogy" quantifies a 13% decline in Twitter-based scholarship in 2024 following the API's $42,000/month repricing, while [[Yang2026-tq]] extends this diagnosis across five social-science disciplines, showing social-media-based publishing tripling and then plateauing precisely as API restrictions bit. Bastos2025-ya and [[Freelon2024-sc]] provide complementary histories — the former a retrospective on Twitter's now-vanished API suite and the "Twitter studies" subfield it enabled, the latter a periodization (prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation) culminating in a taxonomy of current "official" and "unofficial" access regimes, with the CrowdTangle-to-Meta-Content-Library transition as the paradigm case of platform-led access cycling through cosmetic replacements. [[Cullen2026-cb]]'s interviews with 20 CrowdTangle users theorize this infrastructural mediation directly, arguing that APIs are never neutral conduits but actively constitute what can be seen, asked, and concluded — a framework ("Seeing like an API") that [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends to the successor "clean room" model (Meta Content Library, ProQuest TDM Studio), showing how mandatory monthly data deletion, code-only environments, and web disconnection systematically exclude qualitative, longitudinal, and less-resourced researchers even as they nominally satisfy Article 40. [[Zheng2026-bi]], [[Rieder2025-ju]], and [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] supply granular technical audits in this vein: TubeStats/TokStats show neither YouTube nor TikTok offers genuinely representative sampling; Rieder's audit of YouTube's search API finds a severe "forgetful by design" temporal decay that undermines retrospective DSA-relevant research; and Entrena-Serrano's evaluation of TikTok's expanded Research API finds persistent, undocumented limitations despite its DSA-driven 2023 expansion. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] adds a methodological warning that even successful scraping introduces systematic, non-random distortions relative to in-situ user experience. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] use DSA transparency mandates themselves as research objects, auditing YouTube's election-related moderation and cross-lingual disparities in human moderator allocation respectively — both concluding that transparency-as-compliance falls well short of transparency-as-accountability.

## Industry Capture of the Evidence Base

A closely related strand interrogates the structural dependency this infrastructural precarity produces. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] document quantitatively and conceptually how platform influence pervades even top-tier social media science: roughly half of high-profile papers carry undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small recurring cohort of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this in terms borrowed from tobacco and pharmaceutical research, arguing that unlike those fields, social media research has no possibility of independent data generation at all. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest internal critique of the flagship attempt at platform-academic collaboration, the Meta 2020 Election Study: he argues its field experiments lack both "temporal validity" (platforms change too fast for findings to generalize) and "poetic validity" (core constructs like "echo chamber" are inconsistently operationalized), concluding that reactive social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms and that proactive regulatory mandates are the only remedy — a conclusion [[Allen2025-ot]] partially revisits by showing how "platform-independent" experimental methods (LLM-based browser reranking) are emerging precisely to route around this dependency. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] furnishes a cautionary methodological case study from within the Social Science One partnership itself: the 100-share anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset produces a spurious platform-wide artifact that can be mistaken for substantive cross-national findings, a lesson [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] build on to argue that the same CrowdTangle/URL-Shares-derived data, handled carefully with breakpoint and time-series methods, can reveal Facebook's news feed and reach-shaping interventions as detectable, non-black-box phenomena — directly usable for the kind of systemic-risk auditing Article 40 envisions.

## Meta's Political Content Retreat and the Politics of Platform Discourse

A tightly focused sub-thread examines Meta's 2021–2025 political content reduction policy and its January 2025 reversal as a paradigmatic case of opaque, high-stakes platform governance. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is the empirical anchor here: using the Meta Content Library itself, it detects via structural breakpoint analysis that the policy's effects on Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, produced a 72% reach reduction, and — after the reversal — recovered to only 65% of baseline, with extremist accounts compensating for per-post losses by dramatically increasing posting volume. The paper is notable for treating the MCL simultaneously as flawed and as genuine transparency progress, modeling the "collaborative rather than adversarial" posture that [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] and [[Pierri2025-hm]] also advocate. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examines the discursive and professional fallout of the adjacent decision to end the Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, finding via interviews with fact-checkers that Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing is empirically unfounded (removal occurs in only ~30% of cases) and that fact-checkers remain deeply skeptical of Community Notes as a substitute — skepticism echoed in [[Farkas2026-lr]]'s account of how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform funding via "differentiation" and "transcendence" strategies amid accusations of collusion, and empirically vindicated by [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s finding that Community Notes' bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing electoral content by design. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] supply the discursive-analytic complement, showing how Meta's Newsroom communications (and platform communications more broadly, via the concept of "changecraft") systematically externalize blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors while narrating policy retrenchment as continuity rather than rupture — a framing [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows has material teeth, since Meta's operational definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has itself narrowed over time from broad "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors," letting much manufactured attention persist on-platform. [[Holt2026-zq]] closes the loop by showing what happens once professional fact-checking recedes and user flagging takes its place: reported "False News" links skew toward polarized political topics that users disagree with rather than content that is actually false.

## Platform Power, Governance Theory, and the Wider Ecosystem

A final set of papers situates this empirical and regulatory work within broader theories of platform power and its historical arc. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] remains the conceptual touchstone, showing how the very term "platform" performs discursive work that forecloses accountability — a thread [[Helmond2026-ll]] revisits a decade on, arguing platformization has entered an AI-centred phase in which behavioral-data extraction from the open web (symbolized by the Like button's retirement) gives way to inference and space-based AI infrastructure. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] and [[Crosset2026-mq]] provide comparative-institutional perspectives: the former tracing convergent phases of platform election policy (protection, civic-integrity expansion, backlash) driven largely by US election cycles, the latter analyzing EU and US content-moderation legislation as competing "circulation regimes" united by a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation." [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter/X's decline as public-debate infrastructure, while [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Marwick2026-ss]] — companion anniversary essays — argue social media has become "parasocial" and alienating relative to an earlier, more reciprocal internet, framing the current governance crisis as continuous with a longer arc of platform financialization. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], [[Bouchaud2026-lr]], and [[Rieder2026-pp]] ground these debates in concrete algorithmic audits (X's post-Musk recommendation shifts, X's inadvertent ideological profiling via embeddings, and YouTube's inability to contain a deplatformed extremist's diffuse "ambient" presence), demonstrating the continued necessity of the very researcher access this whole literature is fighting to secure. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] offers the field's most explicit theoretical reframing, urging political communication to move from individual causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure — a call that [[Schulte2026-df]], with its multi-platform German election case study, and [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Vincent_undated-re]], with their measurement-driven accounts of extremist and disinformation exploitation of algorithmic recommendation, take up as concrete empirical programs. Finally, [[Votta2025-xz]], [[Ahuja2025-ku]], [[Moran2025-qn]], [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Weinbrand2026-sf]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Balluff2026-bv]], [[Swartz2026-zb]], [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]], and [[Unknown2025-qj]] extend this governance apparatus into adjacent frontiers — ad-delivery algorithms, dark-pattern regulation under Article 25, the collapsing trust-and-safety profession, AI Overviews' redefinition of search authority, deepfake-enabled scam ecosystems, and AI red-teaming — collectively signalling that the data-access and accountability struggles chronicled here are propagating, largely unresolved, into the generative-AI era that increasingly subsumes the platforms this literature was built to study.
