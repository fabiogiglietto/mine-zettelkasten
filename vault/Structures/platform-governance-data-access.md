# Platform Governance and Research Data Access

## The Shape of a Crisis: Platform Governance and the Fight for Data

The papers gathered here trace a single, compounding story: the slow closing of the aperture through which independent researchers could observe social media platforms, and the uneven, contested regulatory effort—most concretely the EU's Digital Services Act—to force it back open. Read together, they move from historical diagnosis, to empirical documentation of harm, to methodological innovation under constraint, to an emerging (and fragile) legal settlement whose implementation is still being fought over in real time.

### From API Prehistory to the "Post-API Age"

A cluster of papers establishes the historical arc. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] shows how the very term "platform" was constructed to claim neutrality while enabling substantive editorial control—an early rhetorical move whose consequences the rest of this literature inherits. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] extends this into a full theory of the "platform society," in which private infrastructures absorb public functions and public-value contestation becomes unavoidable. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the rupture precisely: Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure inaugurated a "post-API age" in which TOS-compliant data collection became structurally unreliable. [[Bruns2019-nr]] reframes this as an "APIcalypse," arguing that platforms instrumentalized the Cambridge Analytica scandal to suppress *critical* scholarship specifically, not merely to protect privacy—a thesis echoed and periodized in [[Freelon2024-sc]], which maps five distinct eras of access (prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation) and four current official regimes. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] narrates the Twitter/X-specific version of this same trajectory—Wild West, Golden Age, walled garden—while Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] eulogize the scholarly ecosystem (Twitter studies) that this infrastructure enabled and then stranded; Murtfeldt's bibliometrics make the cost legible: a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 alone. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes the finding across disciplines, showing that empirical social-media research plateaued and declined exactly when API access contracted, with researchers now scrambling toward TikTok, YouTube, and donation-based methods. [[Bruns2026-yv]] closes this historical arc with an obituary for Twitter itself as a space for public debate, arguing that successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) replicate its form without solving its governance failures.

### Industry Capture of the Evidence Base

A second thread interrogates not just *access* but *epistemic dependency*. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] collectively argue that platform research now resembles tobacco or pharmaceutical science: data monopoly creates structural opportunities for undisclosed industry influence, and empirical audits confirm this—roughly half of high-profile social-media papers have disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small cohort of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a methodological verdict on the flagship case of such entanglement, the Meta2020 election partnership, arguing that even its most rigorous studies suffer from low "temporal" and "poetic" validity and that platforms change too fast for reactive social science to keep pace—an argument for *proactive* regulatory disclosure obligations rather than one-off collaborations. [[Cullen2026-cb]] supplies an ethnographic complement, showing via interviews with CrowdTangle users how the API's design and governance rules constrained not just data but the very questions researchers could ask ("Seeing like an API"). [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents the downstream reputational cost of this dependency for fact-checkers, who must rhetorically defend platform funding against charges of co-optation.

### Measuring What Platforms Hide: The URL Shares/Content Library Lineage

A distinct methodological lineage uses Meta-provided datasets to study platform behavior empirically, while simultaneously exposing their limitations. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] both show that the Social Science One URL Shares Dataset's 100-share anonymization threshold introduces severe, non-random bias—inflating apparent fake-news prevalence and masking a platform-wide algorithmic "force" (likely Meaningful Social Interactions tuning) that researchers mistook for organic signal. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends this dataset cross-nationally to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares concentrated among older users. The newer Meta Content Library enables more ambitious work: [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] shows Facebook actively, variably penalizing partisan content and rewarding journalistic quality in ways that track known "break the glass" interventions, directly contesting the idea that platforms are neutral conduits; [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the same infrastructure to detect that Meta's political-content-reduction policy began suppressing Italian MPs' reach ten months before its announced global rollout and disproportionately *failed* to constrain extremist accounts—a transparency failure documented using the very tool the DSA mandated. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] round out this cluster, respectively finding concentrated-but-limited exposure to untrustworthy sources and showing that "False News" user-flagging functions more as political disagreement than falsity-detection—directly relevant as Meta dismantles professional fact-checking ([[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]).

### Auditing the Black Boxes: APIs, Moderation, and Coordination

Several papers turn critical audit techniques onto the very tools regulators now rely upon. [[Rieder2025-ju]] demonstrates that YouTube's Data API search endpoint is "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay undermining DSA-relevant research; [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's DSA-mandated Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose stark linguistic inequities in human moderation staffing, with Global South languages chronically under-resourced relative to English. Jurg2025-ju/[[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] extend algorithmic auditing to YouTube's election-period source prioritization and its inability to contain deplatformed figures like Andrew Tate, respectively, while [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist exploitation of TikTok's moderation gaps. On coordination detection, [[Mannocci2026-kc]] offers a unifying survey and [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] a generalized cross-platform tool (CooRTweet), while [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and [[Thiele2025-ol]] show that Meta's definition of "coordinated inauthentic behavior" has narrowed strategically over time, letting much manufactured attention remain on-platform—a discursive sleight-of-hand analyzed more broadly in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]], which trace how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X linguistically manage and legitimate policy change ("changecraft") to externalize blame. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] theorizes the underlying governance shift from removal to algorithmic *reduction*, arguing this near-invisible mechanism now does most of the moderation work while evading transparency obligations entirely, and [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces the same terminological evolution (fake news → false news → misinformation → borderline) across three US election cycles, alongside the parallel tightening of data access from API to CrowdTangle to Content Library.

### The DSA as Contested Settlement

The regulatory centerpiece of this literature is Article 40 of the DSA, treated across several papers as both breakthrough and unfinished business. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] collectively frame Article 40 as a historic shift from "independence by permission" toward a binding legal right to data—while documenting opaque procedures, inconsistent timelines, and strategic platform resistance that de Vreese and Tromble insist should be read as evidence the regulation is *working*, not failing. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how "data quality" itself became a politically contested category during DSA rulemaking, with academic and NGO stakeholders fighting (with partial success) to get it written into the Delegated Regulation against platform resistance. [[Bruns2026-pn]] details the practical costs of the "clean room" compliance model (the Meta Content Library, ProQuest TDM Studio), which satisfies the letter of access while excluding non-programmers, qualitative researchers, and under-resourced institutions. [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] propose infrastructural correctives—cataloguing 72 access tools and building independent random-sampling dashboards, respectively—as hedges against the DSA's uneven, EU-centric reach. [[Schulte2026-df]] demonstrates the practical costs of fragmentation directly, documenting the infrastructural bottlenecks encountered studying the 2025 German election across X and TikTok simultaneously. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA era demands a conceptual pivot from individual causal-effects research toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure. Broader regulatory comparison comes from [[Crosset2026-mq]], which situates the DSA alongside the Online Safety Act and US state laws as one of three distinct "circulation regimes," and from [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]], which tracks election-policy convergence and recent backlash across five platforms. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] and [[Benkler2018-lw]] widen the lens geographically and politically, respectively showing how platform self-regulation failures and asymmetric media radicalization play out beyond—and before—the DSA's jurisdiction.

### Consequences at the Edges

A final set of papers gestures toward what is lost or newly at risk when this infrastructure fails. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows Community Notes structurally undermoderates polarizing electoral content by design; [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Balluff2026-bv]] examine covert money flows (ad delivery algorithms, government advertising) that regulation has yet to meaningfully touch; [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] show researchers improvising around closed APIs via crowdsourced auditing and ad-based recruitment. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] extend the governance question to AI-driven scams and the normalization of fraud as platform logic, while [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the institutional collapse of trust-and-safety work itself. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Mahl2026-hc]], [[Allen2025-ot]], [[Weinbrand2026-sf]], and [[Boyd2026-op]] close the loop by asking what all this means normatively—for democratic backsliding, for the viability of behavioral (rather than truth-based) moderation, for expert consensus on what interventions matter, for platform-independent experimental alternatives, for the discursive construction of AI search, and for whether "social media" remains a coherent object of study at all. Together, this corpus documents not a single crisis but a recursive one: every fix to platform opacity produces a new site of contestation, and the DSA—however consequential—is best understood not as an endpoint but as the current, provisional terrain of that struggle.
