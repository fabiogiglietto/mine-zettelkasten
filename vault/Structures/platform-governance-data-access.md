# Platform Governance and Researcher Data Access

# Platform Governance and Researcher Data Access

## The arc: from open APIs to the "post-API age"

The baseline historical narrative running through this literature is a story of enclosure. Bruns2025-ya's retrospective on Twitter's REST, Search, Streaming, Academic, and Compliance APIs — cited here as Bastos2025-ya — treats the platform as having briefly functioned as open research infrastructure, enabling a distinct subfield of "Twitter studies." [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the rupture that ends this era: Facebook's 2018 closure of the Pages API inaugurates a "post-API age" in which TOS-compliant extraction becomes impossible overnight, and methodological training built around any single platform's API is structurally fragile. [[Bruns2019-nr]] extends this into the "APIcalypse," arguing that the Cambridge Analytica scandal was less a genuine privacy reckoning than a pretext platforms used to defund and delegitimize critical, independent scholarship, with alternatives like Social Science One functioning as "independence by permission" — access granted at the pleasure of gatekeepers with an undisclosed veto. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this entire trajectory in granular detail (prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation), arguing data access has become permanently contingent, reshaped by scandal, leadership change, and commercial interest rather than settling into any stable regime. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] retells the same arc specifically for Twitter/X — Wild West, Golden Age, commercial paywall — and explicitly generalizes the pattern across Meta, Reddit, and TikTok. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] supplies the bibliometric body-count: a 25%-a-year growth curve in Twitter-based scholarship that stagnates in 2023 and falls 13% in 2024 once Enterprise API pricing hits $42,000/month, converting a qualitative narrative of closure into measurable scientific loss.

## CrowdTangle and the Meta data ecosystem

Much of the empirical weight of this literature concentrates on Meta's shifting data infrastructure. [[Cullen2026-cb]] develops "seeing like an API" to show how CrowdTangle's technical design and governance rules did not merely constrain but actively shaped what could be observed and concluded, well before its 2024 shutdown. [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] both treat that shutdown as a case study in the instability of platform-led access: Meta's cycling through Social Science One, CrowdTangle, FORT, and the Meta Content Library (MCL) illustrates "independence by permission" in its clearest institutional form, while [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s audit of the MCL's clean-room architecture finds that mandatory data deletion, code-only interfaces, and lack of cross-platform integration systematically exclude qualitative researchers, smaller institutions, and the Majority World — privacy and copyright justifications notwithstanding. Methodological artifacts compound these access problems: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows that Meta's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset produces spurious cross-country convergence driven by Feed-ranking changes rather than social reality, a caution echoed by [[Holt2026-zq]]'s finding that user "False News" reports track political disagreement rather than falsity, complicating Meta's post-fact-checking reliance on crowd signals. Against this backdrop of friction, [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] use the newer MCL-based data to demonstrate that Meta is an active curator, not a neutral conduit — the latter documenting that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72% roughly ten months before its announced global rollout, with extremist accounts compensating through sheer posting volume, and with the policy itself absent from Meta's own DSA transparency filings. [[McNally2025-dn]] independently corroborates detectable, lagged algorithmic interventions using CrowdTangle-sourced Guardian engagement data, arguing the "black box" metaphor obscures a tractable, auditable sociotechnical system. Taken together these papers model a shift in tone: from documenting closure ([[Bruns2019-nr]], [[Freelon2018-ao]]) to exploiting newly mandated but imperfect openings ([[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], [[McNally2025-dn]]) as a template for "collaborative" platform-governance research.

## The Digital Services Act as regulatory intervention

A dense cluster treats Article 40 of the EU DSA as the central attempt to reverse the post-API trajectory by converting data access from platform philanthropy into legal obligation. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame this as a genuine, if turbulent, redistribution of informational power — the latter explicitly arguing that platform foot-dragging and legal ambiguity should be read as evidence the regulation is working, not failing, and warning against retreat from binding frameworks. [[Pierri2026-ib]], drawing on direct involvement in EU pilot programs, catalogs persistent legal, technical, and organizational asymmetries (financial, informational, legal, reputational) that favor platforms over academic researchers even post-Delegated Act, and flags LLMs as an emerging regulatory blind spot. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] assess the practical payoff for election research specifically, finding uneven implementation across platforms and continued gaps in campaign- and advertising-related data. Several papers interrogate the DSA's conceptual infrastructure rather than just its procedures: [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows "data" itself is an undefined "floating signifier" in Article 40 and its Delegated Act, which platforms exploit to narrow data to aggregated, quantified, pre-existing datasets — marginalizing qualitative and humanities conceptions of evidence — while [[Peters2026-mo]] performs a parallel discourse analysis showing "data quality" was largely absent from official EU text until academic and NGO stakeholders fought it into the Delegated Act's recitals, over platform resistance (Meta notably inverting the argument to claim its own data was too poor to bother sharing). Tool-specific audits sharpen the picture of compliance theater: [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's Research API still inconsistent and limited despite its 2023 EU expansion; [[Rieder2025-ju]] demonstrates YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within 20–60 days post-publication in ways that directly undermine DSA systemic-risk research; [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content moderation transparency around the 2024 EU elections, proposing "observability" as a richer standard than mere data release; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to expose moderator-allocation disparities disadvantaging Global South languages. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys this entire toolscape. Zooming out, [[Ahuja2025-ku]] shows DSA categories (Article 25's autonomy violations) can also be operationalized for HCI-style design auditing beyond data access narrowly construed, while [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA's "flow optimisation" regime comparatively against US "free circulation" and German "patrolling" models, arguing all converge on a shared logic of security bureaucratization — transparency and auditing as modes of governing platforms as quasi-public infrastructure. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA data regime should prompt a conceptual pivot in political communication away from individual causal effects toward platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure.

## Industry influence, epistemic dependence, and the Meta2020 reckoning

A second major thread interrogates not just access but the epistemic compromise that follows from platforms controlling research conditions. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical study [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] make the strongest claims: drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and pharma science, they document that roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, that these ties are concentrated among a small recurring set of scientists who also populate editorial and review roles (yielding ~80% "industrial saturation" of the field), and that industry-tied work skews toward user-blaming topics like misinformation sharing while underrepresenting platform-level dynamics. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a structural asymmetry unique to platform studies compared to food, pharma, or tobacco research, since platforms are the sole source of the data needed to study them, and calls for both DSA-style regulatory reform and a "research on research" reflexivity within the field. The Meta2020 Election Study anchors much of this discussion as the paradigmatic case: [[Munger2025-cz]] offers an extended meta-scientific critique arguing the collaboration, despite methodological ambition, produced numerical estimates of uncertain external and "poetic" validity, hampered by inconsistent operationalization of concepts like "echo chamber" and a design biased toward null findings — concluding that proactive regulation is needed because reactive social science cannot keep pace with platform change. [[Allen2025-ot]] revisits this debate via Piccardi et al.'s platform-independent, LLM-based reranking experiment, positioning such techniques as a necessary methodological workaround given closed APIs and sporadic collaborations, and reconciling that study's results with the Meta2020 FIES's largely null findings through differences in intervention level and platform moderation regime. [[Pierri2026-ib]] adds a documented case of algorithmic changes being undisclosed to Meta2020 researchers during the study period itself — direct evidence of the asymmetry [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] theorizes.

## Discourse, framing, and the politics of platform self-presentation

Running alongside the empirical-access literature is a strand analyzing how platforms discursively manage their own accountability. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] is the foundational text here, showing how the very term "platform" does ideological work — invoking neutrality and openness to simultaneously court users, advertisers, and regulators while obscuring editorial intervention; [[van-Dijck2018-up]] scales this into a full account of the "platform society," where private infrastructures embed US-centric values in contest with public goods across sectors. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] apply this discourse-analytic lens to contemporary Meta communications specifically: the former identifies "inauthenticity," "political advertising," "technological solutions," and "enforcement" as recurring frames that externalize blame while casting Meta as capable problem-solver; the latter coins "changecraft" to describe how platforms (Meta, TikTok, YouTube, X) narrate policy and feature changes as either visible technical progress or seamless ideological continuity, masking power consolidation. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] identifies "reduction" — algorithmic demotion short of removal — as the undertheorized core of this discursive sleight of hand, arguing it concentrates curatorial power precisely because it evades transparency and accountability frameworks built around removal. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces the terminological drift this enables across Meta's history ("fake news" → "false news" → "misinformation" → "borderline"), showing successive redefinitions shrink and depoliticize what counts as a problem even as borderline content that does perform well remains overwhelmingly right-wing — directly linking data-access restriction (API v1/v2, Graph Search, CrowdTangle) to this depoliticizing project. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] complements this by showing Meta's definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has itself narrowed from broad "false amplifiers" to only "adversarial threat actors," letting much manufactured attention remain on-platform under relabeled categories. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] extend the discourse-and-legitimacy frame to the fact-checking ecosystem Meta has now partly abandoned: the former empirically debunks Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing of fact-checkers (showing removal occurs in only ~30% of cases) while documenting fact-checker skepticism toward Community Notes as a replacement; the latter shows European fact-checkers deploy rhetorical "apologia" to defend their platform funding dependencies, revealing boundary-work tensions structurally similar to those [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] describe for academic researchers.

## Downstream consequences: governance gaps as substantive harms

A final set of papers uses the access and governance machinery discussed above to study concrete harms that under-resourced moderation and data regimes fail to catch. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] both exploit data-donation and large-scale corpus methods to show X's infrastructure carries hidden political risk — inadvertent ideological profiling in recommender embeddings, and systematic undermoderation of polarizing content by Community Notes' bridging design — with direct implications for electoral integrity across jurisdictions. [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] document infrastructural evasion and persistence of extremist and ambient-ideological content (jihadist propaganda on TikTok, Andrew Tate's "ambient" afterlife on YouTube) that outpaces platform moderation regardless of stated policy, reinforcing [[Gillespie2022-jx]]'s point that governance-by-reduction cannot keep pace with recommendation-driven circulation. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] push the governance lens outward to adjacent and Global South contexts — covert media capture via government advertising in Austria, and platform-driven undermining of Brazil's "Fake News Bill" — underscoring that the asymmetries of power documented throughout this literature (platforms versus researchers, platforms versus regulators) recur at the level of platforms versus national publics. [[Moran2025-qn]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] close the loop at the level of institutional capacity, documenting the dismantling of trust-and-safety teams and arguing, in the starkest terms, that democratic backsliding is now inseparable from platform accountability failures — while [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] shows this is a general cross-platform pattern of policy expansion followed by rollback, not an idiosyncrasy of any single company. [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Swartz2026-zb]], and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend the argument to misinformation-at-scale and AI-driven scam ecosystems as structural, revenue-aligned features of platform business models rather than aberrations, suggesting that the data-access and governance struggles chronicled elsewhere in this corpus are symptoms of a deeper incentive misalignment between platform profitability and public accountability — one that tools like [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s random-sampling dashboards for YouTube and TikTok attempt to counteract by rebuilding, outside platform permission, the baseline empirical infrastructure the "post-API age" otherwise denies researchers.
