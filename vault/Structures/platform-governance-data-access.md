# Platform Governance & Research Data Access

## The arc of this topic

This cluster traces a single historical arc: the rise and fall of open platform data, the political struggle to re-open it through regulation, and a growing body of work that interrogates — often using exactly that regulated access — whether platforms are honest about what they do with it. Read together, the papers move from infrastructural history, to regulatory analysis, to methodological audit, to substantive empirical accountability research, closing with theoretical attempts to name what has changed.

### From the Wild West to the walled garden: a history of access

A foundational strand periodizes platform data access as a series of openings and closures. [[Freelon2018-ao]] coined the "post-API age" at the moment Facebook's Pages API closed in 2018; [[Bruns2019-nr]] extended this into the "APIcalypse," arguing that post-Cambridge-Analytica restrictions were less about privacy than about frustrating critical scrutiny. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes nearly two decades of access regimes across six platforms, and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] narrates Twitter/X's trajectory from "Wild West" to "Golden Age" academic API to Musk-era paywall, explicitly tying this history to the DSA's attempt to convert access from corporate philanthropy into legal right. Bastos2025-ya and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantify what was lost when Twitter's APIs closed, the latter showing a measurable decline in published scholarship once Enterprise pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Lukito2026-nb]] surveys 72 current access tools for election research and names the underlying structural problem — "price, proficiency, or permission" — that determines who can still do this work, echoed in [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s earlier framing of platforms as a contested "platform society" rather than neutral infrastructure, and in [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s foundational account of how the rhetoric of "platform" itself was built to evade regulatory capture from the outset.

### The DSA and Article 40: regulation as corrective, friction as evidence

A second strand assesses whether the EU's Digital Services Act, especially Article 40, actually reverses this closure. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offer complementary stock-takes: the former cataloguing what has practically changed, the latter arguing that platform resistance and procedural friction are not signs of regulatory failure but evidence that binding law is finally constraining entrenched power — a "moving targets, moving politics" framing that counsels patience over abandonment. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] dig into implementation mechanics: the former cataloguing legal, technical, and organizational barriers (and flagging LLMs as an emerging regulatory blind spot), the latter showing through discourse analysis of 242 consultation submissions that "data quality" was politically contested terrain, pushed by academics and NGOs against platform resistance. [[Annabell2026-ev]] extends this by showing that even "data" itself functions as a floating signifier in the regulation, narrowed by platforms (notably Meta, Amazon, Booking.com) toward aggregated, quantified datasets that exclude qualitative and humanities understandings. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] apply this regulatory lens specifically to election research, the latter arguing that platform observability itself should be treated as reusable research infrastructure rather than rebuilt ad hoc for every election. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting API/tool landscape directly.

### Auditing the auditors: what the new tools actually deliver

A closely related empirical strand tests whether DSA-mandated or DSA-adjacent tools deliver what they promise. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API persistently inconsistent; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within 20–60 days of publication — a direct threat to systemic-risk research under the DSA; [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's election-period moderation and publisher-labeling practices and finds inconsistent application across languages and broadcasters; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data itself to expose severe cross-lingual disparities in human moderator allocation, with Global South languages receiving a fraction of English's coverage. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds methodologically, building TubeStats and TokStats as independent random-sampling infrastructure precisely because neither platform offers adequate representative access. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] turn the same critical eye on the "clean room" model exemplified by the Meta Content Library and the discontinued CrowdTangle, arguing that technical design — not just legal access — structurally excludes qualitative, non-programming, and non-Western researchers, with Cullen's "Seeing like an API" framework naming how infrastructural mediation shapes what can be known at all.

### The URL Shares Dataset and the artifacts of algorithmic curation

A tight empirical cluster interrogates Meta's own flagship transparency product. [[Allen2021-ai]] shows the dataset's 100-public-share censoring threshold — not differential-privacy noise — badly overstates fake-news prevalence relative to a representative panel. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] finds that near-identical monthly trends across 46 countries reveal a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely Feed/MSI changes) that swamps genuine local signal, warning against naive cross-country comparison. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] uses the successor Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset to show Facebook actively dampens reach for partisan content and rewards journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures — evidence against treating the platform as a neutral conduit. [[McNally2025-dn]] complements this with a longitudinal CrowdTangle-based analysis showing detectable, lagged effects of specific News Feed algorithm changes on hard-news engagement, arguing the "black box" metaphor itself obscures a tractable, auditable sociotechnical system — a claim that dovetails with [[Rieder2020-sl35]]'s earlier theoretical case for replacing "transparency" with "observability" as the organizing regulatory concept.

### Accountability case studies: policy reversals under the microscope

The topic's empirical core is a set of studies holding specific Meta policies accountable, several conducted under the author's own DSA/ECAT vantage point. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses the Meta Content Library to show Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by up to 72%, beginning roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout and undisclosed in its DSA compliance reporting — while extremist accounts compensated through posting volume, a finding in direct tension with Meta's own framing. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces the discursive shrinking of Meta's problematic-content vocabulary across three US elections ("fake news" to "borderline"), showing that even as mainstream content outperforms borderline content by 2024, what remains is overwhelmingly right-wing — undercutting the company's "content-agnostic" self-description. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] interrogate the end of Meta's third-party fact-checking program from different angles: the former showing content removal was always rare (~30%) and fact-checkers never held the censorship power Zuckerberg attributed to them, the latter showing user "False News" reports mostly reflect political disagreement rather than detection of falsehood — both complicating the justification for replacing professional verification with Community Notes. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Miller2026-cp]] extend this scrutiny to X's Community Notes itself: the former showing its bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing election content by design, the latter being the platform's own team's account of scaling the system with AI writers — a useful juxtaposition of independent audit against industry self-report. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] further complicates Meta's moderation categories by showing how "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has been narrowed from broad "false amplifiers" to "adversarial actors" over time, letting much manufactured attention remain on-platform. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] supply the discursive throughline connecting these cases: Meta's Newsroom systematically externalizes blame for problematic content, and platforms generally practice "changecraft" — narrating disruptive policy change as continuity, benevolence, or democratization.

### Industry capture of the research enterprise itself

A reflexive strand asks whether the research literature evaluating these platforms is itself compromised. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this directly: roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals has disclosable industry ties, mostly undisclosed, concentrated among a small recurring set of scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] generalizes the argument across tech more broadly, drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and fossil-fuel agnotology. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the structural asymmetry as unique relative to pharma or food science, since platforms uniquely control the data needed to study them, and [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers the sharpest internal critique of the celebrated Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing its null findings reflect structurally underpowered, temporally fragile science that conveniently serves Meta's interests — a conclusion [[Allen2025-ot]] revisits by situating newer "platform-independent" experimental methods (e.g., browser-extension LLM reranking) as a necessary response to the closing data environment. [[Park2026-tr]] documents the chilling, sometimes severe personal and legal costs borne by researchers "sticking their heads above the parapet," reframing data-access precarity as also a problem of legal risk under laws like the CFAA.

### Theorizing the moderated, governed, parasocial platform

Framing essays anchor the topic's conceptual stakes. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] names "reduction" — algorithmic demotion short of removal — as an underexamined but now-dominant form of moderation, directly relevant to the visibility-suppression findings above; [[Petre2019-zh]] shows how platforms moralize the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate visibility-seeking as "platform paternalism"; and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supply the deeper historical-theoretical grounding for treating platforms as value-laden infrastructures rather than neutral conduits. Anniversary essays by [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] reassess "platformization" and "social media" themselves as eroding or obsolete categories in an AI- and parasociality-saturated environment, while [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend platform accountability debates into adjacent terrain — scam ecosystems and deepfake fraud — where platforms again appear as under-regulated gatekeepers. [[Crosset2026-mq]] and [[Ahuja2025-ku]] connect this to comparative legal analysis, showing how EU/US content legislation encodes distinct "security bureaucratisation" logics and how dark-pattern law (DSA Article 25) can be operationalized through HCI design taxonomies. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Moran2025-qn]], [[Donovan2025-ws]], and [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] round out the picture with longitudinal and sectoral accounts of platforms' election, trust-and-safety, misinformation, and climate-moderation policies retreating under political pressure — the broader democratic backdrop against which the DSA's Article 40 experiment, and this entire body of access-and-accountability research, must be judged. Finally, empirical audits of specific harms — jihadist evasion tactics on TikTok ([[Karo2026-dn]]), Andrew Tate's persistence on YouTube after deplatforming ([[Rieder2026-pp]]), deplatforming's ripple effects on the Arizona Election Review community ([[Simeone2025-vo]]), algorithmic political profiling on X ([[Bouchaud2026-lr]], [[Efstratiou2025-gs]]), media capture via government advertising ([[Balluff2026-bv]]), ad-delivery effects on political campaigns ([[Votta2025-xz]]), cross-platform misinformation monitoring ([[Vincent_undated-re]], [[Allcott2019-gn]]), fact-checker–platform dependency ([[Farkas2026-lr]]), and Twitter/X's broader decline as a public-debate space ([[Bruns2026-yv]]) — together demonstrate why sustained, legally guaranteed, methodologically scrutinized data access is not a researcher convenience but a precondition for democratic oversight of platform power, the thread that ties every paper in this collection back together.
