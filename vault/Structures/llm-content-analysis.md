# LLMs for Computational Content Analysis

## The validation problem as organizing concern

The papers filed here circle, again and again, around a single methodological knot: once an LLM is inserted into a content-analysis pipeline, what counts as validating its output, and against what? [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] makes this the explicit subject of its essay, describing a three-stage pipeline (classification, embedding-based clustering, cluster labeling) built around Italian election Facebook data and arguing that each LLM application needs its own task-specific validation protocol rather than a single blanket evaluation — crowdsourced raters, they note, are often simply worse than the models they are meant to check, pushing the field toward expert human coders instead. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies the companion empirical piece, comparing OpenAI embeddings against an Italian BERT model for clustering political news and showing, via a Grimmer-and-King-style coherence metric scored by a fine-tuned GPT-4o-mini, that proprietary LLM embeddings outperform language-specific transformers — while flagging that algorithm choice (K-means vs HDBSCAN) matters as much as embedding choice, and that high cluster-quality scores can mask large amounts of discarded "noise." [[Farjam2026-zg]] and [[Carammia2024-sl90]] extend this validation logic to fine-tuned, locally-hosted open models (Llama variants), each showing that smaller, properly tuned models can match proprietary ones on reliability (Krippendorff's alpha) while solving cost, privacy, and reproducibility problems that recur as a worry throughout the topic. [[Ober2026-vd]] pushes the same human-in-the-loop logic into qualitative interview analysis, using topic modeling as a transparent scaffold before LLMs are allowed to propose cluster labels, explicitly to preserve interpretive depth. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] reframes the entire enterprise: rather than treating the LLM as a classifier to be validated against ground truth, it argues the model should serve as an "analytic collaborator" whose justifications expose ambiguities in the codebook itself — disagreement with the LLM becomes an opportunity for theoretical refinement rather than an error to be minimized. [[Brown2025-jk]] complicates all of this from a different angle, showing across four contentious annotation tasks that LLM–human disagreement is driven overwhelmingly by item difficulty (label entropy) rather than by annotator demographics, suggesting that fairness audits of LLM coding need to control for task ambiguity before diagnosing bias.

## Classification and fine-tuning in applied pipelines

Several papers move from validation theory to concrete pipelines for specific political-science classification problems. [[Meher2025-qb]] (ConflLlama) fine-tunes Llama 3.1 via QLoRA for multi-label terrorism-event classification from the Global Terrorism Database, demonstrating large gains for rare categories and framing efficient fine-tuning as a successor to both rule-based coding and BERT-era models like ConfliBERT. [[Larsson2026-ro]] offers a substantive case of the hybrid approach advocated by Marino and Farjam: GPT-4 zero-shot sentiment classification validated by humans, applied longitudinally to a decade of Norwegian Progress Party Facebook posts, linking negativity to engagement while also serving as a methodological demonstration of LLM-assisted coding in a non-English language. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] extends classification into the visual domain, showing GPT-4o substantially outperforms specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, FaceNet512) for face recognition and person-counting in German campaign Instagram content — again pairing a validation benchmark against human-coded ground truth with a substantive application to visibility strategies. [[Arminio2025-tw]] generalizes this logic further, arguing that vision-LLMs should replace CNN pipelines not because they denote images better but because they capture *connotative* meaning (e.g., clustering wind turbines with solar panels as "renewable energy"), with interpretability demonstrated through TF-IDF summaries rather than denotative accuracy alone.

## Measuring latent constructs: ideology, position, and narrative

A second cluster treats LLMs not as classifiers of discrete categories but as instruments for scaling latent variables. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place a text on an ideological dimension and averaging many such judgments, validating the approach against established benchmarks across languages and text types. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] adapts this logic to survey methodology, pairing LLMs with paired-comparison techniques to scale open-ended responses into continuous latent traits, addressing the perennial trade-off between rich but costly open coding and cheap but shallow closed items. [[Lai2024-to]] extends ideology estimation to a new unit of analysis — individual YouTube videos — inferring position from Reddit sharing patterns rather than from the LLM directly, a useful contrast showing that not all "AI-era" measurement innovations require language models themselves. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates a more unsettling version of the same capacity: LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, which the authors frame as both a methodological opportunity (studying the politicization of culture) and a privacy risk. Narrative-level measurement is its own sub-thread: [[Waight2025-al]] formalizes "narrative similarity" as distinct from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, building an LLM-based claim-and-subject distillation pipeline validated against text-reuse, topic-modeling, and Relatio baselines, and applying it to track Russian "biolab" narratives across outlets. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas' actantial model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" in YouTube/Reddit discourse on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, finding that surface narrative convergence in comments can mask deeper, LLM-detected differences in how actors are characterized. [[Arora2025-tx]] rounds out this group by extending framing analysis beyond text-only, fixed-frame approaches into multimodal and cross-modal frame extraction for gun-violence news coverage.

## Fact-checking, misinformation, and persuasion

A cluster of papers examines LLMs as tools for combating — or at least diagnosing — political misinformation. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] is a sobering validation study: fifteen LLMs perform poorly at fact-checking PolitiFact claims from internal knowledge or ordinary web search, improving dramatically only with curated retrieval-augmented context, and exhibiting a left-leaning skew in which sources they cite — a direct methodological warning for any pipeline that assumes LLM fact-checking is "solved." [[Costello2024-bg]] takes the complementary persuasion angle, showing that personalized, evidence-based LLM dialogues durably reduce conspiracy beliefs, challenging motivational accounts of belief resistance and reframing the debunking problem as one of depth and tailoring rather than inherent irrationality. [[Szabo2026-rd]] builds a related conversational intervention ("Conversational Inoculation") and finds that chatbot-mediated inoculation outperforms static reading/writing exercises once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled. [[Miller2026-cp]] describes a platform-governance application of these themes — an open API allowing AI systems to draft X's Community Notes while preserving human rating control — illustrating how annotation-pipeline questions scale into live content-moderation infrastructure.

## Limits, critiques, and the epistemics of LLM-as-coder

Running against the generally constructive tone of the pipeline papers is a strand of work insisting on the limits of what LLMs can be trusted to do with political and social meaning. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows that even strong models fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political speech, with chain-of-thought prompting helping only modestly — a direct caution against assuming LLMs grasp pragmatic, manipulative subtext the way human coders do. [[Ptz2024-sl20]], drawing on Luhmann and conversation analysis, argues more radically that chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding, a theoretical counterweight to papers that treat LLM "judgment" as interchangeable with human coder judgment. [[Balluff2026-if]] synthesizes these worries into an explicit methodological critique of political communication's "unreflective" LLM adoption, cataloguing reproducibility failures from opaque corporate updates, demographic and linguistic bias in synthetic data, and environmental costs, and calling for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models — a useful counterpoint to the fine-tuned-open-model optimism of [[Carammia2024-sl90]] and [[Farjam2026-zg]]. [[Nguyen2026-vm]], though focused on news framing of AI rather than on LLM methodology per se, offers a reflexive bookend: much of how publics (and perhaps researchers) imagine what LLMs can do is itself shaped by media narratives of agentic, humanlike AI that this literature should be wary of reproducing uncritically.

## Adjacent infrastructure and emerging frontiers

A final set of papers extends the topic's boundaries. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a quieter but consequential infrastructural problem underlying any LLM-embedding pipeline (including those in [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]): pretrained embeddings encode confounds like source and language that bias downstream clustering and similarity measures, and linear concept erasure offers a low-cost fix. [[Minici2024-tf]] moves into adjacent network-based detection of coordinated information operations, combining language models with graph neural networks rather than relying on text classification alone. [[Tan2024-vl]] provides a broader survey anchor for the whole topic, mapping the wider landscape of LLM-based annotation and synthesis methods and challenges. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] offers a cautionary empirical case for computational personality/content inference more broadly: validated dark-personality traits correlate with self-reported toxic behavior but not with LLM/text-derived toxicity measures, underscoring a gap between psychological constructs and their surface linguistic manifestation relevant to any pipeline inferring psychological traits from text. Looking outward from content analysis to the practice of social science itself, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] test AI agents on reproducing computational findings and reviewing manuscripts respectively — early signals that the methodological questions raised here about validating LLMs-in-the-loop will soon extend from content coding to the entire research pipeline, from data analysis to peer review.
