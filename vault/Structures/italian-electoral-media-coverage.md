# Italian Electoral Media Coverage

## The Italian Core: From Second Screen to Coordinated Networks

The spine of this collection is a decade-long line of inquiry into how Italian elections are contested across, and increasingly co-produced by, television, Facebook, and Twitter. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] opens the arc with the 2013 general election, showing that Twitter functioned as a second screen tightly subordinated to broadcast logic: participation was narrow, politicians and broadcasters largely ignored the connected audience, and tweeting was dominated by opinion and requests for interaction rather than genuine agenda-building. This hybrid-media framing — TV and social media as interdependent rather than competing systems — sets up the questions that the later Italian studies pursue with increasingly computational tools.

[[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] moves the story to the 2018 election and reframes the object of study from participation to *insularity*: sources favored by M5S and League supporters are measurably more closed, and insularity trades off against a Facebook engagement signature in which highly insular sources are shared more than they are commented on. Crucially, M5S alone shows a strategic amplify-positive/contest-negative asymmetry, read as evidence of a populist "cyber party" exploiting a fragmented hybrid ecology rather than merely benefiting from echo chambers. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then pivots from audience-side insularity to supply-side manipulation, extending the same 2018 window into the 2019 European election and introducing "coordinated link sharing behavior" as an ecological, action-based alternative to content- or actor-centric disinformation detection — coordinated networks are shown to be dramatically more likely to circulate problematic domains, and their internal composition (political vs. deceptively non-political) predicts distinct sharing strategies.

## Scaling the Method: Toward the 2022 Snap Election and LLM-Era Tooling

The coordinated-behavior programme matures methodologically in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which builds a workflow to keep pace with adaptive bad actors during the 2022 snap election: rather than static lists, the paper iteratively surfaces new coordinated accounts from known ones, uncovering an M5S-aligned hyperpartisan network, a clickbait operation laundering political content through religious pages, and a proselytism network using Messenger bots — demonstrating that the same detection logic travels across ideological, commercial, and religious motivations. This actor-agnostic, behavior-first stance is the direct descendant of the coordination concept introduced in [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]].

Parallel to this, the MINE programme's Italian corpus becomes a testbed for the LLM turn in computational political communication. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news shared before the 2018 and 2022 elections, finding that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large consistently outperforms the Italian-specific UmBERTo model, with K-means outperforming HDBSCAN — a result that both validates and complicates the reuse of general-purpose multilingual embeddings for non-English political text. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds directly on this by documenting a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (classification, embedding, cluster labeling) across the same 2018/2022 elections, and — more importantly for the field's methodological self-understanding — argues that validating fully LLM-integrated pipelines requires new, task-specific protocols rather than the crowdsourced or single-metric evaluations adequate for earlier transformer models. Together these two papers turn the Italian election corpus into a proving ground for how automated narrative and topic detection should be validated going forward, a concern echoed in [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]]'s multimodal GPT-4o evaluation for visual political communication, albeit in a German rather than Italian setting.

## Platform Governance and the Politics of Visibility

A second cluster interrogates not what parties and citizens do on platforms, but what platforms do to what parties and citizens produce. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] applies breakpoint detection to over 2.5 million posts to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by 72% well before its announced global rollout, that the 2025 reversal produced only partial recovery, and — most consequentially — that extremist accounts compensated for per-post losses by dramatically increasing posting volume, ultimately *gaining* aggregate reach relative to mainstream politicians. This finding of asymmetric, opaque algorithmic governance dovetails with [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]]'s US-focused analysis of the same phenomenon at greater scale: sharing predicts views, but partisan-audience content is systematically penalized and journalistically rigorous content systematically rewarded, with both effects fluctuating in step with known Facebook governance interventions (e.g., 2020's "break the glass" measures). Read together, these two papers argue that platforms are active curators whose calibration shifts with political circumstance — a claim with direct implications for how researchers should interpret any cross-sectional visibility measurement in the Italian (or any) election corpus, including the insularity and coordination findings above.

## Beyond Misinformation: Delegitimization and Media Capture

[[Mosca2026-yh]] pushes the Italian material in a discursive direction, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification (SDSA) model that treats "fake news" accusations themselves as rhetorical weapons of negative campaigning rather than symptoms of an information disorder — media outlets do not merely report misinformation but selectively amplify accusations of it for political effect. This reframing resonates with [[Balluff2026-bv]]'s Austrian case, which uses a difference-in-differences design on two decades of news data to show that covert government-advertising arrangements (the "Inseratenaffäre") produced a visibility boost for Sebastian Kurz and more negative coverage of his rivals without a corresponding favorability bump — a "pay positive to go negative" pattern of media capture in an ostensibly consolidated democratic-corporatist system. Both papers push this topic beyond content-analysis measures of bias toward strategic, actor-driven accounts of how partisan parallelism is actively manufactured, complementing the coordination-based manipulation studies with a media-system and rhetorical lens.

## Widening the Comparative Frame

A substantial share of the collection extends the Italian toolkit and questions to other European contexts, effectively stress-testing the MINE programme's concepts outside their original case. [[Iris2026-pg]] scales the visibility question to five countries around the 2024 European Parliament election, finding a structural, cross-outlet overrepresentation of the Radical Right relative to polling and results — a finding that generalizes the insularity/attention asymmetries seen for Italian populists in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] to a transnational pattern. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] transplants the emotional-engagement question to Scandinavian Facebook politics, showing that anger (especially from populist parties) drives sharing while love-based content depresses it — an affective-affordance complement to the amplification/contestation dynamics documented for M5S. [[Oprea2025-lf]] and [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] extend the coordinated-behavior detection agenda of [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] to Romania's 2024 EP campaign and Germany's 2021 federal election respectively, documenting hyperactive-user amplification and paid/coordinated advertising dynamics that mirror the Italian networks structurally while varying in national regulatory context. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] offers a methodological corrective aimed at this whole comparative turn, arguing that news-sharing research (including much of the Italian and cross-national work here) has relied on oversimplified, outlet-level measures of political leaning and proposing a multi-level "political cartography" spanning story, outlet, and content.

## Infrastructure, Access, and the Future of the Field

Finally, a set of papers steps back from findings to ask whether this entire research programme is institutionally sustainable. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the EU Digital Services Act is reshaping — and unevenly reopening — platform data access for election researchers, a concern that directly underwrites the Meta Content Library-based methodology of [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]. [[Schulte2026-df]] makes the infrastructural argument explicit using the 2025 German election as a case study, contending that the field's bottleneck is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable, standardized multi-platform observability — a diagnosis that retroactively explains why so much of this topic's own methodological innovation (coordinated-account workflows, LLM validation protocols, embedding benchmarks) has had to be built from scratch rather than reused. Read as a whole, the collection traces an arc from the Italian 2013–2022 election cycle's substantive findings about insularity, coordination, and platform governance toward a self-aware, comparative, and infrastructure-conscious research programme whose Italian core remains the empirical anchor for methods now being exported across Europe.
