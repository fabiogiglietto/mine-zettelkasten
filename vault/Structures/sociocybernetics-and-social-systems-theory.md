# Sociocybernetics and Social Systems Theory

## From Network Society to Socially-Aware Infrastructure

The strand begins with macro-theoretical scene-setting: [[Castells2009-sle8]] establishes the network society as a new social morphology in which digital ICT-enabled networks restructure economy, culture, space, and time, producing a "space of flows" that increasingly displaces older organizational logics. This diagnosis of network society as *ontology* — not merely a description of technology but of a transformed social structure — sets the terms that later, more cybernetics-inflected papers in this collection take for granted: that communication infrastructures are not neutral pipes but co-constitutive of social order. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] picks up this thread at the level of Internet architecture itself, arguing that the shift from consumer to "prosumer" demands that social-capital constructs (trust, reach, influence, engagement) be built into networking design rather than treated as an afterthought. Where Castells offers a global political economy of networks, Sofia's paper operationalizes a homologous concern at the level of protocol and infrastructure, insisting that centrality metrics borrowed from social network analysis carry different meanings when transplanted into engineering contexts — an early instance of the recurring tension in this literature between technical and sociological readings of "the same" network structures.

## Cybernetic and Luhmannian Observation of Digital Communication

A second cluster turns explicitly to second-order cybernetics and Luhmannian systems theory as observational apparatuses for digital-media phenomena. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] reframes the "fake news" debate by relocating analytical attention from the intentions of content creators to the recursive judgments of propagators, using a micro–meso–macro model indebted to von Foerster, Bateson, and Luhmann to show that truth and falsity are observer-dependent distinctions reproduced anew at each act of sharing. This treats misinformation cascades as emergent, self-organizing communicative phenomena rather than as traceable to a single authorial intent — a move that directly parallels [[Trilling2024-slc8]]'s argument that "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles" should be replaced by a feedback-loop vocabulary drawn from cybernetics (Ashby) and complex-systems thinking. Trilling's typology of reinforcing versus self-regulating loops, and his insistence that system-level behavior cannot be read off from individual components, echoes Giglietto's insistence on emergent, non-reducible macro-cascades: both papers use classical cybernetic concepts (feedback, recursion, observer-dependence) to dissolve popular but conceptually loose metaphors of digital pathology into more precise, testable dynamic models. Together they demonstrate how sociocybernetics and second-order systems theory furnish tools for observing digital communication phenomena — misinformation spread, algorithmic personalization — without either technological determinism or moralizing about individual bad actors.

## Systems Theory Meets the Machine

The encounter between Luhmannian systems theory and computation sharpens further once artificial communicative agents enter the picture. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] applies Luhmann's distinction between communication and psychic/cognitive systems to argue that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is not understanding but a parasitic reliance on the repair work of human interlocutors: the chatbot participates in communication as a social system without possessing anything like the "understanding" ethnomethodology attributes to competent conversational partners. This is a direct application of the systems-theoretic axiom that communication, not consciousness, is the proper unit of sociological analysis — machines can be nodes in a communicative system while remaining opaque to the semantic content they process. This paper marks a hinge point in the topic: it uses classical Luhmannian theory defensively, to deflate hype about machine "understanding," whereas the final paper in the cluster uses social theory generatively, to *design* better machine social behavior.

## Toward a Formal Sociology of Artificial Agents

[[Ng2026-og]] completes the arc by proposing that social theory should function as a *structural prior* for agentic AI design rather than a retrospective diagnostic tool. Where Ptz2024 asks whether an LLM understands communication, Ng2026 asks what happens once many such agents are embedded in an interaction network and must be modeled as a population-level social system — formalized as a triple (f, g, G) of information exchange, influence dynamics, and network structure. Its four structural priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability), empirically grounded in a large real AI social network, effectively operationalize concerns that Castells, Sofia, Giglietto, and Trilling had already raised in less formal terms: that networks produce differentiated, path-dependent, mutually shaping dynamics that cannot be captured by individualistic or single-agent framings. In this sense Ng2026 reads as a computational-sociology culmination of the topic — taking Luhmann-adjacent, cybernetic insights about self-referential, emergent, feedback-driven social systems and translating them into a formal, testable multi-agent framework aimed squarely at contemporary agentic AI governance.

Read together, the six papers trace a coherent intellectual trajectory: from Castells' macro-theory of the network society, through Sofia's call to embed social capital in Internet architecture, to Giglietto's and Trilling's cybernetic re-descriptions of digital communication dynamics, to Ptz2024's Luhmannian deflation of machine understanding, and finally to Ng2026's formal reintroduction of social theory as a design constraint for artificial agents. The throughline is a persistent conviction — shared across sociocybernetics and social systems theory — that digital and computational phenomena are best understood not as engineering problems with social side-effects, but as social systems in their own right, governed by emergent, recursive, and network-constrained dynamics that classical sociological theory is uniquely positioned to formalize.
