# Political Polarization and Partisan Networks

## Brazil as a laboratory for partisan network dynamics

The empirical core of this collection is a sustained inquiry into Brazil's hyperpartisan digital ecosystem, anchored by [[Marino2026-slef]], a longitudinal study of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network across 2021–2023. Its central finding — that affective engagement (love vs. anger, sharing vs. commenting) is *unstable* and reorganizes sharply around events like Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt — directly challenges the "echo chamber as stable enclave" assumption that structures much of the wider polarization literature. This instability is not idiosyncratic: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel-survey data from the same 2022 election cycle to show that electoral misinformation belief, rather than platform exposure per se, drives declining political tolerance and eroding trust in results, with WhatsApp emerging as a distinctively risky "propaganda machine" for Bolsonaro voters. [[Kansaon2025-id]] traces the same messaging infrastructure at the level of coordinated inauthentic behavior, showing how WhatsApp groups translated online rumor into offline mobilization (doxxing of Supreme Court justices, January 8 protests) — a bridge between narrative and street-level polarization that [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp-deactivation experiment complements from the causal-inference side. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] extends the Brazilian case to elite communication, showing how parliamentarians on both sides of the Manaus healthcare crisis contested epistemic authority rather than simply reporting news, while [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents how a state-imposed platform ban (the 2024 X suspension) itself became a partisan sorting mechanism, durably shifting the platform's ideological composition rightward. Together with the older but foundational [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] on crowdsourced ad auditing, these studies establish Brazil as a recurring stress-test for theories developed on North American and European data, and implicitly argue — echoed explicitly in [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] — that polarization research remains too geographically narrow to generalize.

## What is polarization, and how should we know it?

A meta-level thread interrogates the conceptual and methodological foundations beneath all these empirical claims. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds that scholarly recommendations for depolarization are largely disconnected from the evidence base and instead reflect implicit deliberative-democratic norms — a critique of the field's normative infrastructure that resonates with [[Esau2025-tf]]'s attempt to theorize "destructive polarization" and propose deliberative reciprocity as a corrective, and with the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] calling on scientists to actively test depolarization interventions. [[Hartmann2025-px]] performs an analogous audit for echo chambers and filter bubbles specifically, arguing that disagreement across 129 studies stems largely from heterogeneous operationalizations rather than divergent realities — a diagnosis that [[Trilling2024-slc8]] answers with a reconstructive move, proposing to replace the echo-chamber/filter-bubble metaphor entirely with a feedback-loop framework capable of explaining why reinforcing dynamics rarely produce runaway radicalization. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis operationalizes this methodological worry empirically, showing that measurement choices for media partisanship and polarization outcomes systematically inflate or deflate observed relationships even though they rarely flip their direction. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] frames the entire cluster's ambition: an integrated, globally equitable polarization science, rather than one perpetually re-litigating US-centric findings.

## Echo chambers, curation, and the myth of the isolated bubble

A second thread directly tests whether ideologically segregated "bubbles" exist at all, and where. The classic [[Bakshy2015-rn]] Facebook study remains the touchstone null result — friend networks are homophilous but not hermetically sealed, and individual clicking behavior, not the algorithm, does most of the filtering. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates this by showing that source-level (domain) partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning apparently "moderate" outlets like the Wall Street Journal serve highly divergent partisan audiences depending on which specific story is shared — a networked-curation account of how homogeneity is manufactured *without* needing platform-level segregation. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] pushes this further on Reddit, finding virtually no cross-ideological overlap even between discursively similar subreddits, and proposing that polarization is "sedimented" as a structural precondition of platform architecture, moderation, and discourse rather than an emergent outcome of user choice. [[Zhu2026-tn]] internationalizes the question, showing that alternative-mainstream audience overlap depends on national media systems and the institutional access granted to populist parties — audience segregation is a political variable, not a technical constant. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] critiques the whole tradition methodologically, arguing that story-, outlet-, and content-level dynamics are conflated in most partisan-news-sharing research, while [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a partial remedy: a joint ideology-estimation model from URL-sharing behavior that reveals ideologically extreme politicians dominate the visible news environment and that electoral non-competitiveness predicts more polarized elite sharing.

## Algorithms, feeds, and the causal turn

Where the previous cluster is largely observational, a growing experimental literature asks whether feed algorithms *cause* polarization. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching users onto the algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward and — crucially — that this occurs through a persistence mechanism (users follow new accounts that outlast the algorithm), reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies discussed in [[Allen2025-ot]]. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky registered report extends this logic normatively, showing engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms of political dialogue, while a purpose-built "diversified extremity" algorithm mitigates these effects without harming user enjoyment — direct evidence that platform architecture, not just user psychology, is a lever. [[Allen2025-ot]] itself is a methodological bridge piece, situating LLM-based platform-independent experimentation (as used in the reranking study it discusses) within a widening toolkit necessitated by shrinking API access. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows the same architecture-matters logic from the moderation side: deplatforming a handful of central accounts in the Arizona Election Review network produced network-wide "ripple effects," collapsing the right-leaning cluster's hub structure far beyond the mechanical loss of banned nodes — evidence that targeted network interventions can outperform visibility-reduction alternatives.

## Affect, emotion, and the demand side of engagement

A distinct but related strand foregrounds *emotion* as the currency of partisan engagement, complementing Marino's affective-index approach. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research establishes the empirical baseline: anger reliably drives engagement with political and populist content, while love/haha track lifestyle content — a pattern [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] confirms cross-nationally in Scandinavian elections, where populist parties disproportionately attract "Angry" reactions that in turn predict sharing, and [[Larsson2026-ro]] confirms longitudinally within a single party (Norway's Progress Party), showing negative sentiment increasingly drives shares and comments as leadership changes reshape communication style. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows an adjacent, elite-mediated version of this dynamic in traditional media: British newspapers' selective citation of COVID-19 scientists tracks outlet partisanship even among mainstream researchers, suggesting affective/political sorting operates on expert sourcing, not just user reaction.

## Elites, strategy, and the political economy of misinformation

A large cluster reframes misinformation as an elite strategic practice rather than a diffuse informational pathology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis of parliamentary tweets is the anchor claim here: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts low-factuality sharing, but their *combination* — radical-right populism — does, tying misinformation to a legitimacy crisis of democratic institutions rather than a general social-media pathology. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this by showing institutional and ideological position act as "checks" on elected officials' misinformation sharing. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] jointly interrogate the crowdsourced alternative to fact-checking: Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats under X's Community Notes even though the system requires cross-partisan agreement, yet [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows this same bridging design structurally undermoderates the most polarizing (and electorally consequential) content — two sides of the same governance dilemma. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation itself as cultural narrative rather than false fact, a conceptual move paralleled by [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], whose evidence-frame and deep-storytelling frameworks show election-fraud rumors emerge from the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan frames supplied collaboratively by influencers and audiences — precisely the mechanism [[Yoo2026-ev]] documents at the level of rhetoric, where "fake news" itself becomes a weaponized label redrawing Hallin's spheres of legitimacy. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" and [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s finding that mere media narratives about interference can themselves heighten affective polarization suggest that discourse *about* misinformation is now a polarization vector in its own right — a concern [[Gattermann2025-yx]] extends to far-right electoral performance shaping disinformation anxiety across the EU, and [[Kim2026-wg]] grounds in hard behavioral consequence, showing geo-racially targeted 2016 voter-suppression ads measurably depressed turnout among exactly the populations targeted.

## Radicalization, coordination, and connective (counter)action beyond Brazil

A final cluster generalizes the Brazilian coordination findings to other regimes and movements. [[Bennett2025-xs]] offers the most ambitious integrative claim: democratic backsliding requires marrying "technocentric" disinformation research with "institutionalist" party theory via a notion of "digital surrogate organizations" that pull conservative parties toward illiberalism — a framework [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" operationalizes empirically through the Critical Race Theory case, showing ideologically extreme Republicans are rewarded with disproportionate engagement for aligning with hyper-partisan media networks. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework in the opposite direction, to authoritarian *counter*-mobilization, contrasting centralized pro-Navalny networks with modular, astroturf-like pro-government coordination on Russian Twitter. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] and [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] document comparable coordinated, centrally directed networks — CitizenGO's transnational anti-gender campaign and Serbia's SNS party — that mimic grassroots authenticity precisely to evade detection, a concern about *mimetic* rather than bot-farm manipulation that [[Kristensen2025-ni]] situates within debates over whether alternative platforms independently drive discursive polarization. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] pushes back against purely platform-architectural explanations of radicalization, arguing "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere through symbolic boundary work and affective identity performance across six platforms regardless of algorithmic differences — a claim that dovetails with [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review concluding that online/offline radicalization can no longer be meaningfully separated, and with [[Bailard2024-pj]]'s Granger-causal evidence that Proud Boys' online grievance and solidarity framing predicts subsequent offline violence in a reciprocal, roughly four-week cycle. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers an earlier, formative case (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie) of dissenting counter-hashtag discourse that anticipates these later coordination dynamics.

## Emerging frontiers: narrative, LLMs, and computational methods

Several papers point toward where the field is heading methodologically. [[Elfes2026-jb]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] extend narrative and framing analysis computationally — the former finding that video-level narrative polarization on YouTube is substantially *reduced* in comments, complicating assumptions about echo-chamber reinforcement. The LLM-persuasion cluster — [[Lee2026-je]] (LLMs infer political alignment from nonpolitical text), [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] (perceived bias attenuates LLM persuasiveness), [[Lin2025-xp]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] (AI systems out-persuading professional canvassers and elite debaters) — collectively signal a coming shift in how partisan influence and micro-targeting will be studied, raising the stakes for the governance and detection questions posed earlier by [[Bouchaud2026-np]] and [[Renault2025-uh]]. Read together, this structure note traces an arc from Brazil's Bolsonaro-era networks as an empirical anchor, through conceptual and methodological reckonings with what "polarization" even means, out to the algorithmic, affective, and increasingly AI-mediated mechanisms that sustain — and potentially could redesign — partisan digital publics worldwide.
