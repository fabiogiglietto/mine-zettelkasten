# LLMs in Computational Content Analysis

## The measurement problem: can LLMs stand in for human coders?

The methodological core of this literature is a validation question: do large language models produce annotations, classifications, and embeddings that are reliable enough to replace or augment human content analysis? Several papers offer direct empirical answers. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] form a linked pair examining Italian political news on Facebook: the former benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for unsupervised clustering, finding the general-purpose LLM embedding consistently more semantically coherent, while the latter reports on the full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline built on that finding—classification, clustering, and labeling—arguing that each stage needs its own bespoke validation protocol because LLM outputs are more general-purpose and variably granular than fine-tuned transformer outputs, and that crowdsourced validators are no longer adequate benchmarks since LLMs often outperform them. [[Meher2025-qb]] pushes fine-tuning rather than prompting as the path to reliability, showing QLoRA-adapted Llama 3.1 dramatically improving conflict-event classification, especially for rare categories, on consumer-grade hardware—an argument for domain adaptation over relying on off-the-shelf zero-shot competence. [[Brown2025-jk]] complicates the fairness side of this validation question: across four contentious annotation datasets, demographic bias in LLM-human agreement is small and dataset-specific rather than model-specific, with item difficulty (human label entropy) the dominant predictor of disagreement—suggesting that annotation validity work should center task difficulty rather than model selection or persona engineering. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this whole terrain from the NLP side, organizing the annotation-and-synthesis literature around generation, assessment, and downstream use.

A second methodological cluster interrogates embedding-space representations themselves. [[Fan2025-ut]] shows that pretrained sentence embeddings encode confounding "medium" signals—source, language, style—that bias similarity-based clustering and retrieval, and demonstrates that a simple linear concept-erasure step (LEACE) yields dramatic gains (e.g., multilingual Recall@1 rising from 0.175 to 0.826) without harming out-of-domain performance, positioning erasure as a low-cost default preprocessing step for any researcher pooling heterogeneous text sources. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the embedding-validation question into the visual domain, arguing that CNN-based image clustering captures only denotative content and proposing a VLLM-to-text-to-embedding pipeline that better recovers connotative, culturally-embedded meaning—at a small cost to denotative coherence but a large gain in interpretability via TF-IDF cluster summaries. Together with [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], which finds GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines (RetinaFace, FaceNet512, Google Cloud Vision) for face recognition and person-counting in political Instagram imagery, these papers argue that multimodal LLMs are becoming competitive general-purpose replacements for task-specific vision models in visual political communication research, while also surfacing possible gender bias in the older specialized tools.

## Scaling qualitative and interpretive work

A related strand asks how LLMs can be embedded into human-centered qualitative workflows without sacrificing interpretive depth. [[Ober2026-vd]] integrates topic modeling, LLM-assisted labeling, and iterative human codebook refinement for interview transcripts, treating topic modeling's mathematical transparency as a check against LLM opacity and hallucination while letting the LLM accelerate label generation. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] extend this logic to scaling problems in political text and survey research: the former shows that simply asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging many responses recovers reliable position estimates across languages and text types, while the latter proposes LLM-paired comparisons as a way to convert open-ended survey text into continuous latent scales, addressing the classic tradeoff between closed-ended tractability and open-ended richness. [[Lai2024-to]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] sit adjacent to this cluster, extending latent-variable and framing-detection methods (correspondence analysis on Reddit-sharing behavior; unsupervised ML/network/NLP pipelines) to new units of analysis (YouTube videos, polarized social media discourse) without necessarily using LLMs as the core engine, illustrating that the "scale up interpretation" problem long predates and now converges with LLM-based approaches.

## Substantive applications: framing, ideology, and narrative at scale

Once validated, these tools are put to work on communication-research questions that were previously hard to study at scale. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes Greimas' Actantial Model via an LLM to measure "narrative polarisation" on YouTube videos and comments about the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, finding that comments harmonize surface narratives across partisan groups even as deeper narrative motifs preserve difference—a finding only tractable because an LLM could annotate actantial roles at scale with human-comparable reliability. [[Waight2025-al]] tackles a cognate measurement problem, arguing that "narrative similarity" (same claims about the same events) is conceptually distinct from lexical, topical, or embedding-based similarity, and builds an LLM-based claim-distillation pipeline that dramatically outperforms text-reuse, topic-modeling, and Relatio baselines in recovering the diffusion of Russian "biolabs" disinformation—demonstrating concretely that estimator choice changes substantive conclusions about who is spreading state narratives. [[Arora2025-tx]] pushes framing analysis beyond fixed frame taxonomies and text-only analysis into a multi-modal register, jointly extracting textual, visual, and cross-modal frames from gun-violence coverage. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Waight2026-ts]] both use LLMs to infer or reveal politically-inflected signal that is not overtly political: the former shows GPT-4o and Llama can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse (exploiting culturally politicized terms), raising privacy concerns around scalable inference, while the latter demonstrates that state media control is embedded in and reproduced by LLM training data itself, causing systematically more pro-regime outputs in the languages of low-media-freedom countries—reframing training-data bias as a geopolitical rather than merely technical problem. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Marino2026-slef]] apply GPT-4-based classification to longitudinal political social media data (Norwegian Progress Party Facebook posts; Brazilian pro-Bolsonaro networks), the former validating zero-shot sentiment classification against human coders in a non-English setting to track negative campaigning and engagement, the latter combining LLM-based actor classification with time-series and regression methods to show hyperpartisan communities are far less stable than echo-chamber theory predicts. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] rounds out this cluster methodologically distinct in object (media coverage of AI itself) but consistent in approach, mixing computational and manual content analysis across 18,000 articles to map anthropomorphic framing of LLMs in journalism.

## LLMs as conversational and persuasive agents

A partially separate but overlapping strand treats LLMs not as annotators of existing text but as interactive agents whose outputs are themselves the object of communication research. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] establish that personalized, evidence-based AI dialogue durably shifts beliefs—on conspiracy theories and, more broadly, across climate, vaccination, and inequality attitudes—with effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science, challenging motivational accounts of belief resistance. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates this picture by showing that perceived political bias in the model sharply attenuates its persuasive power, reframing LLM persuasion as contingent on perceived neutrality rather than a fixed property of argument quality. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] extend the conversational-agent paradigm to misinformation resistance and trust: a chatbot-delivered "conversational inoculation" outperforms static reading/writing inoculation, while a "balanced news chatbot" is, counterintuitively, trusted more by high-conspiracy-belief users than low-belief users, undercutting assumptions from the distrust literature. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] offers a theoretical counterweight to this whole strand, arguing via Luhmann and conversation analysis that chatbot conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding—a caution against over-attributing agency to systems whose persuasive success may rest on human interpretive labor rather than genuine comprehension.

## Where LLMs still fail, and reasons for reflexivity

Running against the generally optimistic validation results is a countervailing set of papers documenting specific failure modes and urging methodological caution. [[Paci2025-ag]] shows that even strong models (GPT-4o-mini) fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in Italian political discourse, indicating a real limit on LLM pragmatic competence relevant to any study of manipulative or implicit political language. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] finds that LLMs are poor political fact-checkers on internal knowledge alone, that reasoning capabilities barely help, and that only curated retrieval-augmented context yields large gains—while also documenting an ideological skew toward left-leaning sources in web-search citations. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] finds a gap between validated psychometric dark-personality traits and their manifestation in computationally extracted linguistic features, a caution against assuming text-derived proxies capture stable psychological constructs. [[Balluff2026-if]] is the most directly programmatic of these critiques, arguing that communication research's rapid LLM adoption has ignored reproducibility, environmental, and bias costs, and calling for a "trade-off mindset" that prefers smaller, open, task-appropriate models over default LLM use. [[Matias2025-px]] argues from a different angle—participatory science—that rigorous AI evaluation requires lived-experience public involvement at multiple evaluation stages, not just technical benchmarking. Finally, two papers push the reflexivity question into the infrastructure of research itself: [[Alizadeh2026-es]] shows that frontier coding agents can now reproduce a large share of published social-science findings but are also susceptible to sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting, and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] describes an agentic pipeline for automating peer review of scientific manuscripts—together suggesting that the validation questions this literature poses about LLMs as content analysts are now recursively being asked of LLMs as scientists.

## The arc

Read together, these papers trace a movement from proof-of-concept validation (can an LLM replicate what a human coder, a CNN, or a BERT model already does?) toward substantive application (using validated LLM pipelines to answer new questions about framing, narrative, ideology, and influence at previously infeasible scale) and finally toward a second-order reflexivity (what do LLMs miss, whose interests do they encode, and what does it mean methodologically to rely on them at all). The throughline is that validation is never a one-time event: as [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Balluff2026-if]] both insist in different registers, every new application—every language, every modality, every task—reopens the question of whether an LLM's outputs are measuring what researchers think they are measuring.
