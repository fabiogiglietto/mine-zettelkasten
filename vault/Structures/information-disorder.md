# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

# Information Disorder & Disinformation Narratives

## Reframing the object of study: from false facts to narratives

The most conceptually consequential move across this collection is a shift away from treating disinformation as discrete, verifiable false claims toward understanding it as *narrative*—cross-platform, identity-affirming, and often only tenuously connected to falsity. [[Marwick2026-qd]] sets the terms most explicitly, arguing that successful disinformation behaves like story (characters, settings, plots, catchphrases) and spreads because it satisfies desire and produces catharsis, not because audiences are duped by facts. This is echoed and extended by [[Sadler2025-vu]], who develops a "hermeneutic realist" account in which narratives *disclose* aspects of reality in ethically loaded ways that cannot be reduced to true/false binaries, and by [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], whose second-order-cybernetics model of the hybrid news system insists that propagation—not the creator's intent—is the proper unit of analysis. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this narrative turn empirically, showing that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to a narrative, not single-claim exposure, predicts belief. Collective sensemaking approaches from the CSCW tradition—[[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]]—push further, showing how election-fraud "deep stories" are co-produced by influencers and audiences through quote-tweets and comments that supply implicit frames to otherwise accurate evidence, a dynamic also demonstrated at scale by [[Goel2025-iq]], who shows mainstream-news content is routinely repurposed into misleading narratives via headline and context choices invisible to domain-level blocklists. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies methodological infrastructure for this narrative-first agenda, offering an LLM-based measure of "narrative similarity" that outperforms text-reuse and topic-modeling approaches in tracking how Russian "biolabs" claims diffused into mainstream U.S. outlets. Practitioner-facing work converges on the same diagnosis: [[Ramos2026-qo]], reporting from Maldita.es's coverage of the Valencia floods, argues that claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with self-sustaining narrative clusters and proposes "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" as complements. [[Rogers2026-cy]], from the vera.ai project, operationalizes a related idea at the level of source ecosystems, mapping "post-truth spaces" and their structural proximity to mainstream discourse in Moldova's Facebook environment.

## Political function: elites, populism, and strategic weaponization

A second cluster treats disinformation and the "fake news" label itself as tools of elite political strategy rather than epistemic accidents. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds across 26 countries that misinformation-sharing by parliamentarians is driven specifically by the *combination* of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone—locating disinformation within a broader "disinformation order" tied to radical-right politics. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show the "fake news" *label* itself functioning as a delegitimizing rhetorical weapon in hyperpartisan and mainstream Italian media respectively, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance rather than describing actual fabrication. [[Marwick2025-vx]] complicates individual-level radicalization models by showing far-right "redpilling" as a slow socialization process in which disinformation functions as marshalled "evidence" for a self-image of rational skepticism, a theme paralleled by [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s companion concern with populist epistemology in [[Marwick2025-ov]], which finds ConspiracyTok creators—disproportionately young, non-white, female—engaged in a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously standpoint-empowering and disinformation-prone. State-level strategic communication appears in [[Makeev2026-ma]] (dual domestic/foreign propaganda in the Russo-Ukrainian war), [[Graham2025-gp]] (#IStandWithPutin exploiting platform trust infrastructure), and [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s narrative-ethics reading of pro-Russian NATO-blame tweets. Electoral consequences of elite-driven misinformation are traced longitudinally in Brazil by [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], both showing that belief in electoral fraud narratives—rather than mere social media use—erodes tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps as a key indirect vector; [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with causal evidence from a WhatsApp deactivation experiment. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] turn to citizen-side perception, the latter proposing a "hostile misinformation effect" mirroring the hostile media effect, in which partisans see their own side as disproportionately targeted.

## Platform ecosystems, coordination, and the question of scale

A substantial empirical vein interrogates *how* platforms structure exposure and whether coordinated manipulation is as consequential as feared. Several papers push back against alarmist assumptions: [[Budak2024-ef]] argues public discourse has diverged from evidence on exposure prevalence and algorithmic causation; [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram concentrated but attitudinally inert; [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] shows coordinated accounts in UK election retweet cascades achieve far less influence than optimal or even random placement would predict. Conversely, [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argues the standard "direct referrer" measurement paradigm radically *underestimates* platform effects on misinformation exposure once indirect and counterfactual pathways are modeled, with YouTube and Twitter showing outsized, partisan-asymmetric effects. Measurement-focused monitoring work—[[Vincent_undated-re]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Mosleh2024-op]]—tracks cross-platform and cross-country prevalence, engagement advantages of low-credibility accounts, and demographic skews (notably the concentration of untrustworthy sharing among older users). Coordination detection itself is a recurring methodological problem, addressed technically in [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] (Bluesky), [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] (Russian/Belarusian Telegram), [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] (Russian/Chinese protest and counter-mobilization dynamics), [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] (Serbia's SNS mimicking grassroots support), and theorized comprehensively in [[Thiele2025-ol]], which proposes a rational-choice attribution framework moving beyond Meta's "coordinated inauthentic behavior" concept. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] and [[Marino2023-9137f448]] each trace how alternative influence networks (anti-gender/CitizenGO; COVID-skeptic "Intellectual Dark Web" figures) achieve mainstreaming through legacy-media remediation and centralized coordination masquerading as organic advocacy—directly complementing [[Copland2025-em]]'s account of Sky News Australia as a node bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing propaganda pipelines. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] extends this to scientific discourse, showing a small contrarian coordinated network disproportionately amplifying credentialed anti-consensus experts. Platform-specific studies of TikTok ([[Bosch2024-hj]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]], [[Cabbuag2024-me]]) and Instagram ([[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Gardam2025-er]]) push the field toward affective, multimodal, and sonic dimensions of circulation that text-centric methods miss—an argument also central to YouTube's "ambient ideology" in [[Rieder2026-pp]] and the alternative influence network of Swedish far-right YouTubers in [[Askanius2026-de]].

## Mainstreaming, radicalization, and the far right

Several papers converge on "mainstreaming" as a distinct process from individual radicalization. Rothut2026-tz introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" via Querdenken's Telegram entanglement with far-right networks, while [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization scholarship insists the online/offline divide is no longer analytically tenable and calls for multi-level (macro/meso/micro) frameworks—directly complementing [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s and [[Askanius2026-de]]'s accounts of ideological normalization through humor, lifestyle branding, and monetization rather than overt extremism.

## Psychological effects, belief formation, and interventions

Individual-level belief dynamics are addressed by [[Adam2026-tz]], which combines panel and web-tracking data to model contagion and mitigation effects of alternative versus mainstream media on COVID conspiracy beliefs, and by [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], which implicates journalistic strategies themselves in the *formation* of new conspiracy beliefs. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as measurable supply-demand imbalances correlating with misinformation prevalence. On the intervention side, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that scalable Instagram-native prebunking against emotional manipulation produces durable effects, while [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes vulnerability to subsequent same-format fakes. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] tempers alarm about visual/AI disinformation, finding video outperforms text only for high-salience issues with abundant real footage—a finding in tension with [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s more alarmist "synthetic reality" framework and its "Generative AI Paradox" of rational evidence-discounting. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenyan deepfake experiment shows audience commentary, not the artifact alone, determines persuasive impact—reinforcing the collective-sensemaking thread. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] offers a structural rather than individual intervention, showing that blocking political elites from feeds causally reduces misinformation susceptibility without harming other attitudes, reframing exposure architecture itself as the lever.

## Fact-checking, platform governance, and institutional response

The volume closes on institutional questions. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examine fact-checkers' precarious rhetorical and material position amid platform dependency and Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking, with [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] empirically interrogating what replaces it—user flagging and Community Notes—finding both are shaped by disagreement and partisan asymmetry rather than veracity per se. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLM fact-checking is only reliable with curated retrieval, not raw model capability or web search, while [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] finds audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news. Regulatory and infrastructural debates appear in [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Crosset2026-mq]], which together frame the EU's Digital Services Act (Article 40 data access, content moderation as "security bureaucratisation") as a contested but consequential rebalancing of platform power—reinforced polemically by [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s call to hold platforms accountable for democratic backsliding. Finally, meta-level and cross-national framing pieces—[[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study, [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s critique of virus/warfare/pollution analogies, [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s call to de-WEIRD-ify the field, [[Nenno2025-xa]]'s 24-country news-values comparison, [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s everyday-misinformation diary study, and [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century trust-in-news trends—collectively argue that the field must move toward integrative, comparative, multi-level, and non-Western-centric research, a call also echoed by [[FitzGerald2025-nv]]'s and [[Kim2026-br]]'s attention to the temporal persistence and cross-national adaptability of manipulation campaigns, and by [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] and [[Xue2025-bp]], which show that toxicity and emotionality are not incidental but structurally embedded in how both misinformation and its correction circulate.
