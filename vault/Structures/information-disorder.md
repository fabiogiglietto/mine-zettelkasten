# Information Disorder and Disinformation Narratives

## From falsehoods to narratives: the shifting object of study

A pronounced conceptual reorientation runs across these papers, away from discrete false claims and toward *narratives*, *stories*, and *cultural framings* as the proper object of disinformation research. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this pivot explicit, arguing that successful disinformation operates as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story-telling with recognisable characters, catchphrases, and plots — a view echoed in [[Prochaska2025-ef]]'s account of "deep storytelling" around US elections and in [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame framework, where largely accurate evidence is repeatedly reframed into fraud narratives via quote-tweets and comments. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this argument philosophically, insisting on a hermeneutic-realist and narratological vocabulary (Ricoeur, Phelan) that can identify ethically problematic storytelling without any single false claim. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Waight2025-al]] translate the narrative turn into empirical instruments — the former measuring cumulative narrative exposure across a hybrid media ecosystem, the latter developing LLM-based cross-lingual narrative similarity metrics that reveal diffusion patterns invisible to lexical methods. [[Goel2025-iq]] delivers the strongest empirical justification for the shift: mainstream, factually accurate articles are systematically repurposed into misleading narratives, a phenomenon that source-level classifiers of "fake news" categorically miss.

## Elite architectures, radical-right populism, and the "creator problem"

A second thread reframes disinformation as an elite and institutional phenomenon rather than a bottom-up pathology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis shows that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation-sharing by parliamentarians; only their conjunction — radical-right populism — does. [[Tai2026-qk]] similarly locates institutional and ideological constraints on elite sharing behaviour, while [[Renault2025-uh]] demonstrates that Republican posts are flagged 2.3× more often than Democratic ones even under X's cross-partisan Community Notes algorithm, undermining claims of fact-checker bias. [[Bennett2025-xs]] provides the overarching institutional theory: digitally-networked "surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal) pull conservative parties toward illiberalism, integrating technocentric and institutionalist explanations of democratic backsliding. [[Copland2025-em]] complements this with a case study of Sky News Australia as a node in transnational right-wing network propaganda. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]]'s field experiment closes the loop empirically: blocking political elites in X feeds measurably reduces both misinformation exposure and susceptibility, suggesting that elite architectures — not individual credulity — drive belief accuracy. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates much of this by shifting attention from *injectors* to *propagators*, and [[Mosca2026-yh]] extends it into the terrain of strategic accusation.

## Platforms as objects and infrastructures of manipulation

Platform-specific and cross-platform studies complicate any monolithic account of "social media." [[Mosleh2024-op]] and especially [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argue that different platforms exert distinct, partisan-asymmetric effects, and that direct-referrer measurements dramatically underestimate platform influence relative to counterfactual "embedded" measures. TikTok emerges as a distinctive site: [[Bosch2024-hj]] foregrounds *sound* as a propaganda affordance in the Ukraine war; [[Karo2026-dn]] catalogues five evasion strategies used by jihadists; [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents ConspiracyTok's populist knowledge production by young, non-white creators; and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds Canadian immigration discourse there skewing pro-immigrant, contra Twitter-based findings. [[Vincent_undated-re]] provides monitoring evidence that TikTok also carries the highest exposure-weighted share of misinformation in Europe. Encrypted messaging enters the picture through [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Rossini2026-jn]] on WhatsApp in Brazil, [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] on pro-regime Telegram coordination in Russia and Belarus, and [[Rothut2026-or]] on Telegram-based protest mainstreaming. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] use Meta's URL datasets to map European and US Facebook exposure, while [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the "death of Twitter" and questions whether successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) address the underlying governance failures. Grokipedia, analysed by [[Triedman2025-uy]], suggests a new frontier where AI-generated encyclopedias inherit and amplify low-credibility sourcing.

## Coordination, propaganda infrastructures, and attribution

A cluster of papers investigates the industrial and organisational back-end of disinformation. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] treats the Internet Research Agency as a labour-market actor embedded in Russia's media economy, using OSINT on CVs to reconstruct its hierarchy. [[Thiele2025-ol]] proposes a rational-choice typology for attributing coordinated social media manipulation, moving beyond Meta's blurry "coordinated inauthentic behavior" concept. [[Kim2026-wg]] provides one of the strongest empirical cases for effects, showing geo-racially targeted IRA suppression ads reduced turnout among the targeted subpopulations. Bastos2025-ol identifies visual identity signatures of troll accounts; [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] recasts bots as agenda-builders influencing campaigns; [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking; [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] studies dual-use coordination during China's COVID protests. [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin case study argues that propaganda exploits — rather than evades — the "infrastructure of truth," while [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] tracks how such campaigns persist by opportunistically appropriating emerging events. [[Waight2026-ts]] extends the concern about state-media influence to LLM training data, showing that authoritarian media environments leak into commercial model outputs.

## Audiences, cognition, and identity

Where earlier misinformation research fixated on individual susceptibility, this corpus increasingly situates cognition within identity and community. [[Marwick2025-vx]] reframes "redpilling" as a slow socialisation process in which disinformation functions as "evidence" legitimating extremist identity. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows that Great Reset communities perform symbolic boundary work, reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honour. [[Adam2026-tz]] documents the "marriage" of predispositions and media exposure in conspiracy belief formation, with populists selectively engaging alternative media and counter-arguing mainstream debunks. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] introduces a "hostile misinformation effect" — citizens perceive their in-group as disproportionately targeted — that parallels the classic hostile media effect. [[Choi2026-bz]] documents modality-congruent carryover effects from deepfake authentication difficulty, and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shows that influencer-sourced misinformation generates qualitatively distinct toxicity via parasocial bonds. [[Rossini2026-jn]] links misinformation belief causally to declining political tolerance in Brazil, and [[Lyons2026-ca]] shows low-credibility health exposure is concentrated among older adults with conspiracist worldviews and dubious political news habits. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complicates all of this by showing that users' everyday encounters with misinformation cluster around mainstream news outlets and cost-of-living anxieties, not fringe conspiracies.

## Interventions: inoculation, correction, and their limits

The interventions literature has broadened well beyond fact-checking. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates scalable prebunking on Instagram with effects persisting five months; [[Szabo2026-rd]] introduces conversational LLM-based inoculation; [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that personalised GPT-4 dialogues durably reduce conspiracy belief by ~20%, challenging motivational accounts. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds fact-checks work equally well across textual, AI-image, and video modalities — and that generative-AI disinformation is not uniquely persuasive. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] links antivaccine tweets to vaccine refusals and downstream cases and deaths via a SIRVA epidemic model. Yet the corpus is candid about limits: [[Lieu2025-nl]] finds that CARDS content categories, not FLICC fallacies, drive climate-misinformation credibility; [[DeVerna2025-dl]] shows LLM fact-checkers only work with curated retrieval-augmented context; [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] document how European fact-checkers navigate platform dependence and Meta's post-2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking; [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that generative AI produces "emergent facts" that traditional epistemologies cannot adjudicate; and [[Xue2025-bp]] surfaces the emotional labour embedded in ostensibly neutral fact-checks.

## Reframing the field: skepticism, complexity, and comparativism

A final, self-reflexive strand reconsiders what misinformation research is *for* and whether it has overstated its object. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues that public discourse has diverged from empirical evidence, with exposure lower and algorithmic causation weaker than commonly claimed. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] critiques the dominant "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies and calls for multi-level complex-systems framings. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] imports six insights from climate psychology, urging behavioural rather than cognitive outcomes and portfolio interventions. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study similarly emphasises that no single intervention suffices and rates platform governance and journalism reform above fact-checking. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicises "misinformation-at-scale" as a structural feature of engagement-driven platforms. Ferrara — [[Emilio2026-ik]] — extends this to a "generative AI paradox" in which synthetic realities produce both credulity and cynicism. Comparativist correctives include [[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Nenno2025-xa]] on WEIRD/non-WEIRD news value differences, [[Gattermann2025-yx]] on far-right electoral performance and disinformation concerns, and [[Luhring2025-od]] on the pitfalls of NewsGuard-based measurement. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] warn that even journalistic language ("fake news," conspiracy coverage) can be complicit. Together with [[Gardam2025-er]]'s visual-first methodology, these papers signal a field maturing away from Anglo-American, text-centric, individual-effects paradigms toward comparative, narrative-sensitive, and infrastructurally aware inquiry.
