# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## Mapping the Field: From Taxonomies to Narratives to Measurement

The corpus assembled here traces a decade-long arc in which the study of "fake news" progressively abandons the simple true/false binary that animated early post-2016 scholarship, in favor of systemic, narrative, and infrastructural accounts of how misleading information circulates in what Chadwick calls the hybrid media system. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] supplies the foundational ontology — political communication as an interdependent assemblage of old and new media logics — that much subsequent work, explicitly or implicitly, operationalizes.

### The Definitional Problem and Its Dissolution

A recurring throughline is dissatisfaction with "fake news" as an analytical category. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] and [[Marwick2026-qd]] argue for shifting attention from creators' intent to the propagation and narrative work that actually drives circulation. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] explicitly reframes false content as one instantiation of a broader category of "Deceptive Information Operations," while [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] push the field toward "disinformation" as a structural, systemic phenomenon tied to institutional legitimacy crises rather than discrete fabrications. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] demonstrate empirically how "fake news" itself has become a weaponized rhetorical label — stripped of descriptive content and redeployed to delegitimize opponents — confirming Freelon and Wrubel's warning that the term should be analytically retired. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] extends this conceptual labor into the generative-AI era, proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category that neither positivist, constructivist, nor institutional fact-checking frameworks can capture.

### Narrative and Cultural Turns

A dense cluster of papers converges on narrative as the proper unit of analysis. [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], [[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], and [[Prochaska2026-pr]] collectively argue that disinformation is less about isolated false claims than about recurring, identity-affirming stories, tropes, and "deep stories" (Hochschild) that get collaboratively performed by elites, influencers, and audiences across platforms. [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s evidence-frame framework and [[Prochaska2025-ef]]'s dual-codebook analysis of U.S. election denial show how factually accurate "evidence" becomes misleading only through the frames applied to it — a finding echoed in [[Goel2025-iq]], which demonstrates quantitatively that mainstream, factually true articles are disproportionately co-shared with fake news to support misleading narratives, and in [[Ramos2026-qo]], which proposes "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" as crisis-response tools precisely because claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with narrative mutation. [[Wack2026-bt]] extends this logic to deepfakes in Kenya, showing that a synthetic video's persuasive impact is "socially negotiated" through surrounding comments rather than fixed at production — a participatory-disinformation argument that traces back to [[Starbird2019-qv]]'s foundational CSCW claim that information operations are collaborative "work" performed with, not merely upon, online crowds. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] push this further into questions of epistemology and socialization, showing how ConspiracyTok creators and "redpilled" far-right adherents construct legitimacy through populist, quasi-scientific evidentiary practices rather than through sudden exposure to falsehoods — directly challenging "exposure equals belief" models still implicit in much effects research.

### Measuring Prevalence and Reach: The Scale Debates

Running parallel to (and often in tension with) the narrative turn is a robust empirical tradition measuring how much misinformation actually circulates and reaches audiences. The foundational studies — [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] — established the now-canonical findings that fake news consumption is concentrated among small, older, more conservative subpopulations, and that falsehood spreads faster than truth primarily through human (not bot) sharing. [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Budak2024-ef]] represent a skeptical counter-current, arguing that public and scholarly alarm vastly outpaces the actual ecosystem-wide share of fake news in Americans' media diets. This "minimization" position is contested by [[Yang2023-cg]], which shows that link-based measures have drastically underestimated the problem by ignoring image-based political misinformation, and by [[Dahlke2026-sl34]], whose "embedded referrer paradigm" demonstrates that counterfactual, indirect platform effects on misinformation exposure are 1.5–15× larger than direct-referral estimates suggest. [[Guess2021-ym]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extend the Meta URL-Shares dataset tradition to characterize exposure patterns across age cohorts, countries, and time, with [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] explicitly correcting the field's US-centrism by documenting divergent German, French, and Italian trajectories. The PROMPT-style monitoring briefs ([[Vincent_undated-re]]) continue this measurement tradition into the present, tracking TikTok's rising mis/disinformation share and persistent low-credibility engagement advantages across EU platforms. [[Allcott2019-gn]] offers a complementary temporal diagnostic, showing Facebook/Twitter divergence in fake-news diffusion after 2016 platform interventions — a result that [[Rogers2026-zz]] updates through 2024, tracing Meta's successive discursive redefinitions of "problematic content" (fake news → false news → misinformation → borderline) alongside shrinking researcher data access, and showing that whatever remains viral is overwhelmingly right-coded.

### Elite, Partisan, and Political-Strategic Dimensions

A substantial cluster theorizes misinformation as elite-driven political strategy rather than mass pathology. [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] jointly establish the asymmetric, radicalized-right thesis that recurs throughout the corpus: [[Tornberg2025-ir]] confirms cross-nationally that radical-right populism — not populism or right-wing ideology alone — predicts elite misinformation sharing; [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates partisan out-group animus as the dominant psychological driver of fake-news sharing on Twitter; [[Renault2025-uh]] shows Republican-authored posts are flagged 2.3× more often even under crowdsourced Community Notes, undercutting claims of fact-checker bias; and [[Tai2026-qk]] examines how institutional and ideological positions condition elected officials' own sharing behavior. [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" in the Brexit/2019 UK election as an evolution of political spin rather than a rupture from it. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] offers causal, experimental confirmation of the elite-centrality thesis: blocking political elites from X feeds measurably reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing, reframing the problem as one of exposure architecture rather than individual pathology. Downstream political consequences are documented in the Brazilian and European election contexts: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] show electoral misinformation belief corrodes both political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) as a key indirect pathway ([[Ventura2025-sw]] offers a causal deactivation design on the same platform); [[Berlinski2023-rj]] demonstrates experimentally that unsubstantiated elite voter-fraud claims erode election confidence specifically among sympathetic partisans, with fact-checks failing to repair the damage; [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan-symmetric bias in perceiving one's own side as a disproportionate misinformation target; and [[Karlsson2026-hd]], [[Gattermann2025-yx]] examine how discourse *about* interference and far-right electoral performance itself shapes polarization and disinformation concern, independent of whether underlying campaigns succeed.

### Psychological Mechanisms and Correction

The cognitive-psychological tradition, anchored by [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] and [[Pennycook2021-jq]], reframes susceptibility as a problem of inattention and analytic reasoning rather than motivated partisanship — a claim in some tension with the elite/partisan-strategy cluster above, though [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel/web-tracking study attempts a synthesis, modeling conspiracy-belief formation as a "marriage" of media exposure and predisposition involving both contagion and backfire pathways. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] highlights the paradox that mainstream media, in the act of debunking, may be fake news's largest unwitting amplifier. Correction and prebunking interventions are tested at scale by [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] (Instagram field study) and theorized against media-choice conditions by [[Hameleers2026-mc]], who finds that visual/AI disinformation's persuasive edge over text is issue-dependent, not universal — tempering alarm about generative AI specifically. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes susceptibility to subsequent same-format content.

### Coordinated Manipulation, Platform Infrastructure, and Governance

A technical-infrastructural strand examines how coordination and platform architecture enable manipulation. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers a rational-choice typology for attributing coordinated campaigns to covert principals; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends bot-detection to the emergent Bluesky platform; [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] traces the adaptive persistence of manipulation campaigns across emerging events; [[Graham2025-gp]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] examine how propaganda (the #IStandWithPutin campaign; TikTok's sonic affordances) exploits rather than evades platform "infrastructures of truth." [[Rogers2026-cy]] develops a methodology for mapping "post-truth spaces" for fact-checkers, applied to Moldovan Facebook discourse ahead of EU-adjacent elections — directly serving the PROMPT-style monitoring mission this topic foregrounds. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] audits how Google's AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorial search queries, legitimizing newer conspiracies more readily than established ones. Governance and institutional response are examined through [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], both documenting fact-checkers' defensive rhetoric and the gap between Zuckerberg's censorship framing and actual (modest) content removal rates post-Meta's 2025 fact-checking exit; [[Holt2026-zq]] shows user-driven "False News" flagging on Facebook functions as political objection rather than veracity judgment, raising doubts about Community Notes as a substitute; and [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] extends governance critique to climate misinformation specifically, finding platforms favor performative "soft" moderation over enforceable rules. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] bookend this cluster with structural indictments: misinformation-at-scale is a built-in feature of engagement-driven business models, not a correctable bug, and platforms bear direct responsibility for democratic backsliding.

### Toward Complexity, Comparative Scope, and Synthesis

Several agenda-setting pieces explicitly diagnose the field's limitations and chart its future. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies (virus, warfare, pollution) each illuminate only one level of a multilevel complex system, demanding portfolio-style interventions. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes exactly this multi-level, multi-stakeholder logic, finding experts most confident in platform-governance and journalism-ecosystem interventions over individual-level fixes. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] press for comparative, cross-national frameworks beyond the WEIRD/US bias that still dominates the corpus, a corrective also pursued empirically by [[Nenno2025-xa]] (24-country news-values analysis) and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] (UK political polarization in scientist coverage). [[Hourigan2026-oc]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] return attention to everyday, affectively-laden audience experience — misinformation as encountered in mundane topics and emotionally charged fact-checks — while [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] and [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] examine source- and platform-specific dynamics (influencer-driven toxicity; TikTok immigration framing) that complicate one-size-fits-all models. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] and [[Scalco2026-bd]] close the loop methodologically, respectively proposing "networked exposure" to capture weaponized-but-accurate content circulation, and a quantitative "information void" detection method bridging demand-supply dynamics to downstream misinformation prevalence — evidence, across the whole corpus, that the field has moved decisively from asking *is this claim false* to asking *how does meaning, trust, and harm propagate through an entire hybrid system*.
