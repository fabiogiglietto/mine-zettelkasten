# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## The Shape of the Field: From Fake News Taxonomies to Information Disorder as System

The corpus assembled here traces an arc from foundational conceptual work asking "what is fake news, actually?" toward increasingly systemic, infrastructural, and narrative-centred accounts of how false and misleading information moves through hybrid media environments. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anchors this arc at its origin, arguing against creator-intent-centric definitions of fake news and proposing instead a propagator-centred, cybernetic model of circulation — a move that anticipates much of what follows. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] revisits this conceptual project decades later, showing that the dominant analogies for misinformation (virus, weapon, pollutant) each illuminate only one layer of a genuinely multilevel complex system, while [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study finds that even leading experts remain divided on basic definitional boundaries, favouring integrative multi-stakeholder responses over any "silver bullet." Together these pieces frame a recurring meta-argument of the topic: that misinformation research has been too atomistic — too focused on discrete false claims, isolated platforms, or individual cognition — and needs multi-level, systems-aware reframing.

## Disinformation as Narrative, Not Falsehood

A major throughline reconceives disinformation away from binary true/false judgments and toward narrative, identity, and meaning-making. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the case explicitly: successful disinformation behaves like cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story*, complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes — which is precisely why domain-level classification and fact-checking struggle to contain it. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further with a "hermeneutic realist" narratological framework, arguing that content can be referentially and ethically problematic without being straightforwardly false, breaking the field's habitual info/disinfo binary. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes the narrative turn empirically, showing that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to recurring narratives — not discrete claims — drives belief change in the U.S. context. The CSCW/sensemaking tradition extends this logic to elections: [[Starbird2025-jj]] develops an evidence-frame framework showing that election-fraud rumors typically combine *accurate* evidence with distorting political frames, while [[Prochaska2025-ef]] shows that the "how" of storytelling — style, allusion, implicit cueing — is as constitutive of meaning as claim content, and that 2022 rumors were often legible only through the "deep story" laid down in 2020. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] complements this with a symbolic-boundary-work account of Great Reset discourse, arguing that conspiracy communities cohere through affective identity work (reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honour) as much as through shared content or platform affordances. [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a methodological bridge for this narrative turn, using LLM pipelines to measure "narrative similarity" — showing that lexical-overlap methods miss the very diffusion of Russian "biolabs" narratives into mainstream U.S. outlets that narrative-level analysis reveals. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends this insight structurally: mainstream news content is routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives via co-sharing, meaning source-level trust ratings systematically underestimate the problem — a claim methodologically stress-tested by [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard, which shows how binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings that continuous scores would not.

## Political Elites, Parties, and the Strategic Weaponization of Truth-Claims

A second cluster treats misinformation and "fake news" rhetoric as elite political strategy rather than epidemic accident. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s cross-national analysis of 32 million parliamentarian tweets finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — their *conjunction* in radical-right populism does, tying the disinformation order to a broader legitimacy crisis of liberal institutions. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines how institutional role and ideology function as checks on elected officials' sharing behaviour, while [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show how "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon — a delegitimizing label deployed symmetrically by left and right alternative media, and theorized via a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] offers a granular case of this dynamic during Brazil's Manaus healthcare collapse, showing parliamentarians pursuing "epistemic authority" through distinct objectivity, authority, and proximity claims that bypass journalistic gatekeeping altogether. The Brazilian electoral context recurs across several papers — [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] jointly show that belief in electoral misinformation erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) acting as a distinctive, elite-weaponizable vector, a finding sharpened experimentally by [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation study. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] adds a reflexive twist: media narratives *about* election interference can themselves increase perceived polarization, independent of whether interference campaigns work — meaning alarmist disinformation discourse may be self-fulfilling. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" caps this cluster, showing citizens systematically believe their own political side is more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, a bias amplified by interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation.

## State and Organized Influence Operations

A distinct sub-literature treats disinformation as organized, often state-linked production. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the Internet Research Agency reframes the "troll factory" as an ordinary media-labor-market institution rather than a covert unit — recruitment spiked with Crimea, not the 2016 U.S. election. [[Copland2025-em]] documents Sky News Australia's transformation into a node of transnational "network propaganda," bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing ecosystems via a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a "dual-objective" model of wartime state propaganda (Russia/Ukraine) serving domestic legitimacy and foreign demoralization simultaneously, while [[Graham2025-gp]]'s #IStandWithPutin case study argues propaganda increasingly *exploits* rather than evades the platform infrastructures built for truth-verification. Detection methodology for such operations is treated systematically in [[Thiele2025-ol]], which builds a rational-choice attribution model and typology for coordinated social media manipulation, and in [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s comprehensive survey reconciling fragmented platform and academic definitions of coordinated behaviour around authenticity, harmfulness, orchestration, and time. Empirical detection studies proliferate around this theoretical scaffolding: [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] examine coordinated Twitter and Telegram activity in China, Russia, and Belarus; [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] extends detection to Bluesky; [[Pante2025-pq]] and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] scrutinize (and partly debunk) claims of durable inter-state coordination; [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as bot-assisted narrative contest; and Gaw2025-iq-adjacent [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceives influence operations in the 2022 Philippine elections as a form of political brokerage rather than isolated propaganda. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] extends this to civil-society actors, showing the transnational anti-gender organization CitizenGO running a centrally coordinated multilingual network that "mainstreams" its agenda much like a computational propaganda operation.

## Platform Structure, Deplatforming, and the Architecture of Exposure

A cluster of papers interrogates how platform design and moderation shape exposure rather than content per se. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts produced disproportionate "network ripple effects," destroying the AER cluster's authority structure even without removing most participants. [[Rieder2026-pp]] documents an analogous failure mode on YouTube: Andrew Tate persists via "ambient ideology" across thousands of decentralized channels that recommendation systems amplify faster than moderation can remove them — a dynamic theorized more generally by [[Trilling2024-slc8]], whose feedback-loop framework replaces the blunt "filter bubble"/"echo chamber" metaphors with testable propositions about reinforcing versus self-regulating dynamics. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] offer complementary large-scale measurement correctives: the former shows platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is dramatically underestimated by "direct referrer" models (1.5–15x larger under counterfactual "embedded" modeling), while the latter finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram is low on average, highly concentrated, and produces no measurable attitudinal effects when experimentally reduced — a genuine tension in the literature about how much platform exposure actually matters. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] intervenes causally at the level of elite visibility itself, showing that blocking political elites' posts (and mentions of them) reduces low-credibility exposure and improves factual knowledge without affecting affective polarization — reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] provide platform-scale European and U.S. measurement baselines via Meta's URL Shares dataset, the latter finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in Germany and Italy around election years despite stable view-level exposure. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] extend measurement to newer terrain: TikTok's persistently high (and rising) mis/disinformation prevalence, and stark cross-lingual disparities in human moderation staffing that leave millions of EU users in "blind spot" languages unmoderated.

## Platform-Specific Genres: TikTok, Instagram, and the Aestheticization of Extremism

Several papers zoom into platform-native genres through which conspiratorial and extremist content mainstreams. [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows "ConspiracyTok" is dominated by young, non-White, and female creators building intimacy and "populist knowledge production" via a "generous epistemology" that empowers marginalized standpoints even as it circulates disinformation, antisemitism, and transphobia. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Gardam2025-er]] extend the visual-first argument to Instagram, showing far-right and climate-denial content relies on positive affect, humour, and multimodal memetic softening that text-centric methods cannot capture. Rothut2026-dk-adjacent Rothut2026-yo (protest-facilitated mainstreaming) documents how the Querdenken Telegram movement's anti-elite framing bridged moderate sympathizers into far-right and conspiracist networks over two years, while its companion scoping review [[Rothut2026-wt]] synthesizes 25 years of online radicalization research into a macro/meso/micro, Lasswellian framework, concluding the internet is a catalyst rather than a cause and that online/offline separation is no longer conceptually tenable. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates assumptions imported from Twitter-centric immigration research, finding pro-immigration content actually dominates Canadian TikTok, with ideologically opposed users deploying shared platform "vernacular." [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] round out the platform-genre cluster by foregrounding sound as an under-examined propaganda technique on TikTok (Ukraine war) and "dogshow" humour as a vehicle for normalized incivility among Filipino Gen Z influencers, respectively.

## Fact-Checking Under Siege: Institutional, Rhetorical, and Epistemological Crises

The fact-checking institution itself is treated as embattled and epistemically contested. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] documents the empirical aftermath of Zuckerberg's 2025 dismantling of Meta's Third-Party Fact-Checking Program, showing content removal is rare and unevenly distributed, and that fact-checkers broadly favour transparency and counter-speech over deletion while distrusting Community Notes as a replacement. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform-funding relationships via apologia strategies of differentiation and transcendence, revealing an institution negotiating legitimacy under structural dependency. [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] interrogate the crowdsourced alternatives directly: Facebook users' "False News" flags track political disagreement more than falsity, while X's Community Notes — designed to be bias-resistant — still flags Republican posts 2.3 times more often than Democratic ones, undercutting the platforms' stated rationale for abandoning professional fact-checking. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] pushes the epistemological ground further, arguing generative AI outputs require a wholly new category — "emergent facts" — beyond evidence-based, interpretative, or rule-based factuality. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] supplies a sobering technical counterpoint: even reasoning- and search-enabled LLMs fail at political fact-checking without curated retrieval augmentation, and exhibit systematic left-leaning citation bias. [[Rogers2026-cy]] offers fact-checkers a practical mapping tool for locating "post-truth spaces" via network proximity to mainstream discourse, while [[Ramos2026-qo]] (from Maldita.es) argues, from the Valencia flood disaster, that claim-level fact-checking must be supplemented with narrative monitoring and argument-checking to keep pace with rapidly evolving conspiracy narratives. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] closes the loop by asking whether journalistic strategies themselves inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs — implicating the correction apparatus in the problem it aims to solve.

## Generative AI: New Vectors, New Vulnerabilities, New Trust Regimes

A rapidly growing cluster addresses generative AI's disruption of the information ecosystem. [[Emilio2026-ik]] frames the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic realities proliferate across a content-identity-interaction-institutions stack, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself and demanding a new research agenda around "epistemic security." [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s comparative audit of Grokipedia versus Wikipedia empirically substantiates this concern, finding heavy derivation from Wikipedia alongside systematically higher citation of unreliable and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial and political topics. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates alarmism about AI-generated visual disinformation experimentally, finding AI-image and video disinformation do not universally outperform text, with effects heavily issue-dependent (salient only for the Ukraine war, not MH370) — suggesting low-tech decontextualization may remain as potent as synthetic generation. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect," whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes vulnerability to subsequent same-format fakes. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan field experiment reframes deepfake impact as socially negotiated rather than fixed: audience comments shape whether a fabricated confession damages a politician, moderated by partisanship — evidence for "collective sensemaking" over individual credibility judgment. On the corrective side, [[Costello2024-bg]] shows personalized GPT-4 dialogues durably reduce conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, challenging motivational accounts of conspiracist entrenchment, while [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Dubey2026-bl]] explore conversational and chatbot-based interventions — the former showing LLM-powered "conversational inoculation" outperforms static reading/writing prebunks, the latter finding counterintuitively that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust "balanced news chatbots" *more* than low-belief users. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI drawing on the mass-media and social-media trust literatures, while [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] shows Google's AI Overviews respond unevenly to conspiratorial search framing — reliably debunking established theories like chemtrails but sometimes legitimizing newer ones like "15-minute cities" as open "debates."

## Structural Conditions: Information Voids, Trust Decline, and Diagnostic Infrastructure

A final cluster supplies diagnostic and infrastructural concepts for the field's broader ecology. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" — periods when reliable supply fails to meet demand — showing these gaps are empirically linked to lower credible-content share and higher misinformation prevalence around vaccine rollout events. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] extends exposure theory via "networked exposure," arguing ordinary content (mainstream news, Wikipedia) becomes politically weaponized once embedded in conspiracy-aligned networks — a claim resonant with [[Goel2025-iq]]'s co-sharing findings above. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century global analysis of trust in news complicates any simple "post-truth decline" narrative: trust erosion predates the internet, is not universal (rising in "Not Free" regimes), and the U.S. is a historical outlier rather than a global bellwether — a crucial corrective given how much disinformation theorizing is implicitly Americanized. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] makes this WEIRD-bias critique explicit as an agenda-setting editorial, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes it empirically, finding news-value patterns in flagged misinformation differ meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the U.S. and Brazil forming an outlier cluster. [[Adam2026-tz]] models the psychological mechanics of belief formation directly, showing conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of alternative-media contagion, mainstream-media mitigation, and populist/mistrust-driven selective exposure and counterarguing. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] examine affective and source dynamics in belief-adjacent behaviour: emotionality as a (norm-violating) fact-checking strategy, and influencer credibility as an infrastructural mechanism amplifying toxic responses to brand misinformation. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] extend the systemic view to health and science communication, showing political polarization in UK newspapers' citation of COVID scientists and Google News' algorithmic gatekeeping shaping which health topics generate public engagement. Finally, [[Lieu2025-nl]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] connect misinformation content typologies (CARDS/FLICC) and electoral context (far-right party performance) to differential public susceptibility and concern, while [[Donovan2025-ws]], [[Bruns2026-yv]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], and [[Swartz2026-zb]] provide macro-historical and polemical bookends — arguing respectively that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven platform business models, that Twitter's "death" marks a broader decline of prosocial public debate, that platforms bear direct accountability for democratic backsliding, and that scams have become the central, not peripheral, organizing logic of the platform economy itself. Read together, these pieces suggest the field's centre of gravity has shifted from asking "is this claim true?" toward asking how architectures — algorithmic, narrative, institutional, and now generative — structure what publics can even come to believe is knowable.
