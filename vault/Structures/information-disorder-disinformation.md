# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## The narrative turn: rethinking what disinformation *is*

A recurring current running through this collection is dissatisfaction with the "false claim vs. true claim" ontology that dominated post-2016 disinformation research. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens this line by arguing that creator-centred typologies of "fake news" cannot explain how falsehood actually circulates in a hybrid media system, proposing instead a micro/meso/macro model centred on propagators' judgments. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes this further, reframing disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative*—stories with characters, catchphrases, and moral arcs that travel through memes, satire, and late-night comedy, immune to domain-level fact-checking. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical apparatus for taking this seriously, importing narratology and hermeneutic realism to show that content can be referentially and ethically dubious without containing a single falsifiable lie, while [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] operationalise the narrative view empirically: the former shows that cumulative exposure to recurring disinformation narratives (not discrete claims) predicts belief, and the latter demonstrates that *true* mainstream articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives—a phenomenon invisible to source-list metrics. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extend the narrative/sensemaking lens to election-fraud rumouring on Twitter, showing how "evidence" and "frame" interact through quote-tweets and how 2020's deep story of fraud was later invoked wordlessly in 2022 through allusive cues ("Box 3," "Sharpie"). [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] complete this arc by showing that the term "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization rather than a descriptor of falsity, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance.

## Measuring exposure: prevalence, concentration, and the limits of the metric

A second cluster interrogates how much problematic content people actually encounter and whether prevailing measurement paradigms capture it. PROMPT-adjacent monitoring work—[[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]—tracks mis/disinformation trajectories across European platforms, finding rising TikTok prevalence and persistent low-credibility engagement advantages, alongside evidence that European untrustworthy-URL shares diverge sharply by country and election cycle even as *exposure* (Views) stays comparatively stable. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Budak2024-ef]] complicate alarmist readings at the aggregate level, showing concentrated, small-scale virality rather than ubiquitous exposure and challenging assumptions about algorithmic drivers. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this "concentration" finding to health misinformation, showing exposure clusters heavily among older, conspiracist-leaning users and travels cross-domain rather than via search or social referral. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] directly critiques the dominant "direct referrer" paradigm, proposing counterfactual "embedded referrer" simulations that reveal platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is many times larger than previously estimated—while also showing platform bans would disproportionately cut high-quality news. [[Scalco2026-bd]] offers a complementary supply-side innovation, operationalising "information voids" as measurable demand-supply imbalances that predict misinformation uptake. Methodologically, [[Luhring2025-od]] audits the NewsGuard infrastructure underlying much of this work, cautioning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort findings even where continuous scores are stable—a caution echoed by [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]], both of which stress that WEIRD-centric measurement (news values, platform dynamics) does not generalise globally.

## Who spreads it, and why: elites, platforms, and political incentive structures

Several papers relocate agency from anonymous "bad actors" to identifiable political and institutional incentives. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation-sharing across 26 countries—only their *combination*, radical-right populism, does. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this by showing institutional role and ideology jointly condition US officials' sharing behaviour, while [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents a concrete mechanism—covert advertising-for-coverage arrangements—through which political actors capture even Western media systems. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations themselves as a form of political brokerage in the 2022 Philippine elections, foregrounding the political-economic infrastructure of manipulation rather than isolated propagandists. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] provides an experimental causal test of elite-driven exposure, showing that simply hiding political-elite content on X reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing without affecting polarization—evidence that the problem is architectural, not merely dispositional. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicises this whole terrain, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven platform business models rather than an aberration, a claim [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] substantiates discursively by showing how Meta's Newsroom rhetorically externalises blame onto "inauthentic" actors while eliding its own platform affordances. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] turn to the fact-checking industry itself, showing how professional fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform partnerships and how Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking rarely translated into actual content removal, undercutting censorship narratives from both directions.

## Platform ecosystems, propaganda, and moderation politics

A platform-specific cluster examines how particular affordances shape disinformation's form and reach. [[Copland2025-em]] traces Sky News Australia's transformation into a transnational right-wing propaganda node via a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy; [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-examined, TikTok-specific propaganda technique in Ukraine-war content; [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] examine hashtag hijacking and real-time bot detection as tactics and countermeasures in the attention economy; and [[Graham2025-gp]] shows how #IStandWithPutin exploited rather than evaded the platform "infrastructure of truth." [[Tonneau2025-bv]] exposes a structural inequity underlying all of this: content-moderation workforces are drastically under-allocated to non-English and Global South languages, leaving millions of EU users effectively unmoderated. [[Triedman2025-uy]] extends platform-adjacent concerns to AI-generated knowledge infrastructure, finding Grokipedia derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically skewed toward low-quality and blacklisted sources on controversial topics—a novel front in the politics of algorithmic reference works.

## Generative AI: new threats, new verification tools

This cluster speaks most directly to vera.ai's mandate of AI-based verification. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the broadest theoretical frame, arguing GenAI's central risk is not discrete deepfakes but "synthetic realities" that induce a societal "epistemic tax," while [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemological category needed because GenAI outputs are plausible but evidentially unanchored. [[Choi2026-bz]] documents a psychological mechanism—modality-congruent carryover—by which difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes vulnerability to the next. On the verification side, [[DeVerna2025-dl]] provides a sobering benchmark: LLMs fact-check poorly on their own, and even web search and reasoning capabilities yield only moderate gains, whereas curated retrieval-augmented context improves performance dramatically—directly relevant to vera.ai-style tool design. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows the flip side: personalised, evidence-rich AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief by ~20%, challenging motivational accounts of belief entrenchment, and [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalises a related "conversational inoculation" chatbot that outperforms static reading/writing inoculation formats. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] rounds out the AI cluster from the audience side, finding readers cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news quality, though disclosure boosts short-term but not durable engagement.

## Countermeasures: prebunking, debunking, and their limits

Beyond AI-specific tools, the collection tracks the broader intervention literature. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] provides rare field-scale evidence that a 19-second Instagram prebunking ad durably improves fearmongering recognition at population scale. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates assumptions about visual/AI disinformation's unique persuasive power, showing text, AI-images, and video perform similarly except where authentic footage already exists to lend plausibility—and that fact-checks work roughly equally across modalities. [[Lieu2025-nl]] finds content category, not logical-fallacy type, drives climate misinformation's perceived veracity, with solution-attacking claims most persuasive and most polarizing. [[Xue2025-bp]] identifies an underexamined tension: fact-checks themselves lean on emotionality to drive engagement, in seeming contradiction with their objectivity claims. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] steps back to argue the whole intervention paradigm is too cognition-centric and too US/online-focused, urging misinformation researchers to borrow climate psychology's lessons on motivational drivers, systems thinking, and organised resistance to vested interests—a call [[Frischlich2025-vn]] answers directly by proposing a four-level complex-systems framework to replace reductive "virus/warfare/pollution" analogies, and [[Mahl2026-hc]] operationalises via Delphi consensus, finding experts rate platform governance and journalism-ecosystem reform above individual-level fixes like fact-checking.

## Conspiracy, radicalization, and identity-driven belief

A final substantial cluster concerns the psychological and cultural roots of belief. [[Adam2026-tz]] traces how alternative and mainstream media exposure combine with populist predispositions to produce both contagion and mitigation effects on COVID conspiracy belief, while [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] shows journalistic strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely diffuse existing ones. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] jointly overturn the stereotype of the isolated white male conspiracist: ConspiracyTok is dominated by young, diverse creators practising "populist knowledge production," while redpilling narratives reveal gradual socialisation rather than sudden conversion, with disinformation functioning as "evidence" that legitimises extremist identity. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review situates both within a broader radicalization literature, arguing the internet is catalyst rather than root cause and that online/offline separation is no longer tenable. [[Gardam2025-er]] contributes a visual-first methodology for analysing climate-denial conspiracy content on image-first platforms like Instagram. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] shows a milder, adjacent phenomenon—political polarization in which UK newspapers cite mainstream (not fringe) scientists selectively—demonstrating how legitimate scientific disagreement gets weaponised similarly to misinformation.

## Democratic consequences and cross-national variation

Closing the arc, several papers examine downstream political consequences and the need for comparative scope. [[Rossini2026-jn]] shows belief in electoral misinformation directly erodes political tolerance in Brazil, partly channelled through messaging-app news use, while [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends the WhatsApp/Global-South angle experimentally. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect"—the perception that one's own side is disproportionately targeted—paralleling the classic hostile media effect and correlating with lower institutional trust. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that even *discourse about* election interference, independent of its real efficacy, can heighten perceived and affective polarisation, suggesting alarmist reporting may itself carry democratic costs. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to shifts in public disinformation concern, and [[Mota2026-sl59]] broadens the frame geopolitically, showing Global North media frame AI as risk/threat while Global South media frame it as developmental opportunity—underscoring, alongside [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue call, this collection's cumulative argument: disinformation research must outgrow its WEIRD, platform-siloed, claim-centric origins and engage the narrative, infrastructural, political-economic, and increasingly AI-mediated systems within which false and misleading communication now circulates.
