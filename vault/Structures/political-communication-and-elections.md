# Political Communication and Elections

## Political Communication and Elections

This is by far the largest and most heterogeneous topic in the collection, and it functions less as a single research programme than as a shared empirical terrain — national elections and their surrounding digital ecologies — approached from at least five distinct angles: platform effects and echo chambers, coordinated/inauthentic behaviour, elite disinformation strategy, affective and narrative polarization, and the infrastructural politics of data access. What ties the corpus together is a recurring empirical anchor in the MINE programme's core cases — Italy 2018/2022, Germany 2021/2025, Brazil post-Bolsonaro — read comparatively against the dominant U.S. literature.

### The echo chamber debate and its slow deflation

A long methodological arc runs from early Twitter- and Facebook-based ideology-estimation work — [[Barbera2015-je]], [[Barbera2015-fw]], [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Messing2014-jc]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]] — through to the large industry-academic collaborations of the 2020s. These early papers established the now-canonical, cautiously optimistic finding: online networks are homophilous but contain substantial cross-cutting ties, and individual choice, more than algorithmic curation, drives selective exposure. The Meta2020 partnership papers — [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] — scaled this design to population-level field experiments and found precisely estimated null effects of algorithmic ranking and like-minded exposure on polarization, even as they documented steep, asymmetric ideological segregation concentrated on the political right. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest meta-critique of this entire research programme, arguing that even flagship, well-resourced platform collaborations suffer from low "temporal" and "poetic" validity and cannot outpace the platforms they study — a critique echoed institutionally by [[Bechmann2026-dr]], which calls for moving beyond individual causal-effects paradigms toward analysing "platform collective behaviour" as democratic infrastructure. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the entire measurement tradition further by showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" outlets are often bundles of highly polarized content — a methodological warning that also motivates Gaisbauer2025-br's call for multi-level "political cartography" of news sharing, and [[Eady2025-vm]]'s new joint model of elite and mass ideology built from sharing rather than following behaviour. [[Schulz2026-ts]] extends the debate into a new register, arguing that much observed online polarization is itself an artifact of who chooses to speak — "false polarization" via self-selection — while [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] and [[van-Eck2026-xg]] step back to note that polarization scholarship remains disciplinarily fragmented, geographically skewed toward the US, and — crucially — long on diagnosis and short on evidence-based depolarization prescriptions.

### Fake news, misinformation exposure, and the limits of individual persuasion

A parallel, more sceptical strand — [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Eady2023-xg]] — repeatedly finds that fake-news exposure and sharing, while real, are heavily concentrated among small, older, ideologically extreme subpopulations, and that any resulting attitudinal or behavioural effects are too small to detect credibly. [[Appel2026-qr]]'s study of Meta's 49 deceptive networks around the 2020 U.S. election reaches a structurally similar conclusion — reach is highly concentrated, largely indirect (via reshares), and correlational associations with downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. Yet this "minimal effects" consensus is increasingly contested from two directions. First, targeted and adversarial interventions can matter even when average effects are null: [[Kim2026-wg]]'s reanalysis of 2016 voter-suppression advertising finds a 17% turnout depression concentrated precisely among the geo-racially targeted subgroup, a heterogeneity that average-treatment-effect designs miss. Second, a cluster of Brazil-focused studies — [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], [[Ventura2025-sw]], [[Kansaon2025-id]] — shows that in messaging-app-centric, Global South information ecosystems, misinformation belief does erode political tolerance and trust in electoral outcomes, and that WhatsApp coordination fed directly into the January 8, 2023 uprising. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary analysis reframes misinformation sharing itself as elite political strategy — driven specifically by the interaction of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone — a claim that resonates with [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s account of how "CRT Republicans" cultivate ties to hyperpartisan media for factional advantage, and with [[Tai2026-qk]]'s finding that institutional role and ideology condition officials' own misinformation-sharing behaviour.

### Coordinated inauthentic behaviour: from Italy to Brazil to Serbia

The MINE programme's home institution has produced a sustained methodological lineage on coordinated link-sharing behaviour, running from [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] (which established insularity and coordination metrics on 2018/2019 Italian election data) through [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]'s workflow for continuously updating coordination lists, into the recent LLM-assisted pipelines of [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], and culminating in [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s discovery that Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach nearly a year before its announced rollout — while extremist accounts compensated through posting volume. This Italian thread sits alongside comparable coordination studies elsewhere: [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] and [[Keller2019-nk]] (UK 2019, Korea 2012) show coordination and automation are orthogonal and that even well-funded astroturfing campaigns have modest measurable impact; [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extend connective-action theory to authoritarian "connective counteraction" in Russia and China; [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents Serbia's SNS mimicking grassroots support to evade detection; and [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] examines coordinated advertising around the 2021 German election. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers a theoretical corrective across this literature, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposing a principal–agent typology for attributing campaigns to their commissioning actors — a framework [[Gaw2025-ru]] operationalizes ethnographically for Philippine influence-operation brokers. [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows deplatforming's "network ripple effects" flipping authority structures in the Arizona election-audit community, while [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a parallel but state-driven mechanism — Brazil's X ban producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's political composition.

### Narrative, affect, and the rhetoric of delegitimization

A distinct cluster treats disinformation and polarization as fundamentally narrative and affective phenomena rather than informational ones. [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s foundational reframing — disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story — is extended by [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s "deep story" and evidence-frame analyses of U.S. election-fraud rumouring, and by [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s computational operationalization of Greimas' actantial model to detect "narrative polarisation" beneath surface-level convergence in Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse. The rhetorical weaponization of "fake news" itself is examined by [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]], both arguing the term functions as a delegitimization tool rather than a description of falsity, echoing [[Gaber2022-bk]]'s account of "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election, where the veracity of a claim is irrelevant to its priming function. On the affective side, [[Marino2026-slef]] finds hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro engagement dynamically unstable rather than a fixed echo chamber, pivoting sharply around the 2023 coup attempt; [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Larsson2026-ro]], and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] trace how "Love" and "Angry" Facebook reactions structure sharing and reward populist negativity across Scandinavian and Norwegian contexts; and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by falsehoods. [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]], both on Brazilian parliamentarians, show how politicians adapt communicative style to platform incentives — favouring antagonism and epistemic-authority claims over substantive legislative content — with real democratic-accountability costs. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]] ground much of this in the underlying social-psychological architecture of affective polarization and partisan-motivated sharing, while [[Bail2018-fk]] delivers the classic cautionary finding that cross-partisan exposure can backfire rather than moderate.

### Comparative and cross-platform dynamics of the far right

A substantial cluster documents the digital infrastructure of radical-right and populist communication across borders and platforms. [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] provide the foundational "disinformation order" and asymmetric-radicalization framework that much subsequent work operationalizes; [[Bennett2025-xs]] extends this into a "digital surrogate organization" account of MAGA-era Republican radicalization, explicitly generalizable to European cases. [[Heft2021-ky]], [[Groebner2026-pc]], and [[Kakavand2026-kt]] map transnational and multi-platform far-right networks (hyperlinks, YouTube commenters, five-platform network structures in Germany), consistently finding the U.S. as a central hub and platform affordances — not ideology alone — shaping network form. [[Darius2026-xl]], [[Iris2026-pg]], [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], and [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] turn to the 2024 European Parliament elections specifically, showing populist radical-right parties enjoying disproportionate audience support (especially on TikTok), disproportionate mainstream-media visibility, and platform-tailored securitizing and AI-generated visual strategies. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] examine downstream consequences — far-right performance shaping disinformation concern, and media narratives about interference themselves fuelling perceived polarization even absent evidence of real effects. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] complicate the "insulated alternative sphere" thesis with audience-overlap network evidence contingent on national media systems and populist parties' institutional access, a finding paralleled by [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit study showing polarization as "sedimented" precondition rather than discursive outcome. State-level and authoritarian variants of these dynamics appear in [[Makeev2026-ma]], [[Beacken2026-zb]], and [[Kim2026-br]], extending the far-right/populism nexus to propaganda, GenAI, and cross-national troll operations in Korea.

### Platform governance, data access, and research infrastructure

Running beneath all of the above is an increasingly self-reflexive strand on the conditions of possibility for this research itself. Bastos2025-ya eulogizes the lost open-API era; [[Lukito2026-nb]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Schulte2026-df]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] chart the post-API landscape shaped by the EU's Digital Services Act, cataloguing access tools, persistent "price, proficiency, permission" barriers, and the political contestation surrounding Article 40 implementation. [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] audit platform content-moderation and election-policy regimes directly (YouTube, and five platforms' evolving election rules across three phases culminating in post-2022 rollback), while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows how X's Community Notes bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content — a democratic risk sharpened during elections. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] widens the lens to argue that platform privatization of public discourse compounds asymmetrically in the Global South, linking Brazil's failed anti-disinformation bill to broader "data colonialism" dynamics. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Oprea2025-lf]] document grassroots and manual auditing responses to this opacity (crowdsourced Facebook ad monitoring in Brazil, hyperactive-user detection in Romania), while [[Brady2026-ln]]'s registered-report field experiment on Bluesky demonstrates that algorithmic redesign — not just detection — can reduce norm misperception and partisan animosity without sacrificing platform enjoyment, offering one of the few constructive interventions in an otherwise diagnostic literature.

### Methodological and historical throughlines

Finally, a set of papers anchors this topic in its longer disciplinary history and methodological toolkit. [[Chadwick2013-ns]], [[Chadwick2015-ii]], and [[Hallin2004-kg]] supply the hybrid-media-system and comparative-media-systems frameworks that many empirical papers invoke to interpret cross-national variation, while [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] traces this hybridity back to 2013 Italian second-screen politics. [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and [[Freelon2020-yp]] provide foundational statements on cascade dynamics and disinformation's conceptual boundaries, respectively, and [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], [[Sarmiento2025-as]], [[Zhao2025-ny]], [[Gerard2025-br]], and [[Mosleh2024-op]] push the field's computational frontier — multimodal LLM annotation, unsupervised framing detection, cross-platform narrative embedding, and cross-platform engagement comparison — precisely the toolkit needed to keep pace with the fragmenting, multi-platform election environments (Bluesky, Truth Social, TikTok) that now define the field's empirical horizon, as seen in [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]]'s studies of cross-platform curation and synergistic politicization. [[Much2026-gu]]'s account of "gendered media spaces" in 2024 U.S. podcasting and [[Mota2026-sl59]]'s Global North/South comparison of AI framing suggest the topic's next expansion: beyond text-and-network analysis of elections toward the audio, visual, and infrastructural media that increasingly carry political communication's affective charge.
