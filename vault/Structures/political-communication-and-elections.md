# Political Communication & Elections

## The shape of the arc

This structure note gathers a large and rapidly growing MINE strand on how political information circulates, concentrates, and polarizes publics around elections. Read together, the papers trace a movement from *detecting coordinated manipulation* (the programme's founding empirical problem) to *theorizing why elites and audiences produce and consume such content* (narrative, rhetorical, and strategic accounts) to *interrogating the very metrics of polarization* (methodological self-scrutiny) and finally to *confronting the infrastructural conditions* — platform governance, data access, trust — under which any of this can still be studied. Italy, Brazil, and Germany function as recurring anchor cases, but the corpus increasingly reaches outward to Serbia, Russia, the Philippines, Scandinavia, and cross-national EU comparisons, pursuing exactly the kind of "globally equitable" polarization research that [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] argues is still missing.

## Coordination and the infrastructure of manipulation

The programme's methodological backbone is the detection of *coordinated* rather than merely *false* content. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] establishes this ecological, behavior-first paradigm for the 2018–2019 Italian elections, and [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] operationalizes it as a continuously updating workflow, surfacing not only political hyperpartisan networks but click-economy and religious-proselytism operations hiding in plain sight. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] frames the broader stakes: networked, weak-tie political organization and attention amplification outrun the regulatory and methodological tools built for an earlier, ad-centric conception of political communication.

This lineage extends geographically and technologically. In Brazil, [[Kansaon2025-id]] traces coordinated WhatsApp activity from "fake news" into real-world mobilization (the January 8 attacks), while [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] pioneers crowdsourced auditing of Facebook political ads in the 2018 election and [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Ventura2026-yc]] show how messaging-app deactivation and platform bans reshape partisan information flows and produce durable rightward "sorting ratchets." In Serbia, [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a subtler, harder-to-detect form of digital authoritarianism in which ruling-party CIB mimics authentic grassroots support; in Russia, [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action framework to *counter*-mobilization, showing pro-government astroturfing as a modular, team-based mirror to centralized pro-Navalny protest networks; in the Philippines, [[Gaw2025-ru]] reconceives influence operations as political brokerage with its own political-economic infrastructure. Closer to the programme's European core, [[Oprea2025-lf]] quantifies hyperactive-user amplification on Romanian party pages, [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a bot-assisted attention contest, and [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows that targeted deplatforming of a handful of accounts can flip an entire network's authority structure — a "network intervention" argument in tension with current visibility-throttling approaches. On the US side, [[Appel2026-qr]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] push back against null-effect orthodoxy: reach is extremely concentrated among a few networks and largely driven by reshares, but geo-racially targeted suppression advertising shows real, heterogeneous turnout effects invisible to average-treatment designs. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] extends the coordination lens to the 2021 German federal election, linking the Italian- and Brazilian-anchored methodology to the programme's German strand.

## Elite strategy: who spreads misinformation and why

A second cluster asks not how manipulation is organized but who does it and to what strategic end. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary dataset delivers the strand's signature finding: neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing, but their *combination* — radical-right populism — does, reframing misinformation as elite strategy rather than generic platform pathology. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] complements this with a "logic of connective faction," showing how the sudden rise of Critical Race Theory as a GOP talking point tracks a feedback loop between ideologically extreme legislators and hyper-partisan digital surrogates, an argument that [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes into a full theory of "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism — explicitly positioning itself against purely technocentric disinformation research. [[Tai2026-qk]] narrows the aperture to US elected officials, asking which institutional and ideological positions check or enable elite misinformation sharing.

The Brazilian election strand supplies some of the richest elite-level material. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus healthcare collapse through three distinct rhetorical postures (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and local brokerage), while [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel data to show that belief in electoral fraud misinformation both erodes political tolerance and undermines trust in results — with mainstream news use acting as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters, and WhatsApp group composition mattering more than platform use per se. [[Marino2026-slef]] adds a longitudinal twist: pro-Bolsonaro Facebook engagement is not the stable echo chamber the literature assumes but highly volatile, destabilized around Lula's inauguration and the January coup attempt. [[Makeev2026-ma]] provides an authoritarian counterpoint from the Russo-Ukrainian war, theorizing dual domestic/foreign propaganda objectives that parallel the strategic, audience-differentiated communication these Brazilian and US studies document empirically.

## Narrative and rhetoric: disinformation as story, not fact

A distinct theoretical current insists that misinformation is best understood as cultural narrative and rhetorical weapon rather than discrete false claims. [[Marwick2026-qd]] is the touchstone here, showing how "Pee Tape," "Crisis Actor," and "Lottery Manure" function as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic stories legible through catchphrases rather than facts. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] extend this sensemaking lens to election-fraud rumoring on Twitter, arguing that *how* a story is told — and the surrounding evidence-frame interactions in quote tweets — constitutes meaning as much as content does, with 2022 audiences interpreting ambiguous events through a 2020 "deep story" of voter fraud. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes narrative polarization computationally via Greimas' actantial model and an LLM pipeline, finding that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are far more narratively polarized than the comments beneath them — a genuinely novel dimension orthogonal to audience ideology. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both treat "fake news" itself as a weaponized rhetorical label — the former via Hallin's spheres in US alternative media, the latter via a proposed Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model for Italian newspapers — converging on the claim that accusations of fakeness function as negative-campaigning tools that reshape the boundaries of legitimate debate. [[De_Leon2025-qn]] generalizes this insight methodologically, arguing that even "ordinary" mainstream content becomes politically weaponized once absorbed into conspiracy-aligned networks, demanding a shift from direct to "networked" exposure as the unit of analysis.

## Polarization: measurement, mechanisms, and productive skepticism

A substantial and self-critical body of work interrogates polarization research itself. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review is damning: depolarization recommendations in the literature are almost never grounded in the evidence produced by the same studies, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals — a methodological warning that resonates through the rest of the cluster. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] challenges the "town square" model of platforms empirically, finding minimal cross-ideological overlap across 11 political subreddits and arguing polarization is "sedimented" through an entanglement of platform design, moderation, and discourse rather than emergent from discourse alone. [[Schulz2026-ts]] and [[Green2025-ap]] independently complicate what we think we are measuring when we measure online polarization: the former shows that visible discourse is more polarized than underlying attitudes because ideologically stronger users self-select into posting ("false polarization"), while the latter shows that domain-level partisanship scores systematically mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, especially for "moderate" outlets. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis makes the meta-point starkest: 504 alternative operationalizations of media slant and polarization change effect *magnitude* substantially, though not direction — a companion piece to [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s critique that most news-sharing research still relies on oversimplified, outlet-level, single-country measures of political leaning.

Normative and psychological accounts sit alongside these measurement critiques. [[Esau2025-tf]] proposes deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as theoretical antidotes to "destructive" polarization; [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel "hostile misinformation effect" — citizens believe their in-group is disproportionately targeted by misinformation — across three 2024 European election contexts; and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows experimentally that mere media *narratives* about election interference can raise perceived societal division, suggesting alarmist interference discourse may itself be a polarizing force independent of whether interference campaigns work. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] gesture toward further empirical and methodological extensions — alternative-platform effects and unsupervised, non-predefined framing detection, respectively — while [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the field's foundational counterpoint, showing that individual click choices, not Facebook's algorithm, do most of the work suppressing cross-cutting exposure.

## National and cross-national election case studies

The Italian election strand functions as something close to a long-running laboratory for the whole programme's methods. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establish early hybrid-media baselines (#JeNeSuisPasCharlie dissent; Twitter-as-second-screen during 2013 talk shows), while [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows the Five Star Movement's distinctive capacity to mobilize insular, strategically asymmetric social-media attention. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the URL-trustworthiness paradigm cross-nationally to Germany, France, and Italy, finding untrustworthy sharing spikes in election years even as exposure stays comparatively stable, and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] turn to methodological validation of LLM-based clustering and classification pipelines for Italian political news — directly relevant to [[Paci2025-ag]]'s sobering finding that current LLMs still fail to reliably interpret implicit, manipulative content in Italian political speech. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] closes this strand with a policy-relevant intervention, showing Meta's political-content-reduction policy suppressed mainstream Italian politicians' Facebook reach far more than extremist accounts, which compensated through posting volume — a transparency-deficit finding with direct bearing on the platform-governance cluster below.

Beyond Italy and Brazil, the programme's German strand ([[Schulte2026-df]], [[Kakavand2026-kt]]) documents the infrastructural and platform-affordance challenges of multi-platform electoral analysis, showing German far-right networks assume structurally distinct shapes on Twitter, Telegram, YouTube, Instagram, and Facebook rather than replicating uniformly. The EU-wide and Scandinavian material rounds out the comparative picture: [[Iris2026-pg]] documents systematic radical-right overrepresentation in 2024 European election news coverage; [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric platform-by-platform, prioritizing TikTok memes for younger audiences; [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance to voter disinformation concerns; [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] track negative sentiment and "Angry" reactions as engagement drivers for Nordic populist parties; and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] finds UK newspapers politically sorting which COVID-19 scientists they cite, echoing the broader pattern of media outlets amplifying congenial rather than simply accurate sources.

## Platform governance, data access, and infrastructure

A late but increasingly central concern is whether any of this research can continue to be done at all. [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] together map the post-API research landscape: DSA-mandated access partially reopens data but unevenly across platforms and researcher communities, election-specific platform policies have converged and then rolled back since 2022–2023, and researchers face a "price, proficiency, or permission" triad of access barriers that skews the field toward WEIRD contexts. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] illustrate the stakes empirically — undisclosed algorithmic suppression, fragmented scraping infrastructures — while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows that even ostensibly participatory moderation systems like Community Notes structurally undermoderate polarizing content by design, a finding with direct electoral-integrity implications. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment offers a rare constructive counterpoint, showing that a "diversified extremity" algorithm can reduce amplification of intergroup, moralized, emotional content and improve social-norm perception without sacrificing platform enjoyment — reframing the platform-effects debate from persuasion to algorithm-mediated social learning. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] extend the underlying empirical base (Facebook diffusion trees; seven-platform engagement comparison), and [[Eady2025-vm]] contributes a methodological advance allowing media, politician, and public ideology to be jointly estimated from sharing behavior alone — showing, among other things, that electorally uncompetitive districts produce more polarized elite sharing. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] surveys the accumulated evidence on Facebook Reactions as a proxy for exactly the affective dynamics several papers above (Larsson, Kalsnes, Marino2026) exploit empirically.

## Trust, visibility, and the wider media environment

The note closes on the broadest frame. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s near half-century, 115-country analysis dismantles the "golden age" myth of media trust, showing a real but non-universal decline that accelerates around 2000 — with trust actually *rising* in "Not Free" media environments, a troubling counterpoint to the transparency and accountability aspirations of the platform-governance cluster. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] extend the visibility question outward: audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media is generally low but varies with populist parties' institutional access, while a genuinely transnational far-right audience has coalesced on YouTube around Fox News as a US-centered hub, with Sky News Australia acting as connective tissue. Together with [[Iris2026-pg]]'s finding of disproportionate radical-right news visibility, these studies suggest that the programme's next arc lies less in detecting individual bad actors than in mapping the transnational, algorithmically and institutionally mediated visibility structures within which elite strategy, coordinated behavior, and narrative contestation all now operate.
