# Generative AI and Synthetic Media

## The arc of inquiry

The papers gathered here trace a single trajectory: from the *mechanics* of how generative AI persuades, through the *artifacts* it produces (deepfakes, synthetic images, AI-generated encyclopedias), to the *systemic* consequences for trust, epistemology, and democratic infrastructure. Read together, they suggest a shift in scholarly concern — from asking "can AI fool people?" to asking "what happens to a public sphere in which nobody can any longer assume good faith or verifiable provenance?" [[Tornberg2026-lc]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] frame this shift most explicitly, arguing that the object of study is no longer discrete synthetic artifacts but entire *synthetic realities* and *post-social* media ecologies in which human and machine-generated content are functionally indistinguishable.

## The mechanics of AI persuasion

A cluster of large-scale experimental papers establishes what actually drives conversational AI's persuasive power. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and its follow-up [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] converge on a striking finding: it is not model scale, personalization, or rapport that makes AI persuasive, but *information density* — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a system can produce per conversation. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this further by pitting AI against elite human debaters and professional canvassers, showing AI wins even against coached experts, and that artificially throttling AI to human typing speed collapses its advantage — strong evidence that throughput, not deception per se, is the mechanism. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this across four real elections, finding AI persuades chiefly through relevant facts rather than manipulation, though with a troubling asymmetry: models advocating right-leaning candidates are systematically less accurate. Read against [[Hackenburg2025-dj]]'s own finding of a persuasion–accuracy trade-off (the same levers that boost persuasiveness degrade factual reliability), this body of work complicates the simple "AI = disinformation weapon" narrative — the danger is less inherent deceptiveness than a volume-based erosion of the link between persuasiveness and truth.

## Visual persuasion and synthetic artifacts

A second thread turns to the visual and audiovisual register. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers the most granular case study in this set — coordinated Facebook gambling promotion — showing how generative AI does not invent new persuasion drivers so much as *intensify and recombine* established ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization) at industrial scale, with posting volume exploding by four orders of magnitude after ChatGPT's launch. This exemplifies the course's titular concern with "synthetic gambling promotion" directly. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] provides a useful corrective, however: in controlled experiments, AI-generated images do not universally outperform text or even low-tech decontextualized video — visual disinformation's power is issue-bound, contingent on plausible existing footage, suggesting that alarm about generative AI's persuasive supremacy may sometimes outrun the evidence. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a psychological mechanism at the reception end: difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over, modality-congruently, to shape confidence and vulnerability toward subsequent fakes — meaning the harm of synthetic media is cumulative and psychological, not just located in any single artifact's persuasive force.

## Coordination, swarms, and information operations

Beyond individual persuasion, several papers examine AI's role in *coordinated* manipulation. [[Orlando2025-ul]] provides an important mechanistic study, using generative agent-based modeling to show that networked LLM agents spontaneously reproduce hallmark features of real information operations — narrative convergence, synchronized amplification, rapid hashtag adoption — and that mere mutual awareness among agents (short of explicit collusion) suffices to generate near-maximal coordination. This gives empirical texture to the more speculative but urgent claims of [[Schroeder2026-im]], which theorizes "malicious AI swarms" as a qualitative escalation beyond botnets, capable of manufacturing consensus, poisoning training data ("LLM grooming"), and eroding institutional trust — while cautioning that symmetric "pro-social" swarms cannot simply counter them, since the attention economy structurally rewards bad-faith tactics. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] grounds these abstractions in the financial-fraud domain, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of platforms, telecoms, and money-laundering networks that let AI-driven deception operate at scale, and arguing — consonant with [[Schroeder2026-im]]'s skepticism of voluntary compliance — that liability must shift from individual victims to gatekeeping intermediaries.

## Epistemic and institutional erosion

A more theoretical cluster asks what generative AI does to the *category of fact itself*. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that conventional fact-checking epistemologies (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot accommodate AI outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category demanding relational rather than binary verification. [[Emilio2026-ik]] scales this concern up into the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic content saturates the information environment, societies may rationally begin discounting *all* digital evidence, producing an epistemic tax that benefits strategic deniers regardless of whether any given artifact is fake. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical dissection of Grokipedia is a vivid case study of this dynamic in action — an AI-generated encyclopedia that is derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically cites lower-quality and ideologically slanted sources, illustrating how generative rewriting can quietly launder unreliable provenance into ostensibly authoritative form. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] provides the widest-angle diagnosis, arguing that the very paradigm of "social media" is dissolving under algorithmic recommendation, user retreat into private spheres, and AI-mediated communication — with AI chatbots now used by more Americans than post on major platforms, marking a genuine paradigm shift that existing methods (network analysis, content analysis) are ill-equipped to study.

## Reception, governance, and comparative context

The final cluster turns to how audiences and institutions are responding. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] offers a reassuring counterpoint to alarmist framings: readers judge AI-generated and human-written news as equivalent in quality, and disclosure of AI authorship even boosts short-term willingness to continue reading — though this doesn't translate into durable acceptance of AI journalism, cautioning against over-generalizing single-study engagement effects. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] examines how news media themselves frame LLMs, finding that anthropomorphic "mentalistic-agentic" coverage is real but far from dominant, and driven by editorial idiosyncrasy rather than regional culture — an important check on assumptions that hype is monolithic or geographically determined. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] complicates geographic and platform generalizations further: contrary to X/Twitter-based findings, Canadian TikTok immigration discourse skews pro-immigration, though generative AI still appears as a tool for racist "speculative worldbuilding," showing that platform affordances and generative tools can be mobilized by all sides of a debate. [[Beacken2026-zb]] insists on comparative, structurally grounded analysis over techno-determinism, showing across six democratically weakened states that GenAI propaganda adoption is shaped by local political-economic conditions rather than following one inevitable trajectory. Finally, [[Unknown2025-qj]] turns to the governance response itself, reframing red-teaming as a sociotechnical, public-interest practice rather than a narrow cybersecurity exercise — a fitting bookend, since it asks who gets to define "harm" in exactly the systems the rest of this literature shows are already reshaping persuasion, coordination, and truth.

## Synthesis

Taken together, these eighteen papers resist a single alarmist or dismissive verdict. The persuasion-mechanics literature ([[Hackenburg2025-dj]], [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[Lin2025-xp]]) shows real, measurable AI advantages rooted in mundane information throughput rather than exotic manipulation; the visual and platform studies ([[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], [[Hameleers2026-mc]], [[Hollingshead2026-vx]]) show that context, platform affordance, and issue salience matter as much as the technology; and the systemic/epistemic theorists ([[Tornberg2026-lc]], [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Dierickx2026-tw]], [[Schroeder2026-im]]) push us to see the cumulative, infrastructural stakes beyond any single deepfake or scam. The governance-oriented papers ([[Schiffrin_undated-gi]], [[Unknown2025-qj]], [[Beacken2026-zb]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]]) close the loop by insisting that responses must be structural — targeting gatekeepers, incentives, and institutional design — rather than resting on individual media literacy or artifact-level detection alone.
