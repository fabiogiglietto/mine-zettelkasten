# Generative AI & Synthetic Media

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster of papers tracks a single historical hinge: the point at which generative AI stopped being a curiosity and became infrastructure — for deception, for persuasion, for culture production, and for the ordinary texture of the media environment itself. Read together, the papers move from concrete, high-stakes cases of AI-enabled fraud and propaganda, through theoretical attempts to name what has actually changed epistemically, out to broader diagnoses of a "post-social" media order and a emergent vernacular culture of "slop." The throughline is that synthetic media does not introduce a wholly new kind of lie so much as it industrializes, accelerates, and launders existing persuasive and manipulative logics — commercial, political, and cultural — while eroding the shared evidentiary ground that let audiences, regulators, and institutions tell the difference.

## Synthetic media as industrial-scale deception infrastructure

The empirical heart of the topic is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], which documents an exponential, ChatGPT-coincident surge in AI-generated imagery used by coordinated Facebook gambling networks — a case study in how generative tools intensify, rather than replace, known persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization) while exploiting Meta's organic/paid regulatory grey zone. This sits alongside [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]], which maps a parallel "scam ecosystem" of deepfake-enabled financial fraud and argues that liability must shift from individual victims to platform and telecom gatekeepers — a policy response structurally mirrored by the gambling case's call to regulate amplification infrastructure rather than content after the fact. Both papers converge on a diagnosis: AI lowers the cost and raises the scale of deception, but the exploitable gap is organizational and regulatory, not merely technical.

Audience-side work complicates any simple story of gullibility. [[Hove2026-sl49]] finds that even attentive, high-digital-literacy populations perform barely better than chance at spotting AI-generated images — worse, in fact, than a naive 50/50 guess — suggesting that individual-level detection is an inadequate safeguard. [[Choi2026-bz]] adds a dynamic, carryover dimension: difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content shapes confidence and vulnerability toward *subsequent* fakes of the same modality, implying that deception effects compound across a media diet rather than resetting with each exposure. [[Wack2026-bt]] pushes further into the social, showing that a deepfake's persuasive damage is not fixed at the moment of production but is negotiated collectively through surrounding commentary — credulous comments deepen harm, skeptical ones partially repair it — reframing deepfakes as "flexibly deceptive cues" within a participatory disinformation ecosystem rather than discrete artifacts with stable effects.

## Political persuasion, propaganda, and electoral influence

A second strand asks what generative AI actually does to political persuasion, as distinct from fraud. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] run large controlled experiments and arrive at a genuinely unsettling finding: conversational AI persuades mainly through information density — more fact-checkable claims, not manipulative psychological tactics — yet the same levers that boost persuasion (post-training, information-heavy prompting) degrade factual accuracy, and models advocating right-leaning candidates are measurably less accurate than those advocating left-leaning ones. This empirically grounds worries that elsewhere remain speculative, while also pushing back on alarmist assumptions about microtargeting and personalization, whose effects both papers find to be comparatively small.

Where these two papers focus on dyadic persuasion, [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Orlando2025-ul]] scale the question up to coordinated, multi-agent manipulation. Schroeder et al. theorize "malicious AI swarms" that fuse LLM reasoning with autonomous multi-agent coordination to fabricate consensus and poison training data ("LLM grooming"), while Orlando et al. empirically demonstrate, via generative agent-based modeling, that LLM agents spontaneously reproduce real-world information-operation coordination patterns — network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — with mere mutual awareness among agents nearly sufficient to match explicit top-down direction. Together they suggest that coordinated inauthentic behavior may increasingly require no human orchestrator at all.

Comparative and case-based work grounds these dynamics in actual political contexts: [[Beacken2026-zb]] insists that GenAI propaganda's form and intensity vary by political, economic, and social structure across weakening democracies (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey), resisting techno-deterministic narratives; [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Crichton2026-kn]] document the far right's operational embrace of synthetic imagery, with Crichton's "re-generative pipeline" showing how commercial image models sanitize extremist content's explicit markers while preserving its underlying message — a laundering function structurally analogous to [[Waight2026-ts]]'s finding that state-coordinated media is itself memorized and reproduced by commercial LLMs, making models carriers of institutionally shaped political valence even without deliberate campaign design. [[Topinka2026-hb]] offers the clearest theoretical reframing: "slopaganda" does not need to deceive to work: it operates as an affective lure attuned, via RLHF and platform optimization, to the moods of the feed rather than to belief, meaning AI's political danger is as much about engineered affect as about misinformation per se.

## Rethinking facts, truth, and epistemic security

A third cluster steps back to ask what "truth" and "fact" even mean once generation displaces retrieval. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues that established fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, institutional) cannot capture AI outputs, proposing "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category requiring relational, indicator-based assessment rather than binary verification. [[Emilio2026-ik]] scales this concern to the societal level with the concept of "synthetic realities" and the "Generative AI Paradox": as fabrication becomes cheap and ubiquitous, rational actors may discount *all* digital evidence, producing an epistemic tax that benefits strategic deniers as much as it harms genuine victims. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] supplies an empirical instance of this fragility within existing infrastructure, showing that Google's AI Overviews inconsistently moderate conspiratorial search practices — debunking well-established theories like chemtrails but sometimes legitimizing newer ones (15-minute cities) by framing them as open "debates." Read against [[Waight2026-ts]], these papers suggest that the erosion of stable facticity is not only a user-side perceptual problem but is partly built into the training and retrieval pipelines themselves.

## The reconfigured media and knowledge landscape

A further set of papers treats generative AI not primarily as a vector of harm but as a force reorganizing media institutions and knowledge infrastructures outright. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] offers the widest-angle claim: "social media" as an analytic category is dissolving into three post-social formations (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, AI-mediated communication), with generative AI's "platforms without users" severing the human-content link that defined the social web. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical audit of Grokipedia gives this a concrete institutional face, showing an AI-generated encyclopedia that is derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically shifts toward lower-quality and ideologically slanted sourcing on controversial topics — a case study in AI remediating, rather than replacing, existing knowledge infrastructure while degrading its reliability norms. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] complicates assumptions about audience rejection of AI content, finding that disclosed AI authorship does not depress perceived quality and even boosts short-term willingness to engage, though without durable acceptance — evidence that the "crisis of trust" narrative needs empirical qualification. [[Manovich2026-ih]] deliberately steps outside the harms framing altogether, treating generative AI as a new creative medium with constitutive properties (probabilistic variability, "media cognition," stylistic entanglement) that must be understood on its own aesthetic terms, not merely policed as a source of deception — a useful counterweight to the volume of harm-centered work elsewhere in this set.

## Slop, vernacular culture, and audience reception

A final cluster examines how ordinary users and creators are making sense of — and making money from — AI-generated content's quality collapse. [[Li2026-zv]] grounds "AI slop" in folk understanding via Reddit discourse, finding it is less a fixed aesthetic category than a contested, platform-produced relational artifact defined by the gap between algorithmic output and audience expectation. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes this same material through Ngai's concept of the gimmick, reading slop and "brainrot" as simultaneously labor-saving and strained, and provocatively suggesting they might carry a frictional, resistant potential against platform enshittification. [[Tang2026-gu]] shows the production side of this coin empirically: Chinese creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable, benchmarkable output, formalizing and industrializing what used to be unpredictable cultural production. Together these three works insist that "slop" is not simply media pollution to be filtered out, but a new genre and labor regime with its own audience politics, echoing — from the culture side — the same industrialization-of-persuasion logic that [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents from the gambling-fraud side.

## Governance, labor, and institutional response

The topic closes on the question of what should be done. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] insists that AI red-teaming, the central safety practice meant to catch these harms before release, is itself a sociotechnical system riddled with unexamined value judgments and precarious, psychologically costly labor — echoing the content-moderation histories that several papers above show generative AI now reproduces and scales. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] offers the most constructive institutional vision, proposing LLM-enabled collective dialogue, bridging, and community moderation systems as a route toward more deliberative digital public squares, while cautioning that synthetic participation remains democratically fraught. Read against [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]]'s liability-based regulatory framework and [[Schroeder2026-im]]'s layered governance proposal for AI swarms, these papers converge on a shared conclusion that runs through the whole topic: no single technical fix — detection, watermarking, disclosure, or red-teaming — suffices, and durable responses must target the commercial, infrastructural, and labor incentives that make synthetic deception and degraded culture profitable at scale.
