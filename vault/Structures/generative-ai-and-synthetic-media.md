# Generative AI and Synthetic Media Manipulation

## The arc: from platform society to AI society

The broadest frame for this topic is the claim, advanced most directly by [[Tornberg2026-im]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]], that generative AI is not simply adding a new content type to existing platforms but precipitating a structural transition—from a "platform society" organized around social graphs, behavioral capture, and predictive recommendation to an "AI society" organized around generative infrastructures, cognitive lock-in, and alignment politics. This theorization sets the stakes for everything else in the collection: if platforms once shaped visibility, the new contest is over what models can say, simulate, and withhold. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] offers the normative counterpart to this diagnosis, imagining how LLM-enabled collective dialogue, bridging systems, and community moderation might be designed to push digital public squares *toward* democratic ideals rather than away from them—an explicitly aspirational rejoinder to the more diagnostic and often alarmed tenor of the rest of the corpus.

## Persuasion: mechanisms, limits, and the accuracy trade-off

A tight empirical cluster interrogates *how* conversational AI persuades, converging on a single mechanism: information density. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] isolates post-training and prompting as stronger levers than scale or personalization, and shows that the same density of fact-checkable claims that drives persuasion also erodes factual accuracy—a genuine persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, pitting AI against elite human persuaders (championship debaters, professional canvassers) and finding AI reliably wins, with coaching narrowing but not closing the gap, and with throughput-matching alone eliminating AI's advantage—strong confirmation that fact density, not rapport or "humanness," is doing the work. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] extend this logic to durable, real-world belief change: tailored AI dialogue reduces conspiracy belief for months and shifts policy attitudes on climate, vaccines, and inequality, especially among skeptics—suggesting the same mechanisms that threaten epistemic security could be redirected toward inoculation and correction. [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalizes this prosocial use case directly, showing a chatbot-delivered "conversational inoculation" outperforms static reading/writing interventions when adaptive dialogue design (trust, independent thinking, minimal friction) is attended to. Together these papers establish that LLM persuasion is a well-characterized, dual-use capacity—its power source identical whether aimed at manufacturing consent or defending against it.

## Visual propaganda: aesthetics, industrial production, and affect

A second cluster turns from text-based persuasion to the visual register, tracking how generative image/video tools are being absorbed into propaganda and promotional ecosystems. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents this most concretely: coordinated Facebook gambling networks show an exponential, ChatGPT-synchronized rise in AI-generated persuasive imagery, with generative tools intensifying rather than replacing existing drivers (aspirational wealth, FOMO, cultural localization) and exploiting the regulatory gap between paid and organic content. [[Crichton2026-kn]] finds an analogous "re-generative pipeline" in far-right propaganda, where commercial image models sanitize extremist imagery's most explicit markers while preserving its underlying message—moderation as laundering rather than filtering. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] extend this to electoral and comparative-authoritarian contexts, the latter cautioning against techno-deterministic readings: GenAI propaganda's form is contingent on national political-economic structure, not a uniform global trajectory. [[Tang2026-gu]] reframes virality itself as an engineered, industrialized object of AI-assisted creative labor in Chinese short-video production, while [[Topinka2026-hb]] and [[Galip2026-ix]] supply the theoretical vocabulary for why this content works even when manifestly fake: "slopaganda" operates as an affective lure rather than a deception, attuned via RLHF and platform training data to ambient moods rather than to belief, and "AI slop" more broadly is read through Ngai's gimmick theory as a form that works simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions in cultural production under platform capitalism. [[Manovich2026-ih]] stands somewhat apart, treating generative AI not as propaganda infrastructure but as a creative medium in its own right—its probabilistic, conventional, "make it old" logic nonetheless helps explain the aesthetic homogeneity (hyper-real, nostalgic, nondisruptive) that recurs across the slop and propaganda literatures.

## Can audiences tell, and does it matter?

A parallel strand asks whether synthetic media actually succeeds at deceiving audiences, and under what conditions. [[Hove2026-sl49]] delivers a stark baseline finding: Danish respondents discriminate AI-generated from authentic images barely above chance, and worse than a flat 50% guess, with confidence decoupled from accuracy. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates the discernment picture temporally, showing that difficulty authenticating one piece of (deep)fake content carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to weaken confidence and raise susceptibility on subsequent exposures. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] pushes back against generative-AI exceptionalism, finding that AI-generated stills are not more credible than text, and that even sophisticated video deepfakes only outperform text when ample authentic footage exists to plausibly recontextualize—suggesting old-fashioned decontextualization can rival cutting-edge synthesis. [[Wack2026-bt]] relocates the question from individual perception to collective sensemaking: in Kenyan survey experiments, a deepfake's political impact is substantially renegotiated by surrounding audience commentary, with credulous comments deepening harm and skeptical ones partially restoring it—impact is socially distributed, not fixed at the point of generation.

## Infrastructural and institutional bias in language models

A further cluster treats LLMs themselves as a site of institutional influence rather than neutral tools. [[Waight2026-ts]] offers the most systematic evidence, showing state-coordinated media is memorized in training data and causes measurably more pro-regime outputs in politically salient languages—an indirect "laundering" pathway distinct from direct regulation. [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents an adjacent phenomenon in Grokipedia, finding it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically citing lower-quality and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial topics, suggesting ideological slant introduced through selective rewriting rather than wholesale fabrication. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] examines Google's AI Overviews as a related infrastructural chokepoint, finding moderation against conspiratorial query framing works unevenly—robust for established theories like chemtrails, porous for newer ones like 15-minute cities, where AI summaries can actively legitimize fringe claims. These three papers jointly argue that bias and manipulation in the "AI society" often enter not through overtly manipulative content but through the quiet politics of training data, sourcing, and query-response design.

## Coordinated and agentic threats

[[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Orlando2025-ul]] theorize and simulate a further escalation: the fusion of LLMs with multi-agent architectures. Schroeder et al. map the governance stakes of "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus and poisoning training data at scale, while Orlando et al. provide empirical substantiation via generative agent-based modeling, showing that even minimal "teammate awareness" among simulated influence-operation agents produces coordination levels nearly matching explicit collective deliberation—an unsettling demonstration that sophisticated coordination may require little more than shared awareness, not centralized command.

## Scams, trust, and the news/companion interface

[[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends the persuasion-and-synthesis story into financial harm, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of deepfake fraud across platforms, telecoms, and banks, and arguing—consonant with the governance arguments above—that liability should shift from individual victims to gatekeeping intermediaries. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] turn to everyday trust dynamics: Gilardi finds audiences cannot distinguish AI-assisted from human news on quality grounds and even show increased engagement upon disclosure, while Slack et al. flag a related concern in companion chatbots, whose accommodating conversational policies in emotionally vulnerable moments elude conventional safety audits.

## Theorizing truth and facticity under generative AI

Closing the loop conceptually, [[Dierickx2026-tw]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] attempt to update epistemology itself for this landscape. Dierickx argues existing fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot accommodate GenAI outputs and proposes "emergent facts" as a new, relationally-evaluated category. Ferrara's concept of "synthetic realities" generalizes this further, arguing the paradigmatic risk is not the isolated deepfake but coherent, interactive, personalized fabricated environments that produce a rational discounting of all digital evidence—the "Generative AI Paradox" that closes the circle back to [[Tornberg2026-im]]'s enclosure of cognition: across persuasion, propaganda, infrastructure, and epistemology, this literature converges on the claim that generative AI's deepest disruption is not any single synthetic artifact but the erosion of the shared evidentiary and institutional ground on which judgment itself depends.
