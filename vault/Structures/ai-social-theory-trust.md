# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The Line of Argument

This cluster of papers approaches AI's social position from three converging directions: theoretical work asking what kind of "agent" AI actually is and how social theory should model its relations, empirical survey work measuring public trust and attitudes, and applied studies of specific trust-mediated encounters (chatbots, disclosed AI journalism, red-teaming labor). Together they trace an arc from conceptual foundations to lived public experience, repeatedly circling back to a shared insight: agency and trust in AI are not properties of the technology itself but outcomes of social attribution, institutional arrangement, and communicative practice.

### Demarcating Agency: Social Status, Not System Property

The theoretical core is set by [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes the "agency" question as a demarcation problem in social theory: agency is a socially granted and monitored license to act, not an intrinsic capacity of a system. This relational, anti-essentialist move underwrites much of what follows. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] operationalizes a version of this insight empirically, using Luhmann's systems theory and conversation analysis to show that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding — performance without understanding, agency attributed rather than possessed. [[Ng2026-og]] extends the demarcation project from the individual to the population level, arguing that agentic AI operating in social environments cannot be modeled as isolated task-completors; its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework formalizes strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability as structural priors that only make sense once agency is understood as socially and relationally constituted — directly continuing Anicker's claim that agency emerges from practices and networks rather than internal properties.

### Trust as a Multilevel, Configurable Phenomenon

A second cluster theorizes *trust* itself as the organizing construct for AI's social role. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes the most general architecture, arguing that trust in generative AI should be studied as a multilevel phenomenon inheriting from — but distinct from — trust research on mass and social media. This framework is empirically cashed out by several papers examining how trust bifurcates by *configuration* of AI use. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting trust is not a monolithic attitude toward "AI" but varies by institutional role. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] complicates the disclosure story further: undisclosed AI-written and human-written news are judged equivalently in quality, but disclosure boosts short-term willingness to continue reading without changing long-term acceptance — a decoupling of message-level and system-level trust. [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines this with task-specificity, showing that disclosure penalties depend on *which* journalistic task AI performs (fact-checking hurts trust most) and on reader profiles, undermining generic "AI vs. human" framings. [[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a counterintuitive twist: users with higher conspiracy beliefs trust "balanced" chatbots *more*, not less, suggesting perceived neutrality and usefulness — not source skepticism — drive chatbot acceptance, a finding that resonates with [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]'s demonstration that perceived political bias (not actual argument content) is what degrades LLM persuasive power. Across these studies, trust functions as a socially negotiated, context-dependent variable rather than a fixed property of the technology — precisely the pattern the demarcation-of-agency papers predict.

### Public Perception at Scale: Survey Evidence

A third strand supplies the descriptive infrastructure of public attitudes that the theoretical and experimental work seeks to explain. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offer large national surveys (US and Italy respectively) documenting rising AI adoption alongside persistent skepticism, demographic fractures (age, gender, education, party), and what Fattorini terms "critical ambivalence." [[UnknownUnknown-db]] complements this with occupation-level data linking self-reported displacement anxiety to actual measured AI exposure, showing that public sentiment is not free-floating but tracks material economic experience. These survey papers function as empirical ballast: they show that the ambivalence, context-dependence, and configuration-sensitivity theorized elsewhere are not artifacts of experimental design but reflect durable structures of public opinion — mediated, as [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows, by news framing itself. Nguyen's content analysis of mentalistic-agentic framing in global news media closes a loop back to the demarcation problem: journalism is itself a site where AI's "agency" is socially constructed and contested, with anthropomorphic language serving critique as often as hype.

### Institutional Undersides of Trust and Agency

Finally, [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Matias2025-px]] pull the discussion toward the institutional labor and epistemic practices that produce trustworthy (or untrustworthy) AI in the first place. Gillespie's sociotechnical account of red-teaming reveals that the "safety" underwriting public trust is manufactured by precarious, psychologically taxing labor whose value judgments are opaque — an institutional analogue to the discovery that chatbot "understanding" is parasitic on invisible human repair work. Matias argues, conversely, that public participation can *improve* the science of AI evaluation itself, reframing lay involvement not as a concession to public trust but as an epistemic asset. Together these two papers insist that the social position of AI cannot be theorized purely at the level of perception or interaction; it is co-produced by the hidden labor and participatory structures that generate the systems being trusted or distrusted in the first place — bringing the arc back full circle to Anicker's founding claim that agency, and by extension trust, is a status conferred through social practice, not a fact read off the machine.
