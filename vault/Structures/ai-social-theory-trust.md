# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

### From "does it understand?" to "who says it acts?"

The corpus opens with a foundational conceptual move: refusing to treat machine agency as a technical property to be discovered and instead treating it as a social accomplishment. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes the demarcation problem explicitly—agency is a socially granted, monitored license to act, not an intrinsic capacity, which means the question "is the AI an agent?" dissolves into "under what social practices does a community attribute agency to it?" [[Ptz2024-sl20]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a very different route: drawing on Luhmann and conversation analysis, it shows empirically that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding. Both papers converge on separating *performance* from *ontological status*—agency and understanding are things attributed and interactionally sustained by human participants, not properties intrinsic to the system. This sets the conceptual baseline for everything downstream: if agency is socially conferred, then media framing, trust judgments, and public attitudes are not merely reactions to AI but constitutive of what AI *is* allowed to be.

### Formalizing the social environment of agentic AI

[[Ng2026-og]] extends this relational premise into a formal register, arguing that once AI systems are deployed as populations of interacting agents (not isolated task-solvers), social theory itself becomes a *structural prior* for their design and governance. The Multi-Agent Social Systems framework operationalizes exactly the kind of environment in which the demarcation and licensing dynamics described by [[Anicker2024-vp]] would actually unfold at scale—strategic heterogeneity, network effects, co-evolution, and distributional instability are the formal signatures of a population whose "agency" is constituted relationally rather than individually. Read together, [[Anicker2024-vp]] supplies the theoretical vocabulary for why agency is social, and [[Ng2026-og]] supplies a mathematical apparatus for modeling social AI once agency is granted at scale, with empirical grounding in a real AI-agent social network. [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s study of public red-teaming complements this by showing how the *practices* that monitor and contest AI's capacities (per Anicker's "licensing") are institutionally organized—red-teaming is precisely a social mechanism for testing and adjudicating what an AI system may be trusted to do, situated between industry control and public accountability.

### Public epistemologies: media, imaginaries, and industry narrative work

A second cluster examines how agency-attributions circulate in public discourse before they ever reach individual users. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] provides the empirical backbone here, showing that mentalistic-agentic framing in global news is real but far from dominant, is shaped more by newsroom practice than regional culture, and serves purposes ranging from hype reproduction to critique—directly instantiating the "social attribution" mechanism theorized by [[Anicker2024-vp]] at the level of mass media. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] zooms into a single controversy (the Altman ouster) to show how industry actors actively manufacture AI imaginaries—Longtermism, Techno-Hagiography, Regulatory Ambivalence—using premediation and preclusion to control which concerns become salient. Together these two papers show agency-attribution and trust-formation are not neutral outcomes of technology but contested discursive terrain, with journalists and industry elites each doing "agency work" for different stakes. [[Matias2025-px]] pushes this further into method: it argues that the sociotechnical nature of AI (the same premise underlying the demarcation and imaginaries papers) means public participation is not just legitimating but epistemically necessary for rigorous AI evaluation, citing cases (Allegheny tool, Chicago police complaints) where lived-experience knowledge caught what purely technical audits missed.

### Trust as a communicative and relational variable

The largest cluster operationalizes trust empirically, largely within journalism and political communication contexts, and can be read as testing—often disconfirming—simple expectations from algorithmic-aversion or distrust literatures. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers the conceptual scaffold, proposing that generative-AI trust be studied as a multilevel phenomenon inheriting from mass-media and social-media trust research rather than treated sui generis. Several empirical papers populate this scaffold. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds no baseline quality penalty for AI-generated or AI-assisted news when authorship is undisclosed, and even a short-term *increase* in engagement upon disclosure—challenging assumptions that disclosure straightforwardly damages trust. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates this by showing that task-specific disclosure *does* reduce trust, with effects concentrated on tasks central to accuracy (fact-checking) rather than on visibility in the value chain, and identifies heterogeneous audience "preference profiles" that resist a single trust story. [[Dubey2026-bl]] delivers a genuinely counterintuitive finding in an adjacent domain: users higher in conspiracy belief trust and accept "balanced" news chatbots *more*, not less, complicating standard assumptions that distrust of institutions generalizes to distrust of AI intermediaries. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows the flip side—perceived political bias (not disclosure of AI-ness per se) is what undermines persuasive trust, reducing belief correction by roughly a quarter and shifting user engagement toward argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends the persuasion question across three contested domains, finding AI dialogue is most persuasive precisely among skeptics and moderated strongly by trust in science rather than trust in AI itself—suggesting that generalized "trust in AI" may be less causally central to belief change than domain-specific epistemic trust. Read as a set, these five papers jointly argue against a monolithic "trust in AI" construct: trust is task-specific, source-specific, moderated by prior ideological commitments, and sometimes decoupled from behavioral engagement (as [[Gilardi2026-hw]] shows explicitly)—precisely the multilevel disaggregation that [[Suk2026-ai]] calls for theoretically.

### Public attitudes at scale: adoption outpacing understanding

The final cluster grounds these theoretical and experimental findings in broad survey data on lived attitudes and adoption. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] both document a striking pattern across the US and Italy: rising day-to-day adoption of AI tools coexists with flat or worsening skepticism about societal impact, regulation, and information security—an "adoption/attitude" gap that echoes the disclosure/engagement decoupling found by [[Gilardi2026-hw]] at the individual level. Both surveys also find durable demographic cleavages (age, gender, education) shaping both use and evaluation, and both note that direct experience with AI tools (e.g., chatbot use) tends to soften threat perceptions—a finding that resonates with [[Dubey2026-bl]]'s and [[Kotz2026-lk]]'s observations that concrete interaction, not abstract exposure, drives trust judgments. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s occupational survey data adds an economic dimension to this picture, showing that self-reported displacement anxiety tracks observed AI exposure by occupation and is concentrated among early-career workers—suggesting that trust and threat perception are stratified not just by ideology or media diet but by structural position in the labor market.

### The arc: from ontology to institution

Across the set, the argument moves from a foundational reframing of agency as socially attributed ([[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Ptz2024-sl20]]) through formal and institutional accounts of how that attribution is structured and contested at scale ([[Ng2026-og]], [[Unknown2025-qj]], [[Matias2025-px]]), into empirical accounts of how attribution and trust circulate publicly through media and industry narrative ([[Nguyen2026-vm]], [[Stanusch2026-ec]]), and finally into fine-grained experimental and survey evidence on how trust, disclosure, and persuasion actually operate for ordinary users across journalism, politics, and everyday adoption ([[Suk2026-ai]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Kotz2026-lk]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[UnknownUnknown-db]]). The throughline is that none of agency, trust, or public perception can be treated as a fixed property of AI systems themselves: all three are relational, contingent, contested, and disaggregated by task, context, and social position—demanding theoretical frameworks (social-theoretic priors, multilevel trust models) that can hold this heterogeneity rather than collapsing it into a single verdict on "AI acceptance."
