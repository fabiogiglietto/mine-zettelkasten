# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

The twenty papers in this collection trace a layered argument: before we can ask whether people *trust* AI, we need a theory of what kind of social thing AI *is*. The collection moves from foundational conceptual work on machine agency, through formal and sociological frameworks for multi-agent AI, into empirical studies of trust and persuasion, and finally into large-scale survey data on public attitudes. Read together, they suggest that AI's social position is neither purely technical nor purely psychological, but constituted relationally — through communication, social attribution, and institutional context.

### Demarcating Agency: Is AI a Social Actor?

The conceptual anchor for the whole topic is [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes agency not as an intrinsic property of a system but as a socially granted and monitored "license to act" — a demarcation problem to be solved through social theory rather than engineering benchmarks. This relational view finds a strikingly compatible echo in the Luhmannian/conversation-analytic tradition represented by [[Ptz2024-sl20]], [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]], and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]]. All three argue, in different registers, that large language models should not be understood as cognitive agents that "understand," but as communication partners whose apparent competence is parasitic on human interpretive labor and repair work. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] demonstrates empirically how ChatGPT fails to initiate repair of conversational trouble and merely echoes user wording; [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] generalizes this into a critique of "Artificial Intelligence" as a framing, arguing outputs become meaningful only through human sense-making; and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] extends the communication-partner framing historically, situating LLMs alongside archives and catalogs as information-management infrastructures. Together these three position papers constitute a coherent sociological counter-narrative to cognitivist AI hype, directly operationalizing Anicker's claim that agency is attributed, not intrinsic.

[[Ng2026-og]] pushes this relational turn into formal and empirical territory, arguing that agentic AI research has inherited an individualistic, single-task paradigm that social theory can correct. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework — formalized as information exchange, influence dynamics, and network structure — operationalizes exactly the kind of "socially monitored" interaction Anicker gestures toward, and validates four structural priors (heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) against real AI-agent-network data. Where Anicker and the Esposito-indebted papers ask what agency *is*, Ng et al. ask how multi-agent AI populations behave once agency is distributed across a network — a bridge from demarcation to governance.

### Public Epistemology and Media Framing

A second cluster examines how agency and intelligence are *represented* to publics, rather than philosophically defined. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows empirically that news media's mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is real but far from dominant or monolithic — journalists deploy anthropomorphic language for critique as often as credulity, complicating any simple "media hype" narrative. This sits usefully alongside [[Goldberg2026-eb]], which treats LLMs as a potential infrastructural shift for democratic deliberation itself, proposing collective dialogue, bridging, and moderation systems as applications of AI's social agency at the level of the public sphere rather than the individual utterance. Both papers assume, implicitly, the Anicker-style premise that AI's social standing is constructed through discourse and institutional practice — whether journalistic framing or platform design — rather than given by its internal architecture.

### Trust as a Relational, Multilevel Construct

The conceptual move from agency to trust is made explicit by [[Suk2026-ai]], which proposes a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI modeled on the trajectory of mass-media and social-media trust research — effectively asking the trust-equivalent of Anicker's demarcation question: at what levels (interpersonal, institutional, infrastructural) is AI trusted, and how does that differ from trust in prior communication technologies? This framework provides a scaffold for the cluster of empirical trust studies that follow. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]] both interrogate disclosure effects in journalism: Gilardi et al. find that undisclosed AI-written news is rated no differently than human-written news, with disclosure producing only a short-term curiosity boost rather than durable trust; Mattis et al. complicate this by showing that *task-specific* disclosure (e.g., fact-checking vs. idea generation) matters more than blanket AI/human labels, uncovering five distinct audience trust profiles. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends this logic across configurations, finding Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news — suggesting trust is not a property of "AI" per se but of the institutional position AI occupies within the information ecosystem, directly vindicating Suk's multilevel premise.

Trust also proves selectively exploitable or resistant to manipulation. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that perceived political bias — rather than actual argument content — sharply attenuates LLM persuasiveness, revealing trust as contingent on perceived neutrality of the "messenger." [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates intuitions about who trusts AI most: counter to expectation, higher-conspiracy-belief users trusted a balanced-news chatbot *more* than skeptics of conspiracy, driven by perceived usefulness within a Technology Acceptance Model. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends the persuasion question further, showing conversational AI dialogue shifts beliefs and policy support across ideologically contested domains, with effects strongest precisely among skeptics — contingent heavily on trust in science as a moderator. Read against [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], these two persuasion studies suggest trust in AI is simultaneously fragile (collapsing under perceived bias) and potent (capable of moving even skeptics) depending on how neutrality and institutional backing are signaled.

Two further papers probe the limits of trust calibration. [[Hove2026-sl49]] shows people are barely better than chance — and worse than naive 50% guessing — at detecting AI-generated images, with confidence decoupled from accuracy, implying that individual trust judgments are an unreliable safeguard requiring structural intervention. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] turns to AI companions in emotionally vulnerable interactions, arguing that existing safety audits miss the implicit "accommodation" policies governing sustained trust-laden relationships, a concern that dovetails with [[Matias2025-px]]'s broader argument that AI evaluation itself must be treated as a sociotechnical, participatory process rather than a purely technical one — trust in AI, on this view, depends on the trustworthiness of the science and public involvement behind it.

### Public Attitudes at Scale

The final cluster grounds these conceptual and experimental findings in large-scale survey evidence. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] document national publics' ambivalence toward AI — American and Italian respondents alike show rising adoption alongside persistent skepticism about pace, security, and governance, with Fattorini's "critical ambivalence" framing nicely capturing the mixed trust/use decoupling seen throughout the collection. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] adds an economic dimension, showing self-reported displacement concerns track observed occupational AI exposure, while productivity gains cluster at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution — evidence that public perception of AI's social role is also shaped by material, labor-market experience, not just media or interface framing.

### Synthesis

Across this arc, a shared theoretical commitment emerges: AI's social role is not resolved at the level of internal capability but constituted through attribution, communication, and institutional embedding. [[Anicker2024-vp]] and the Esposito-lineage papers supply the conceptual vocabulary; [[Ng2026-og]] formalizes it for multi-agent systems; [[Suk2026-ai]] extends it into trust research; and the empirical studies — from news disclosure experiments to national surveys — repeatedly confirm that trust, perceived agency, and acceptance of AI are context-dependent, relationally produced, and often decoupled from the system's actual technical performance.
