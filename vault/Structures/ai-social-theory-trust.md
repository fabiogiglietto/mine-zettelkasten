# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Framing AI as a Social Object: Theory and Formal Frameworks

At the conceptual core of this collection sits the question of what kind of thing AI *is*, socially speaking. [[Anicker2024-vp]] tackles this head-on by reframing agency not as an intrinsic property of a system but as a socially conferred status — a "license to act" granted and monitored through social practices. This move is significant because it displaces the demarcation problem from engineering (does the system compute, reason, understand?) to sociology (does society treat it as an agent, and under what conditions?). [[Ng2026-og]] extends this social-theoretic orientation from single-agent demarcation to populations of interacting agents, arguing that agentic AI systems deployed at scale require social theory as a *structural prior* rather than an afterthought. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework — formalized as an information-exchange, influence-dynamics, and network triple — operationalizes structural priors like strategic heterogeneity and co-evolution, and validates them empirically against a large AI social network. Read together, [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ng2026-og]] bookend a shift from asking "is this system an agent?" to asking "how do populations of variably-agentic systems behave once embedded in social structure?" — a shift that echoes the paper's own claim that alignment research addressing dyadic user-AI problems is conceptually distinct from population-level governance.

[[Ptz2024-sl20]] offers a complementary, more skeptical theoretical intervention, drawing on Luhmann's systems theory and conversation analysis to argue that chatbot "competence" is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding. This paper is a useful counterweight to the agency-as-status framework: it suggests that even where AI is *treated* as a conversational agent, the practical accomplishment of coherent dialogue is often smuggled in by users doing interpretive labor the system cannot do itself. Together with [[Anicker2024-vp]], it suggests agency-attribution can be socially real (people talk to and trust chatbots as if they understand) while remaining functionally hollow — a tension that the media-framing and trust literatures below take up empirically.

## Public Discourse and Agentic Framing

Nguyen2026-og — [[Nguyen2026-vm]] — bridges the conceptual and empirical registers by asking how *news media* construct AI's social position. Its large-scale content analysis finds that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is present but far from dominant, and that anthropomorphic language does rhetorical work ranging from uncritical hype amplification to explicit critique. This complicates any simple story in which media manufacture machine agency wholesale; instead, journalistic practice mediates and often contests the "sociotechnical imaginary" of agentic AI, resonating with Anicker's claim that agency is socially attributed rather than given, and showing empirically how contested and organizationally variable that attribution process is.

## Trust as a Multilevel, Configurable Construct

A cluster of papers develops and tests trust frameworks specific to generative AI. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes the most general architecture, arguing that trust in generative AI should be studied at multiple levels by importing lessons from decades of mass-media and social-media trust research — essentially providing the theoretical scaffolding that the more empirical papers in this set implicitly need. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] demonstrates why such multilevel thinking is necessary: trust is not monolithic but *configuration-dependent* — Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, meaning the same underlying technology earns different trust depending on its institutional embedding. This configurational sensitivity is sharpened by [[Mattis2026-gu]], whose conjoint experiment shows that trust penalties for AI disclosure in journalism depend on the *specific task* being disclosed (fact-checking hurts trust far more than idea generation) and on reader-level moderators, yielding five distinct audience "preference profiles" rather than a single trust response. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] adds a temporal wrinkle: undisclosed AI-generated news is rated as equal in quality to human-written news, and disclosure even *increases* short-term willingness to continue reading a given article — though this curiosity effect does not translate into durable acceptance of AI journalism. Together, these three papers push back against a simple "disclosure erodes trust" narrative, showing instead that trust effects are task-specific, context-specific, and time-bound.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] extends the trust-and-utility question to politically fraught content, finding — against the authors' own expectations — that people with stronger conspiracy beliefs report *more* trust in a balanced-perspective chatbot, suggesting that within a Technology Acceptance Model, perceived usefulness and trust dominate over ideological priors in ways not predicted by the motivated-reasoning literature. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complements this by showing the flip side: explicit signals of perceived *political bias* substantially undermine an LLM's persuasive power, and this operates through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement — evidence that source-credibility mechanisms familiar from mass-communication research transfer directly onto conversational AI. [[Kotz2026-lk]] pushes further into persuasion dynamics, showing that adaptive AI dialogue can shift beliefs and policy support across ideologically disparate issues, with the largest effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated most strongly by generalized trust in science — suggesting that AI's persuasive capacity is contingent not just on perceived neutrality (per DiGiuseppe) but on the broader institutional trust ecology surrounding a topic.

## Public Attitudes, Adoption, and the Limits of Trust Frameworks

A final set of empirical survey papers grounds these theoretical and experimental findings in population-level attitude data. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] both document a pattern of "critical ambivalence" — rising AI adoption alongside persistent skepticism, with support highly domain-dependent (higher for security, lower for journalism, creative work, or employment decisions) and structured by demographic cleavages in age, gender, and education. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] additionally reveals the political contingency of trust in AI *governance* specifically, with Democratic and Republican confidence in regulatory capacity moving in opposite directions — a macro-level echo of the politically contingent persuasion effects documented by [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] at the individual level. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] shifts the register from attitudes to lived economic experience, showing that self-reported displacement anxiety tracks objectively measured occupational AI exposure, while productivity gains are concentrated at the high and low ends of the wage distribution — a reminder that public perception research on AI's social role cannot be divorced from its material economic effects.

[[Matias2025-px]] closes the loop by arguing that the solution to many of these trust and legitimacy problems is not better persuasion or framing but structural: incorporating lived-experience publics directly into the *evaluation science* of AI systems. This reframes "public trust" not as an attitudinal outcome to be measured or engineered but as something that should be earned through participatory epistemic practice — a normative counterpoint to the largely descriptive trust and attitude studies elsewhere in this collection, and a fitting return to the collection's opening claim that AI's social position is not a fixed technical fact but something continuously negotiated, attributed, and contested.
