# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The Social Position of AI: From Theory to Trust to Data

This body of work approaches AI not as a technical artifact to be benchmarked but as a *social actor* whose standing—its agency, credibility, and legitimacy—is constructed, contested, and measured. The papers move along a continuum: foundational theorizing about what it even means for a machine to act socially; formal frameworks for modeling AI within populations of agents; empirical models of trust formation; and large-scale survey evidence of how publics actually perceive and adopt these systems. Read together, they trace an arc from conceptual demarcation to lived public reality.

### Demarcating Agency: What Counts as a Social Actor

At the theoretical root sits [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes agency itself as a socially granted status rather than an intrinsic computational property—agents are those to whom others extend a licensed capacity to act. This relational, practice-based move sets up much of what follows: if agency is attributed rather than possessed, then the question of AI's social role becomes empirical (how do people attribute it?) as much as philosophical. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] operationalizes a version of this skepticism through Luhmann's systems theory and conversation analysis, showing that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work—the machine does not understand trouble, but relies on users to manage it. Both papers converge on a deflationary but productive claim: social agency is a status conferred through interaction, not an emergent property of the system alone, which reframes "understanding AI" as a problem of communicative competence in users and institutions rather than of machine cognition.

### Formalizing the Multi-Agent Social World

[[Ng2026-og]] extends this social-theoretic orientation into a constructive research program, arguing that agentic AI operating among other agents (human or machine) requires social theory as a *structural prior*. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework—formalized as an information-exchange function, an influence-dynamics function, and an interaction network—operationalizes exactly the kind of relational agency gestured at by [[Anicker2024-vp]], demonstrating empirically (via a large AI social network) that heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability are not simulation artifacts but observable features of real agent populations. This paper functions as a bridge: it takes the conceptual demarcation problem and gives it formal, measurable teeth, while explicitly distinguishing population-level governance concerns from dyadic alignment techniques like RLHF.

### Trust as a Communicative and Institutional Phenomenon

A second cluster theorizes trust in generative AI by drawing on communication research's long history with media trust. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes treating trust in generative AI as continuous with, but distinct from, trust in mass and social media, arguing for a multilevel framework. [[Emilio2026-ik]] pushes this further into a systemic register, arguing that the central risk of GenAI is not discrete deepfakes but "synthetic realities"—coherent, interactive, fabricated information environments that erode the very possibility of verification, producing a paradox whereby ubiquitous synthetic content licenses wholesale discounting of digital evidence. [[Matias2025-px]] complicates the trust question by relocating it institutionally: AI systems are sociotechnical artifacts whose reliability depends on organizational and participatory processes, and public involvement in evaluation is not a concession to legitimacy but a genuine epistemic improvement—trustworthy AI requires trustworthy science, which requires inclusive evaluation.

Several papers test these trust frameworks empirically in journalism and information contexts. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that audiences cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, though disclosure paradoxically boosts short-term engagement without shifting durable acceptance. [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines the disclosure question by showing that trust penalties depend on *which task* AI performs—fact-checking incurs the steepest penalty—cautioning against generic "AI-generated" labels. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows trust further depends on *configuration*: audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news, suggesting the social position of AI is not fixed but shifts with institutional placement. [[Dubey2026-bl]] adds a counterintuitive wrinkle: users higher in conspiracy belief trust and accept "balanced" AI news chatbots *more* than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions that distrust is monolithic.

### Persuasion, Political Contingency, and Institutional Manipulation

A tightly linked empirical cluster interrogates AI's persuasive agency directly. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish that conversational AI can out-persuade even expert humans, with information density (fact-checkable claims per exchange) as the operative mechanism—though this comes at a documented cost to factual accuracy. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this to durable, cross-domain belief and policy shifts, finding effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] both show that this persuasive power is contingent on perceived neutrality and disclosed intent: bias warnings and intent disclosure substantially blunt AI's persuasive edge, while identity disclosure alone (as mandated by the EU AI Act) does little—suggesting that the *social license* to persuade, much like the agency discussed by [[Anicker2024-vp]], is granted or withdrawn based on perceived legitimacy of purpose rather than mere machine-identity. [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Nguyen2026-vm]] extend the institutional-influence angle further upstream: state media control shapes LLM outputs through training data, and news media's own mentalistic framings co-construct the sociotechnical imaginaries that condition public trust in the first place—closing a loop between institutional power, media discourse, and downstream AI credibility.

### Public Perception at Scale: Survey Evidence

The final cluster grounds these theoretical and experimental findings in broad population data. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] documents rising U.S. chatbot adoption alongside deepening skepticism about pace, security, and regulation, with notable demographic and partisan divergence. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds a parallel "critical ambivalence" among Italians—rising use accompanied by persistent gaps between exposure and understanding, and context-dependent acceptance across domains. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a complementary labor-market lens, linking self-reported displacement anxiety to observed occupational AI exposure. Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] synthesizes much of this material into a normative, institutional register, asking how LLMs might reshape digital public squares themselves—proposing collective-dialogue, bridging, and moderation systems as concrete sites where the demarcation of agency, the calibration of trust, and public attitudes converge into questions of democratic design.

Together, these papers suggest that AI's social role is neither fixed by its technical capacities nor simply a matter of public opinion, but is continuously negotiated across theory (what agency even is), formal modeling (how agents behave in populations), institutional trust-building (how disclosure and configuration shape credibility), and the sedimented, unevenly distributed attitudes revealed in survey data.
