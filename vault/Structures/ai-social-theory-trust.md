# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Framing the Problem: Where Does AI's Social Role Even Begin?

At the root of this cluster sits a demarcation question: can AI systems be called "agents" in any meaningful social sense, and if so, on what grounds? [[Anicker2024-vp]] answers by relocating agency outside the machine altogether, arguing that agency is not an intrinsic computational property but a socially granted and monitored status—a license to act conferred through social practices. This move is consequential for everything that follows in the topic: it reframes questions about trust, perception, and persuasion not as questions about what AI *is* but about what societies *attribute* to it and *permit* it to do. [[Ng2026-og]] extends this relational premise into formal territory, proposing that agentic AI deployed in social environments should be modeled with social theory as a structural prior rather than as isolated task-completing agents. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework—formalized as an information-exchange function, an influence-dynamics function, and an interaction network—operationalizes four priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) and validates them empirically on a large AI-agent social network, effectively asking what Anicker's social-theoretic demarcation looks like once agents are interacting at scale with each other, not just with humans.

## The Understanding/Performance Divide

A second throughline, strongly associated with Elena Esposito's work, insists that the apparent sociality of LLMs must be decoupled from any claim about machine cognition. [[Ptz2024-sl20]], drawing on Luhmann and conversation analysis, shows empirically that ChatGPT manages conversational "repair" without grasping what the trouble is about—its competence is parasitic on human interpretive labor, not evidence of understanding. [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] generalize this into a conceptual program: algorithms like ChatGPT should be understood not as artificial intelligences approximating human cognition but as communication partners or "answer engines" whose outputs are made meaningful by human users, situating LLMs within a longer media-historical lineage of archives, catalogs, and libraries. Together these three papers form a coherent systems-theoretic/communication-theoretic countercurrent to agency-talk: where [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ng2026-og]] ask how agency gets attributed and structured, this cluster asks what is actually happening underneath that attribution—and answers that it is communication, not cognition, all the way down.

## Trust as a Multilevel, Contingent, and Configurable Phenomenon

A substantial swath of the topic turns from ontology to the social-psychological and institutional mechanics of trust. [[Suk2026-ai]] supplies the scaffolding, proposing a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI that explicitly borrows from the long trajectory of mass-media and social-media trust research—implicitly positioning every empirical paper below it as a test case for such a framework. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows that trust is not monolithic even within a single national public: Italians trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting that the *configuration* in which AI appears within the information ecosystem matters as much as the fact of AI involvement itself. [[Mattis2026-gu]] pushes this configurational logic further, demonstrating via conjoint experiment that task-specific disclosure of AI involvement in journalism produces variable trust penalties depending on where in the news value chain the task sits (fact-checking is penalized most) and depending on reader profile—identifying five distinct audience segments rather than a single "AI-averse public." [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates assumptions about who distrusts AI-mediated information: contrary to expectations, people with higher conspiracy beliefs trust "balanced news chatbots" *more*, not less, with perceived usefulness and trust (via a TAM lens) driving acceptance regardless of prior ideological disposition. Collectively these four papers suggest that generative AI trust is contextual, segmented, and only loosely predicted by the demographic or ideological variables that dominate folk intuition.

## Persuasion, Disclosure, and the Limits of Transparency

Closely linked to trust is a pair of rigorous experimental papers on AI persuasion and transparency mandates. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] finds that a chatbot's persuasive power to correct factual misconceptions is substantially blunted merely by *perceived* political bias—even when the content itself remains accurate and neutral—implicating source-credibility dynamics familiar from political communication (Zaller, Lodge & Taber) in the generative-AI context. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] isolates a related but distinct lever: disclosing that an interlocutor is AI (as EU AI Act Article 50 requires) does almost nothing to reduce persuasion, but disclosing the chatbot's *persuasive intent* cuts its effect roughly in half, triggering persuasion-knowledge activation and counterarguing. Read together, these two papers argue that what undermines AI persuasion is not machine-identity per se but perceived motive and alignment—a finding with direct implications for the disclosure-focused regulatory instincts documented in [[Mattis2026-gu]]: labeling AI involvement is necessary but, on its own, strikingly insufficient to produce the kind of audience vigilance regulators seem to assume it will.

## Public Perception at Scale: Surveys, Media Framing, and Imaginaries

A final cluster anchors these theoretical and experimental findings in large-scale public-opinion and media-discourse data. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offer parallel national snapshots—American and Italian, respectively—both documenting rapid adoption of generative AI tools alongside persistent skepticism, context-dependent acceptance (e.g., high trust in security applications, low trust in journalism and creative work), and demographic fault lines around age, gender, and education. Fattorini's notion of "critical ambivalence" and Gottfried's finding that adoption and distrust are rising in tandem both resist a simple deficit-model explanation of public wariness, echoing [[Dubey2026-bl]]'s and [[Mattis2026-gu]]'s findings that attitudes are structured, not merely reactive. Nguyen2026-og—more precisely [[Nguyen2026-vm]]—traces the upstream discursive machinery shaping these public imaginaries, showing that news media's mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is real but not dominant, varying by outlet and serving critique as often as hype; this paper effectively supplies the media-ecology explanation for why publics in Fattorini's and Gottfried's surveys hold the particular (ambivalent, symbolically robotic, selectively trusting) imaginaries they do.

## Governance, Labor, and Participation Behind the Curtain

Two papers push the topic beyond perception toward the institutional work that produces AI's trustworthiness in the first place. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] argues that red-teaming—AI's central safety-assurance practice—is itself a sociotechnical and labor system riddled with the same value-opacity, outsourcing, and psychological harms that beset early content moderation, meaning that public trust in "safety-tested" AI rests on an almost entirely invisible human infrastructure. [[Matias2025-px]] complements this by arguing that public and lived-experience participation can improve not just the legitimacy but the actual scientific rigor of AI evaluation, citing cases (e.g., the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis) where participatory methods caught flaws invisible to standard technical metrics. Together they relocate the question of AI trustworthiness from the interface (where [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] situate it) to the back-end processes of evaluation and harm-discovery, suggesting that durable public trust cannot be engineered through disclosure alone but requires reforming who gets to define and detect AI's failures.

## Synthesis

Read as a whole, this body of work traces an arc from foundational questions about whether AI can be a social agent at all ([[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Ng2026-og]]), through a sustained theoretical insistence that its apparent sociality is communicative performance rather than cognition ([[Ptz2024-sl20]], [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]], [[Slack1783073271-slbb]]), into an empirically dense middle layer examining how trust, persuasion, and disclosure actually operate in practice ([[Suk2026-ai]], [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]), and outward into population-level attitudes and the media imaginaries that shape them ([[Gottfried2026-ww]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Nguyen2026-vm]]), before finally turning reflexive about the labor and participatory structures that underwrite whatever trust AI does or doesn't deserve ([[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Matias2025-px]]). The connecting thread is a shared refusal to treat AI's social standing as a fixed technical fact: agency, trust, and legitimacy are consistently reframed as *socially constructed, context-dependent, and politically contestable* achievements—built through media framing, disclosure design, labor arrangements, and institutional practice rather than emerging automatically from model capability.
