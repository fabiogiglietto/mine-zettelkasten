# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The demarcation problem: what is an AI agent, socially speaking?

Before we can ask whether the public trusts AI, or how AI systems behave in social settings, we need an account of what kind of thing an AI system is in relation to social order. [[Anicker2024-vp]] supplies the foundational move for this cluster: agency is not read off internal system properties (reasoning, intentionality, cognition) but treated as a *socially granted status* — a license to issue actions that is conferred, monitored, and revocable through social practices. This relational, practice-based demarcation deliberately displaces essentialist debates about "true" machine understanding, and it sets the terms for nearly everything else filed here: trust, framing, and adoption become questions about how status is attributed and sustained, not about what is inside the machine.

[[Ptz2024-sl20]] arrives at a compatible conclusion from a different angle. Using Luhmann's systems theory and conversation analysis on empirical ChatGPT dialogs, the authors show that chatbots manage conversational trouble without understanding it — their apparent competence is parasitic on the interpretive and repair labor of human interlocutors. This is, in effect, an empirical demonstration of Anicker's point: whatever agency ChatGPT exhibits in conversation is sustained by the social/communicative work of its human partner, not by any intrinsic grasp of meaning. Both papers converge on a deflationary but non-dismissive stance — agency and competence are achievements of interaction, not properties to be located in the model.

## Formalizing the social environment of agentic AI

[[Ng2026-og]] scales this insight up from dyadic conversation to populations of interacting agents. Where Anicker asks how *any* status of agency is granted, and Ptz2024 shows how a *single* system leans on human repair work, Ng2026 argues that once AI systems are deployed in networks of mutual influence, single-agent and even dyadic (alignment/RLHF-style) framings become structurally inadequate. The proposed Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) framework — formalized as an information-exchange function, an influence-dynamics function, and an interaction network — operationalizes exactly the kind of structural, relational thinking that Anicker motivates conceptually, and grounds it empirically in large-scale AI social network data (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability). Together these three papers form a conceptual spine: agency as social status → agency as interactionally accomplished and human-dependent → agency as an emergent, network-conditioned property of populations of agents. The move from single system to social system is the same move that runs through Luhmann-inflected communication theory more broadly, and it reframes AI governance as a population-level, not merely alignment-level, problem.

## Media framing and the public epistemology of AI agency

If agency is socially attributed rather than intrinsic, media discourse becomes a primary site where that attribution happens. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] documents this directly: across 18,032 news articles, mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from dominant, coexisting with technical, ambivalent, and critical framings, and driven more by outlet/editorial practice than by regional culture. This is the mass-mediated analogue of the "license to issue actions" that Anicker theorizes — journalism is one of the practices through which agency-status gets provisionally granted, withheld, or contested for AI systems, and Nguyen2026 shows this granting is uneven, techno-capitalist in its master frame, and concentrated on a few branded products (chiefly ChatGPT). This sets up the survey papers below: public attitudes are not formed in a vacuum but against a background of media imaginaries that are only partially agentic and often ambivalent.

## Modeling trust in generative AI as a communicative phenomenon

[[Suk2026-ai]] proposes the connective tissue between the theory-of-agency papers and the empirical-trust papers: a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI that explicitly inherits from decades of communication research on trust in mass media and social media. This lineage matters because it reframes generative AI not as a wholly novel object requiring bespoke trust theory, but as the next node in a trajectory of institutional and interpersonal trust in communication technologies — a framing that resonates with Anicker's insistence that agency/status is granted through ongoing social practice rather than fixed at design time.

Several empirical papers operationalize pieces of this multilevel picture. [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows that task-specific AI disclosure in journalism produces heterogeneous, non-monotonic trust penalties — fact-checking disclosures hurt trust most, image/idea-generation least — and identifies distinct reader-preference profiles, demonstrating that "trust in AI" fractures along task and audience segment rather than behaving as a single scalar. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends this configurational logic to the boundary between AI-as-search-intermediary and AI-as-news-producer, finding Italians trust automated search results more than AI-produced news — evidence that the *institutional role* AI occupies, not just its technical capability, governs trust, exactly as Suk2026's media-trust lineage would predict. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the intuitive picture further: perceived usefulness and trust (not ease of use) drive acceptance of a "balanced news" chatbot in a TAM framework, but counterintuitively, users with *higher* conspiracy beliefs trust and adopt the chatbot more than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions that distrustful publics will reject AI-mediated information tools.

[[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] pushes the trust-and-persuasion link into explicitly political territory: LLM persuasiveness on factual/economic misconceptions collapses substantially once users perceive the model as politically biased against their in-group, an effect mediated by increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. This is a direct demonstration of Suk2026's claim that generative AI trust behaves like source credibility in classic media-trust research — perceived neutrality functions as a lever of persuasion much as it does for human messengers and institutions, reinforcing that trust in AI is inherited from, not orthogonal to, communication-trust theory.

## Public attitudes at scale: adoption outpacing understanding

The survey papers close the loop by showing what these dynamics look like at national scale. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] both document a common pattern across the US and Italy: rapid growth in everyday AI/chatbot adoption running well ahead of self-reported understanding, with persistent skepticism about societal impact, demands for regulation, and demographic cleavages (age, gender, education, political affiliation) shaping both use and threat perception. Fattorini2026's notion of "critical ambivalence" — simultaneous use and distrust, context-dependent acceptance (high for security applications, low for journalism, education, and worker management) — gives empirical texture to Suk2026's theoretical claim that trust in generative AI must be studied at multiple levels (task, institution, society), since acceptance clearly varies by domain rather than forming a single attitude. Gottfried2026's finding of declining confidence in government's capacity to regulate AI, alongside rising adoption, further shows that use and trust are decoupled — people adopt tools they don't necessarily trust or believe are advancing responsibly, a tension that echoes Mattis2026's and Dubey2026's findings that usage/attitude relationships are moderated by knowledge, ideology, and task rather than following simple technology-acceptance logic.

## Synthesis

Read together, these eleven papers trace an arc from foundational theory to lived public experience. [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ng2026-og]] establish, at different scales, that AI agency is a relational/social-structural phenomenon rather than an intrinsic property — one that must be granted, monitored, and modeled as embedded in networks of interaction. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] and [[Nguyen2026-vm]] show how this status gets contested and sustained in actual conversational and journalistic practice. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes the communication-theoretic scaffolding for treating resulting public trust as multilevel and historically continuous with media trust, and [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], and [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] test pieces of that scaffolding empirically, each finding that trust is configurational — dependent on task, institutional role, perceived neutrality, and audience predispositions rather than a single monolithic attitude. Finally, [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] show these dynamics playing out at the level of national publics, where adoption, ambivalence, and demand for regulation coexist. The throughline is that neither AI's agency nor the public's trust in it can be treated as fixed technical facts; both are ongoing social accomplishments — attributed, framed, and renegotiated across media, institutions, and everyday interaction.
