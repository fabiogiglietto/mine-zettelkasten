# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Demarcating machine agency

At the conceptual root of this cluster sits the question of whether AI systems can be agents at all, and what work that designation does. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes agency not as an intrinsic property of a system but as a socially granted and monitored status — a license to issue actions that is acquired through social practice rather than computed internally. This relational, practice-based account displaces essentialist debates about "true" machine cognition with a sociological question: under what conditions do humans attribute and revoke agentic status? [[Ng2026-og]] extends this move from single-agent demarcation to population-level dynamics, arguing that once agentic AI operates in social environments, social theory itself must become a *structural prior* for system design. Its formal Multi-Agent Social Systems framework (a triple of information exchange, influence dynamics, and interaction network) operationalizes four theoretical priors — strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability — and validates them empirically on a large AI-agent social network. Read together, these two papers bookend the topic: [[Anicker2024-vp]] asks what it means to call something an agent, while [[Ng2026-og]] asks what follows, structurally and empirically, once many such attributed agents interact.

## Communication without understanding

A second strand, drawn from Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis, refuses the agency question altogether in favor of a communicative one. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] shows empirically that ChatGPT manages conversational "trouble" not by understanding it but parasitically, riding on the repair work of human interlocutors; apparent competence is an artifact of surface wording rather than a stable grasp of meaning. [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] generalize this insight into a broader reframing: large language models should be understood as communication partners or "answer engines" that let humans generate meaning from outputs, not as artificial replicas of cognition. This trio effectively dissolves the agency debate from the opposite direction of [[Anicker2024-vp]] — rather than asking what social status licenses agency, they argue the more useful register is communication theory, situating LLMs alongside archives, catalogs, and other historical information infrastructures. The practical upshot, shared across all three, is that improving human–AI interaction is a matter of cultivating human communicative competence, not engineering deeper machine cognition — a claim that quietly underwrites much of the trust and disclosure literature that follows.

## Trust: frameworks and empirical tests

[[Suk2026-ai]] supplies the connective theoretical scaffolding for this topic's largest empirical cluster, proposing a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI modeled on the trajectory of media-trust and social-media-trust research. Several papers can be read as tests or extensions of pieces of that framework. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows that trust is configuration-dependent: Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting the "AI as intermediary" versus "AI as institutional producer" distinction matters more than a generic AI label. [[Mattis2026-gu]] pushes this further by disaggregating disclosure itself: task-specific AI disclosures in journalism all reduce trust, but unevenly — fact-checking disclosures are penalized far more than idea-generation ones — and audiences sort into distinct preference profiles rather than reacting uniformly. [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the intuitive assumption that low-trust populations (conspiracy believers) would reject AI-mediated information; contrary to expectations, higher-conspiracy users trusted and accepted a "balanced news chatbot" more than low-conspiracy users, underscoring that trust in AI systems does not map straightforwardly onto general institutional distrust. Together these three empirical papers substantiate Suk's core claim: trust in generative AI is not a single variable but a multilevel, context-sensitive construct that behaves differently depending on domain, task, and audience segment.

## Persuasion, disclosure, and the limits of transparency

A closely related empirical arc examines AI's persuasive power and what, if anything, checks it. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline capability: frontier conversational AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an advantage traced to sheer information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport or perceived humanness. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then ask what erodes this advantage. DiGiuseppe finds that *perceived partisan bias* — merely being told the model favors the other side — cuts persuasive belief-correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased pushback and counterarguing rather than disengagement. Rauchfleisch shows that *disclosure of AI identity alone* (the EU AI Act Article 50 standard) does essentially nothing to blunt persuasion, but disclosing persuasive *intent* halves it, because intent disclosure — unlike identity disclosure — supplies genuinely new information that activates persuasion knowledge. Read together with [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], this arc suggests AI persuasion is powerful, information-driven, and resistant to identity-based transparency mandates, but sensitive to interventions that disturb users' processing of source motive rather than source type — a nuance any AI-trust framework (cf. [[Suk2026-ai]]) must accommodate.

## Public attitudes: survey evidence across contexts

A third strand supplies broad descriptive data against which the theoretical and experimental work can be calibrated. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] documents rapid U.S. adoption of AI chatbots alongside deep and growing skepticism about societal impact, regulation, and security — with pronounced age, gender, and partisan divergences. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Italy: rising tool use accompanied by a persistent exposure–understanding gap and what the authors term "critical ambivalence," with support highly domain-dependent (high for security, low for journalism and worker management). [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a complementary, occupation-level lens: self-reported displacement anxiety among Claude users tracks measured AI exposure by occupation, while productivity gains concentrate paradoxically at both the highest- and lowest-paid ends of the labor market. These three survey-based papers converge on a shared finding — public sentiment toward AI is neither monolithic technophilia nor technophobia but a highly conditional calculus shaped by domain, demographic position, and direct use — which grounds the more theory-driven claims elsewhere in the topic (e.g., Suk's multilevel trust argument, or Mattis's audience-segmentation findings) in population-level reality.

## Media framing, participatory science, and institutional context

Two further papers address how public understanding and scientific evaluation of AI are institutionally produced. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that news media's mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs — often assumed to drive public anthropomorphism — is actually a minority mode, varying by outlet and editorial practice more than by region or culture, and serving critique as often as hype; this complicates any simple story in which media framing alone explains the "critical ambivalence" documented in [[Fattorini2026-bo]] or the skepticism in [[Gottfried2026-ww]]. [[Matias2025-px]] shifts the frame from public *perception* to public *participation*, arguing that lived-experience involvement can materially improve the scientific rigor of AI evaluation itself — reframing publics not merely as trust-granting audiences (as in the trust literature) but as co-producers of reliable AI science. Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] and [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] extend the topic toward AI's institutional and interpersonal embedding: Goldberg situates LLMs within democratic theory of the public sphere, cataloguing how collective-dialogue, bridging, and moderation systems could reshape deliberation at scale (while warning that synthetic participation remains democratically fraught), whereas the companion-chatbot audit in Slack's paper turns toward the intimate end of the spectrum, arguing that current safety evaluations miss the sustained accommodation dynamics that shape vulnerable users' emotional trajectories. Across both, AI's "social role" appears not as a fixed property to be discovered but as an ongoing accomplishment — negotiated in newsrooms, survey instruments, formal models, and one-on-one conversations alike — that this topic's papers approach from remarkably different theoretical altitudes yet converge on describing as fundamentally social rather than computational.
