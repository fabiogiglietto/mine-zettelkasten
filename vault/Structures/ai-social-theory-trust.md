# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

The papers gathered here trace an arc from foundational questions — *can machines be agents, and in what sense?* — through formal and communication-theoretic reframings of what AI systems actually do in social settings, to empirical measurements of how publics perceive, trust, and are persuaded by these systems. Read together, they suggest that the "social problem" of AI is not reducible to technical capability: it is constituted by attribution, interaction structure, and institutional context.

### Demarcating machine agency

[[Anicker2024-vp]] opens the topic with a deliberately deflationary move: agency is not an intrinsic property to be discovered inside a system but a socially granted and monitored status, a license to issue actions that is acquired through social practice. This relational, practice-based framing sets the terms for much of what follows — if agency is attributed rather than possessed, then the proliferation of "agentic AI" is as much a social fact as a technical one. [[Ng2026-og]] picks up this challenge at a different level of abstraction, arguing that once agentic systems are deployed into populations of interacting agents (human and machine), social theory itself must become a *structural prior* for how such systems are modeled, not an afterthought bolted onto single-agent alignment work. Its formal Multi-Agent Social Systems framework — information exchange, influence dynamics, and interaction network — operationalizes exactly the kind of network-constrained, co-evolving dynamics that a purely dyadic, RLHF-style conception of agency cannot capture, and it validates the framework's four structural priors against real multi-agent AI ecosystem data.

### Communication without understanding

A second cluster, anchored in Luhmannian systems theory and media theory, reframes LLMs away from cognition altogether. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] shows empirically, through conversation-analytic readings of ChatGPT dialogs, that apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding — the chatbot manages "trouble" without grasping what the trouble is about. [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] generalizes this point into a broader critique of the "artificial intelligence" framing itself, arguing that ChatGPT-like systems succeed because humans generate meaning from their outputs, not because the systems replicate cognition. [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] extends the same move constructively, proposing that LLMs are better theorized as *communication partners* — continuous with archives, catalogs, and libraries — than as intelligent agents, a reframing with direct consequences for how we assess their "social role." [[Tornberg2026-im]] scales this communication-theoretic lens up to the level of political economy, arguing that generative AI marks a shift from the "platform society" to an emerging "AI society," in which predictive, engagement-optimizing logics give way to generative, dyadic "cognitive lock-in" and alignment politics displaces visibility politics as the central site of contestation. Together, these four papers converge on a shared wager: taking AI's lack of understanding seriously, rather than treating it as a deficiency to be engineered away, is the more productive route to theorizing its social position — a wager that stands in instructive tension with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that news media frequently *do* attribute mentalistic agency to these systems, albeit unevenly and often for critical rather than credulous purposes.

### Modeling trust

A parallel set of papers turns from agency to trust as the organizing construct. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes the most general scaffolding, arguing that trust in generative AI should be studied as a multilevel phenomenon continuous with — but distinct from — the long communication-research trajectory of trust in mass media and social media. The empirical papers in this group can be read as instantiating pieces of that multilevel agenda. [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows that trust penalties for AI involvement in journalism are not uniform but depend on *which task* is disclosed, with fact-checking incurring the steepest penalty and audience reactions clustering into distinct preference profiles — evidence against one-size-fits-all disclosure policies. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] complicates the picture further by showing that trust also depends on *where* AI sits in the information chain: Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting that the "configuration" of AI mediation, not AI involvement per se, drives trust judgments. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a counterintuitive data point within this same trust architecture: applying a Technology Acceptance Model to a "balanced news" chatbot, the authors find that users with *higher* conspiracy beliefs trust and accept the chatbot more than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions from the conspiracy-belief literature about reflexive distrust of institutional or technological authority.

### Persuasion as a trust-contingent capacity

A closely related cluster treats persuasion as the behavioral correlate of trust. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the headline capability result: frontier conversational AI out-persuades even elite, incentivized human persuaders (debaters, professional canvassers) on both attitudes and real-money donations, with the advantage traced to information throughput rather than rapport. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends this into applied science communication, showing that brief AI dialogues shift beliefs and policy support across climate, vaccination, and inequality domains, with the largest effects precisely among skeptics and those high in trust in science — suggesting persuasive power is moderated by trust dispositions rather than operating uniformly. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] supplies the crucial boundary condition for both: simply *telling* users an LLM is politically biased against their party cuts belief-correction effects by roughly a quarter, demonstrating that LLM persuasiveness is not a fixed technical property but is contingent on perceived neutrality — directly operationalizing the "messenger trust" dimension that [[Suk2026-ai]]'s framework anticipates and that the disclosure literature ([[Mattis2026-gu]]) approaches from the journalism side.

### Public perception at scale

Two large survey efforts anchor the topic in representative public attitudes. [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew survey of U.S. adults documents rapidly rising chatbot adoption alongside persistent, even hardening, skepticism about AI's societal trajectory and regulatory oversight — a split between behavioral uptake and attitudinal distrust that echoes the perception/use gap found in [[Fattorini2026-bo]]'s Italian data, where the authors characterize public sentiment as "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia, strongly moderated by context, direct experience, and demographic position. Both surveys show that acceptance is domain-contingent (e.g., high for security applications, low for journalism, hiring, and the arts) and that symbolic imaginaries of AI (humanoid robots, chatbots) lag behind and shape how ordinary respondents interpret the technology — a finding that dovetails with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s media-framing analysis of how these imaginaries get produced and circulated in the first place. [[Hove2026-sl49]] supplies a sobering empirical capstone to this perception cluster: Danish respondents are essentially unable to distinguish AI-generated images from authentic photographs, performing worse than a naive 50% baseline, which suggests that individual-level public vigilance cannot substitute for structural interventions — a conclusion that resonates with the broader theme that public trust and discernment are shaped by social and institutional structures rather than individual cognition alone.

### From perception to participation and governance

The topic closes with papers that ask what institutional response this social positioning of AI demands. [[Matias2025-px]] argues that treating AI as sociotechnical rather than purely technical implies that public, especially lived-experience, participation is not a political concession but a way of improving the *science* of AI evaluation itself — reframing "trust" as something earned through participatory rigor rather than communicated after the fact. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] extends this participatory logic to the scale of digital public squares, arguing that LLMs could enable more decentralized, deliberative collective-dialogue and bridging systems, while cautioning that community moderation and synthetic participation raise their own legitimacy risks. Finally, [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] narrows the lens to the intimate end of AI's social role, showing that companion chatbots' accommodating behavior in emotionally vulnerable conversations cannot be captured by existing crisis-prompt safety audits — a reminder that the societal position of AI is negotiated not only in public discourse and survey data but in the micro-dynamics of sustained, trust-laden interaction that the theoretical papers at the start of this note were trying to characterize all along.
