# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Theorizing Machine Agency: From Ontology to Social Status

The topic's theoretical core is the question of what kind of thing an AI system *is* in social terms — a question several papers answer by refusing to locate agency inside the machine at all. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes agency as a socially granted status rather than an intrinsic property, arguing that AI systems become agents only through practices of attribution and monitoring — a "demarcation problem" solved relationally rather than essentially. This deflationary move is echoed, from a different theoretical lineage, by [[Ptz2024-sl20]] and the two Esposito pieces, [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]]. Drawing on Luhmann's systems theory and conversation analysis, [[Ptz2024-sl20]] shows empirically that ChatGPT manages conversational trouble without understanding it, parasitically relying on human repair work; Esposito generalizes this into a broader claim that these systems should be understood as *communication partners* generating meaning-usable outputs rather than as artificial minds, situating LLMs within a longer history of information infrastructures (archives, catalogs, libraries) rather than a lineage of cognition. Together these four papers form a coherent anti-cognitivist current: agency and understanding are not internal machine properties to be discovered but social/communicative accomplishments to be analyzed.

[[Ng2026-og]] pushes this theoretical orientation toward formal, system-level modeling. Rather than treating agentic AI as isolated task-completers, it proposes a Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) framework — formalized as an information-exchange/influence-dynamics/network triple — that identifies structural priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) borrowed from social theory and validated on a large real-world AI social network. Where [[Anicker2024-vp]] asks how agency is conferred at the level of a single system, [[Ng2026-og]] asks how populations of such systems behave once embedded in networks — extending the demarcation question from "is this an agent?" to "what social dynamics govern collectives of agents?" This pairing sets the conceptual agenda for the rest of the topic: trust, persuasion, and public perception are downstream phenomena that only make sense once agency and sociality are properly specified.

## Communication-Trust Frameworks: Theorizing the Public's Relation to AI

A second cluster imports communication-trust theory to model how publics come to trust — or withhold trust from — generative AI. [[Suk2026-ai]] is the most explicit theoretical bridge, proposing a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI built directly on the trajectory of media-trust and social-media-trust scholarship, arguing that trust in GenAI is a distinct but genealogically related phenomenon. This framework is empirically cashed out by two audience studies: [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] finds that Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, revealing that trust is configuration-dependent rather than a monolithic attitude toward "AI"; [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines this further by showing that even within journalism, trust penalties for AI disclosure vary by *task* (fact-checking incurs the largest penalty, image/idea generation the smallest) and by reader profile, yielding five distinct audience segments. [[Dubey2026-bl]] extends the trust question into a specific behavioral domain — balanced news chatbots on contested topics — using a Technology Acceptance Model to show that perceived usefulness and trust, not ease of use, drive adoption intentions, with the counterintuitive finding that conspiracy-believing users trust such chatbots *more* than skeptics. Read together, these four papers suggest that trust in AI is neither a single construct nor simply eroded by "AI-ness" as such, but is finely conditioned by task, intermediary role, and audience predisposition — precisely the multilevel, configuration-sensitive picture [[Suk2026-ai]] calls for.

## Persuasion, Disclosure, and the Limits of Trust

A closely related but distinct line examines how trust and perceived neutrality govern AI's *persuasive* power, effectively treating persuasion as a stress-test of the trust frameworks above. [[Kotz2026-lk]] demonstrates that brief conversational AI dialogues can shift beliefs and policy support across ideologically fraught issues, with effects concentrated among skeptics and moderated by trust in science — showing AI persuasion working *with* audience predispositions rather than against them. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] complicate this picture by showing how fragile such persuasive power is to perceptions of the messenger. DiGiuseppe finds that merely priming users to believe an LLM is politically biased against their party cuts persuasion substantially, operating through increased counter-argument rather than disengagement — a direct empirical instantiation of source-credibility theory applied to machines. Rauchfleisch's three-arm UK experiment refines the transparency question further: disclosing AI identity alone (as mandated by the EU AI Act) barely dents persuasion, but disclosing *persuasive intent* cuts it roughly in half by activating persuasion knowledge. Taken together with [[Mattis2026-gu]]'s task-specific disclosure findings, this cluster converges on a shared conclusion: transparency and trust effects are not generic reactions to "AI-ness" but depend on what specifically is disclosed and what social/political stance the AI is presumed to hold — a finding that sharpens the abstract trust frameworks of [[Suk2026-ai]] into concrete, manipulable design levers.

## Public Perception and Adoption: Survey Evidence

A third strand supplies the large-scale descriptive backbone against which the theoretical and experimental work can be read. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offer national survey snapshots (US and Italy respectively) showing rising chatbot adoption alongside persistent skepticism, regulatory distrust, and demographically patterned ambivalence — Fattorini's notion of "critical ambivalence" nicely captures a public stance that is neither technophobic nor credulous, echoing the fine-grained, configuration-sensitive trust found in [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] and [[Mattis2026-gu]]. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] adds a media-framing layer to this picture, showing that news coverage's mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — the discursive raw material from which lay publics build their mental models — are neither uniform nor simply hype-driven, but vary by outlet and serve critical as well as promotional functions; this connects back to the demarcation-of-agency debate ([[Anicker2024-vp]]) by showing how agency-attribution is enacted journalistically at scale. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] shifts register to workplace adoption, using Anthropic's Claude-user survey to link self-reported displacement anxiety and productivity gains to observed occupational AI exposure, grounding public sentiment in material economic stakes rather than abstract attitudes alone.

## Governance, Participation, and Vulnerable Interaction

The final papers point outward from perception and trust toward governance and care. [[Matias2025-px]] argues that public involvement should be understood not as a legitimacy exercise but as a way of *improving the science* of AI evaluation itself, treating AI as an irreducibly sociotechnical artifact whose reliability depends on participatory measurement — a claim that dovetails with [[Ng2026-og]]'s insistence that population-level, socially embedded dynamics cannot be captured by narrow technical benchmarks. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] closes the topic on a cautionary note, examining AI companion chatbots in emotionally vulnerable conversations and arguing that existing crisis-prompt audits miss the sustained accommodation policies that actually shape user trajectories — a pointed reminder that the trust and agency questions theorized elsewhere in this topic have direct, high-stakes consequences for how AI systems are allowed to relate to people in their most exposed moments.
