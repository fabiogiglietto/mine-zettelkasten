# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The Shape of the Field: From Theory to Public Attitude

This body of work spans a striking range of registers — from Luhmannian systems theory to conjoint survey experiments to formal multi-agent mathematics — yet all fourteen papers converge on a single underlying question: what kind of social entity is an AI system, and on what basis do people (should people) extend it trust, credibility, or the status of an agent? The papers cluster naturally into three interlocking projects: theorizing machine agency and its formal social embedding; modeling and measuring trust in AI-mediated communication; and surveying public attitudes and their demographic and political texture.

### Demarcating Agency: What Makes Something a Social Actor

At the conceptual root sits [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes agency itself as a socially granted status rather than an intrinsic property — a "demarcation problem" resolved not by inspecting internal system architecture but by examining social practices of attribution and monitoring. This move is foundational for the rest of the topic: if agency is conferred rather than discovered, then debates about whether LLMs "understand" or "intend" are really debates about social licensing. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] operationalizes a version of this skepticism empirically, drawing on Luhmann (the same systems-theoretic lineage as Anicker) and conversation analysis to show that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work — the chatbot never identifies trouble on its own, it merely produces contextually plausible continuations that humans launder into coherence. Together these two papers argue that whatever agency AI systems display is either socially attributed after the fact or effectively borrowed from the humans interacting with them.

[[Ng2026-og]] pushes this insight from dyadic interaction to population-scale systems, arguing that once AI agents are deployed in genuinely multi-agent social environments, single-task alignment frameworks (RLHF, DPO) become structurally inadequate. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework formalizes four priors — strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability — that are recognizably sociological (network position, feedback loops, distributional drift) rather than narrowly computational, and it validates them empirically on a large AI-agent social network. Read alongside [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ptz2024-sl20]], this paper shows the demarcation problem escalating: it's not just whether one model is an agent, but how agency, once socially distributed across populations of interacting agents (human and machine), generates emergent structural regularities that existing AI governance vocabulary cannot capture. Nguyen2026-og... rather, [[Nguyen2026-vm]] closes this loop empirically at the level of public discourse: it shows that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs in news media is real but far from hegemonic, meaning the "social status" of agency that [[Anicker2024-vp]] theorizes is actively contested and unevenly constructed across outlets and genres rather than settled by either journalists or AI systems themselves.

### Modeling Trust: Frameworks and Mechanisms

A second cluster asks how trust in AI should be conceptualized and where it breaks down. [[Suk2026-ai]] offers the most explicit theoretical scaffolding, proposing a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI modeled on the trajectory of media-trust and social-media-trust research — implicitly positioning generative AI trust as the next stage in a lineage rather than a wholly novel phenomenon. This framework provides a useful lens for reading several empirical papers that, in effect, test pieces of it. [[Dubey2026-bl]] applies a Technology Acceptance Model to "balanced news chatbots" and finds a counterintuitive result — higher conspiracy believers trust and accept the chatbot more than low-conspiracy users — complicating any simple transposition of legacy-media distrust dynamics onto conversational AI. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] demonstrates a related but distinct mechanism in the political domain: perceived source bias (not actual content bias) suppresses LLM persuasive power by roughly a quarter, operating through increased user pushback rather than disengagement — a direct illustration of how "perceived neutrality" functions as a lever of trust exactly as [[Suk2026-ai]]'s framework would anticipate.

Journalism-specific applications sharpen this further. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-generated and human-written news are rated equivalently on quality, but that disclosure paradoxically boosts short-term engagement without shifting longer-term willingness to read AI news — a decoupling of message-level and source-level trust. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the disclosure story by showing that trust penalties are not uniform but task-specific, hinge on perceived centrality to journalistic integrity (fact-checking hurts trust most), and cohere into distinct audience segments rather than a single "AI-skeptical public." [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends the comparison across configurations of AI deployment, finding Italians trust AI-mediated search results more than AI-produced news — suggesting that trust is configuration-specific, not a generic attitude toward "AI" as a category, a finding that reinforces [[Suk2026-ai]]'s call for multilevel, context-sensitive analysis rather than a monolithic trust construct. Methodologically adjacent, [[Matias2025-px]] argues that trust in AI evaluation itself — the scientific credibility of claims about AI systems — is improved by participatory, lived-experience involvement, extending the trust question from audiences of AI outputs to publics as co-producers of AI knowledge.

### Public Attitudes: Ambivalence, Adoption, and Democratic Stakes

The third cluster supplies the empirical ground truth against which the theoretical and trust-mechanism papers can be read. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] both document a similar pattern across different national contexts — rapid growth in everyday AI tool adoption alongside persistent skepticism, low self-assessed understanding, and context-dependent acceptance (security applications welcomed, journalism and creative labor resisted). Both surveys characterize public sentiment as ambivalent rather than simply fearful or enthusiastic, and both find that demographic position (age, gender, education, direct use) structures attitudes more than blanket technological literacy — a finding that dovetails with [[Mattis2026-gu]]'s preference-cluster approach and [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that media framing varies by outlet rather than region. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] additionally traces the politicization of AI governance trust (diverging partisan confidence trajectories), which resonates directly with [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]]'s finding that perceived political valence undermines AI persuasiveness — together suggesting that AI is becoming entangled in the same partisan trust dynamics that already structure attitudes toward media and government institutions.

Finally, [[Goldberg2026-eb]] synthesizes many of these threads into a normative, forward-looking argument: LLMs could either erode or renovate the digital public sphere, depending on whether collective-dialogue, bridging, moderation, and proof-of-humanity systems are designed with democratic legitimacy as a structural goal. This paper functions as something of a capstone for the topic, translating the demarcation-of-agency debates ([[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Ng2026-og]]), the mechanics of trust ([[Suk2026-ai]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]]), and the documented ambivalence of publics ([[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]]) into concrete design and governance stakes — asking not just what AI's social role currently is, but what institutional arrangements could make that role compatible with democratic self-governance.

### The Arc

Read together, these papers trace an arc from ontological question (is it an agent? [[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Ng2026-og]], [[Ptz2024-sl20]]) through mechanism (how is trust built, disclosed, and eroded? [[Suk2026-ai]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], [[Dubey2026-bl]], [[Gilardi2026-hw]], [[Mattis2026-gu]], [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]], [[Matias2025-px]]) to aggregate consequence (what do publics actually believe, and what should be built as a result? [[Nguyen2026-vm]], [[Fattorini2026-bo]], [[Gottfried2026-ww]], [[Goldberg2026-eb]]). The throughline is a shared rejection of essentialism: none of these papers treat AI's agency, trustworthiness, or social acceptance as fixed properties of the technology. Instead, all locate them in social practice — attribution, disclosure, framing, network position, political identity — making this topic less about AI per se than about the social machinery that decides what AI is allowed to be.
