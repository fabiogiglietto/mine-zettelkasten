# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Framing the Problem: Where Does AI Fit in Social Theory?

The collection opens conceptually with [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes machine agency not as an intrinsic property to be discovered inside a system but as a socially conferred *status*—a license to act, granted, monitored, and revocable through social practices. This relational move sidesteps the sterile "does it really understand" debate and instead asks how societies come to attribute agency to nonhuman actors. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] arrives at a compatible but more deflationary position from conversation analysis and Luhmann's systems theory: ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work, not evidence of genuine understanding. Where Anicker et al. ask *how agency gets attributed*, Pätzold et al. show *what attribution obscures*—namely, that the interactional burden of making sense still falls on humans. Both papers converge on a shared insight that recurs throughout the topic: AI's social position is constituted by human practices of interpretation, trust-granting, and repair, not by internal machine properties.

[[Ng2026-og]] scales this insight from dyadic interaction to populations, arguing that agentic AI operating in multi-agent environments requires social theory as a "structural prior" rather than individualistic, single-task assumptions. Its formal Multi-Agent Social Systems framework—validated against real AI-agent network data—effectively operationalizes the demarcation and status-granting concerns of [[Anicker2024-vp]] into propositions about strategic heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability. Together these three papers establish the topic's theoretical spine: agency, understanding, and social position are emergent, relational, and structurally conditioned, not computational givens.

## Media, Imaginaries, and the Discursive Construction of AI Agency

If agency is socially attributed, media discourse is a primary site where that attribution happens. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows empirically that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs in global news are real but far from dominant, varying more by newsroom practice than by region or culture—complicating any simple story of media-driven anthropomorphism. This sits usefully alongside [[Fattorini2026-bo]], which finds that Italian publics hold onto symbolic imaginaries (humanoid robots) even as their practical engagement with chatbots grows, producing "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia. Both papers treat AI's public meaning as constructed through overlapping registers of information, symbol, and experience, echoing the demarcation-as-status logic from the theoretical cluster above but now tracked empirically at the level of discourse and public opinion.

## Trust as a Structuring Concept

A second thread formalizes *trust* itself as the key mediating construct between AI systems and their publics. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework explicitly borrowing from mass-media and social-media trust research, arguing that trust in generative AI needs its own theoretical apparatus rather than ad hoc borrowing. This conceptual scaffolding is tested repeatedly in the empirical papers on journalism: [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-written news is rated no differently than human-written news, but disclosure paradoxically *increases* short-term reading willingness without shifting durable attitudes—suggesting trust judgments are decoupled across time horizons. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates the picture further by showing that disclosure effects are not uniform but depend on *which task* AI performs (fact-checking incurs the largest penalty) and on reader profiles, cautioning against treating "AI disclosure" as a single trust-relevant event. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends this comparative logic across configurations, showing Italian audiences trust AI-mediated search more than AI-produced news—implying that trust is configuration-specific, not a general attitude toward "AI" as a category. Read together, these three papers operationalize [[Suk2026-ai]]'s call for multilevel analysis: trust varies by level (message, task, intermediary, disclosure regime), not just by attitude toward AI writ large.

[[Dubey2026-bl]] pushes this trust literature into unexpected territory, finding that users higher in conspiracy belief trust and adopt "balanced news chatbots" *more* than skeptics of conspiracy—a finding that unsettles assumptions inherited from human-source trust research and suggests AI systems may occupy a different trust position than human or institutional messengers.

## Persuasion as the Behavioral Face of Trust and Agency

A cluster of large-scale experimental papers turns from attitudes about AI to what AI *does* once granted communicative standing. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline: frontier AI reliably out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an effect traced to information throughput rather than rapport—implicitly challenging trust-based accounts of persuasion in favor of a sheer argumentative-capacity account. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows this persuasive capacity generalizes across issues and is strongest precisely among skeptics, contingent on trust in science as a moderator—linking persuasion effectiveness back to the institutional-trust frameworks of [[Suk2026-ai]]. But [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] show this power is fragile and *contingent on perceived neutrality and intent*: labeling a bot as politically biased, or disclosing its persuasive purpose (rather than merely its AI identity), sharply attenuates its effectiveness. This trio collectively argues that AI's persuasive agency is not a fixed capability but a socially conditioned status revocable through disclosure and suspicion—directly instantiating the "agency as social license" thesis of [[Anicker2024-vp]] in a behavioral, experimental register.

## Institutional and Participatory Responses

The final thread turns from description to normative design. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] surveys how LLMs might reshape "digital public squares," treating deliberation, bridging, and moderation as institutional analogues to the trust and agency problems raised elsewhere—arguing AI applications should augment rather than substitute human judgment, a stance that resonates with [[Ptz2024-sl20]]'s insistence that competence still resides in human interlocutors. [[Matias2025-px]] makes the participatory case most directly for AI *evaluation science* itself, arguing that lived-experience publics improve the scientific rigor of AI assessment—effectively proposing a governance mechanism for the "social license" that [[Anicker2024-vp]] identifies as the substance of agency.

## Public Opinion as the Empirical Ground

Anchoring the topic empirically, [[Gottfried2026-ww]] offers a large-scale snapshot of American attitudes: widening adoption of chatbots alongside persistently negative expectations about societal impact, declining regulatory confidence, and durable demographic cleavages (age, gender, race, party). Read alongside [[Fattorini2026-bo]]'s Italian data, it confirms that public ambivalence—simultaneous use and distrust—is not a national idiosyncrasy but a structural feature of how societies currently metabolize generative AI, giving empirical weight to the theoretical claim, running through this whole topic, that AI's social position is perpetually negotiated rather than settled.
