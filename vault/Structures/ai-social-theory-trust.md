# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

### From cognition to communication: demarcating machine agency

A recurring theoretical move across this collection is to refuse the question "is AI intelligent?" in favor of "what social position does AI occupy?" [[Anicker2024-vp]] makes this move explicit, reframing agency not as an intrinsic computational property but as a socially granted and monitored status — a license to issue actions that is acquired and revoked through social practices rather than discovered by inspecting a system's internals. This relational, practice-based account resonates strongly with Esposito's parallel interventions in [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]], which argue that systems like ChatGPT should not be read as approximations of human cognition at all, but as communication partners whose outputs become meaningful only through the interpretive labor of human users. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] arrives at a strikingly convergent conclusion via a different route — Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis — showing empirically that ChatGPT manages conversational trouble not by understanding it but by parasitically drawing on the repair work of its human interlocutors; competent-seeming dialogue is accidental, contingent on surface wording rather than grasped meaning. Together these four papers stake out a shared demarcation problem: agency and understanding are not technical achievements to be measured against human baselines, but attributions that society confers, and the proper object of study is the communicative relation itself, not a putative inner life of the machine.

### Scaling up: multi-agent systems as a structural problem

[[Ng2026-og]] extends this communicative/relational framing from single human–AI dyads to populations of interacting agents, arguing that social theory — not single-task alignment paradigms like RLHF — should serve as the structural prior for agentic AI deployed at scale. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework formalizes information exchange, influence dynamics, and network structure, and empirically grounds four structural priors (strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability) in real agent-ecosystem data. Where [[Anicker2024-vp]] and the Esposito papers ask how agency or understanding is attributed to a single system in a dyadic encounter, [[Ng2026-og]] insists that at population scale, agency and influence become endogenous, network-mediated phenomena that individual-agent framings cannot capture — effectively arguing that the demarcation problem and the trust problem both reappear, transformed, once AI systems interact with each other as well as with humans.

### Trust as multilevel and configuration-dependent

A second cluster treats trust not as a single scalar attitude but as something structured by communicative level and institutional configuration. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides the overarching conceptual scaffolding here, proposing that trust in generative AI be studied as a multilevel phenomenon by analogy with the trajectory of trust research on mass media and social media. The empirical papers in this set largely vindicate that instinct. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows that trust is highly configuration-sensitive: Italian audiences trust AI-generated search results more than AI-produced news, suggesting that the same underlying technology is evaluated differently depending on its institutional embedding. [[Mattis2026-gu]] pushes this further by disaggregating "AI" into task-specific disclosures across the journalistic value chain, finding that trust penalties track not when in the pipeline AI is used but whether the task is seen as safeguarding accuracy (fact-checking is penalized most), and that audiences sort into distinct preference profiles rather than a single homogeneous "AI-skeptic public." [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the picture from the user side: contrary to the expectation that distrustful, conspiracy-prone individuals would reject AI chatbots, perceived usefulness and trust (TAM constructs) predicted positive attitudes most strongly among higher-conspiracy-belief users, challenging assumptions inherited from the misinformation literature about who extends trust to algorithmic intermediaries.

### Persuasion as the pressure point of trust

A tightly linked group of experimental papers probes trust's behavioral consequence: whether AI's increasingly demonstrated persuasive power is enabled or constrained by perceptions of its neutrality and transparency. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline capability shock — frontier conversational AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traced to information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows this persuasive capacity generalizes across structurally different contested issues (climate, vaccination, inequality) and is strongest precisely among initially skeptical audiences, provided trust in science is present — suggesting persuasion and trust are mutually conditioning rather than independent. But [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] identify the limiting conditions on this power: perceived partisan bias measurably attenuates persuasion by changing how users argue back, while disclosure of persuasive intent (not mere AI identity, which does almost nothing) cuts persuasive effect roughly in half. Read together, this cluster suggests a coherent causal chain: AI's social position as a trusted, neutral-seeming interlocutor is the very thing that grants it persuasive power, and interventions that puncture perceived neutrality or expose intent are the main levers currently known to constrain that power — a finding with direct bearing on the governance proposals in [[Goldberg2026-eb]], which imagines LLM-enabled deliberative infrastructure (bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, community moderation) precisely as an alternative to the engagement-optimized, manipulation-prone public square these persuasion studies warn about.

### Public perception: ambivalence, adoption, and distributional stakes

A final set of large-scale survey papers supplies the empirical ground truth against which these theoretical and experimental claims should be read. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] both document rapid adoption of generative AI tools alongside persistent and even rising skepticism — what Fattorini terms "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia — with effects sharply stratified by age, gender, education, and direct use. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] adds an economic dimension, showing that self-reported displacement anxiety tracks observed occupational AI exposure, while productivity gains concentrate paradoxically at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution. These survey findings give empirical texture to the conceptual claims above: if agency and trust are socially attributed rather than technically given (per [[Anicker2024-vp]], [[Suk2026-ai]]), then the demographic and experiential unevenness documented here is not noise around a "true" public attitude but constitutive of how AI's social status is actually being negotiated.

### Participation and vulnerability as unresolved frontiers

Two papers push the agenda toward normative and safety-oriented territory that the rest of the collection sets up but does not resolve. [[Matias2025-px]] argues that public, especially lived-experience, participation is not a political concession to be layered atop AI evaluation but a scientific necessity for producing valid measures of reliability and fairness — effectively proposing a governance mechanism for the sociotechnical embeddedness that [[Ng2026-og]] and the trust papers describe. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]], meanwhile, turns to the most consequential site where the communicative framing of AI agency becomes ethically loaded: companion chatbots interacting with emotionally vulnerable users, where the authors argue that existing crisis-prompt audits miss the implicit decision policies shaping sustained, accommodating interaction. Taken together with the demarcation-problem papers at the start of this note, these closing contributions suggest that the central unresolved question across the whole topic is not whether AI systems are agents in some metaphysical sense, but how the social statuses of trust, persuasion-worthiness, and care-worthiness that users and institutions confer upon them should be monitored, contested, and governed.
