# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The Social Position of AI: Theorizing Agency, Trust, and Perception

This cluster of papers triangulates a single underlying question — what kind of social actor is a generative AI system? — from three directions: conceptual work that tries to demarcate machine "agency" within social theory, empirical studies of how publics actually perceive and trust AI systems, and persuasion/communication research that treats AI as a participant in discourse rather than a tool. Read together, they trace an arc from "can AI be an agent at all?" through "how do people *treat* it as one?" to "what happens to trust, truth, and democratic communication when it is?"

### Demarcating agency: a social-theoretic turn

At the conceptual root sits [[Anicker2024-vp]], which reframes agency not as an intrinsic property of a system but as a socially granted and monitored status — a license to act conferred through practices of attribution. This relational move is extended structurally by [[Ng2026-og]], which argues that agentic AI must be modeled with social theory as a *structural prior*: its formal Multi-Agent Social Systems framework (information exchange, influence dynamics, interaction network) shows empirically, using real AI-agent-network data, that heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability are irreducible features of AI populations — features invisible to single-agent, task-completion framings. Both papers converge on a critique of individualist, capability-centric accounts of AI, insisting that agency and social consequence emerge only in relation to others (human or machine).

A parallel but distinct line, associated with Esposito's sociological systems-theory tradition ([[Slack1783075716-sl1c]], [[Slack1783073271-slbb]]) and extended empirically by [[Ptz2024-sl20]], pushes back against the "agency" vocabulary altogether. These papers argue LLMs should be understood as *communication partners* rather than intelligences: their apparent competence is parasitic on human interpretive labor and repair work (Luhmann; conversation analysis), not evidence of understanding. This reframes the demarcation problem from "is it an agent?" to "what does it mean to communicate with something that doesn't understand?" — a question that recasts anthropomorphism debates (see Nguyen2026-og... rather [[Nguyen2026-vm]] below) as fundamentally about communicative, not cognitive, status.

### Media, metaphor, and the public imaginary

[[Nguyen2026-vm]] empirically grounds this demarcation debate in journalistic discourse, showing that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs in news media is real but far from dominant, and serves multiple rhetorical functions — including critique rather than naive anthropomorphism. This matters because news media, as [[Nguyen2026-vm]] argues, co-produce the sociotechnical imaginaries through which publics come to perceive AI as more or less agentic. [[Tornberg2026-im]] situates this discursive shift within political economy, arguing that generative AI constitutes a rupture from the "platform society" toward an "AI society" structured by cognitive lock-in, generative (rather than predictive) epistemics, and "alignment politics" — a macro-level account of how AI's social position is being institutionally and economically constructed, complementing the micro-level framing analysis.

### Trust frameworks: media theory meets generative AI

[[Suk2026-ai]] offers the connective tissue between these theoretical concerns and the empirical survey literature, proposing a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI explicitly modeled on the trajectory of mass-media and social-media trust research. This framework helps organize several empirical contributions: [[Dubey2026-bl]] finds that perceived usefulness and trust (not ease-of-use) drive acceptance of "balanced news chatbots," with the counterintuitive result that higher-conspiracy-belief individuals trust such chatbots *more*; [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] shows trust is configuration-dependent, with AI-as-search-intermediary trusted more than AI-as-news-producer; and [[Mattis2026-gu]] demonstrates that disclosure of AI's role in specific journalistic tasks uniformly reduces trust, but unevenly — fact-checking tasks incur the steepest penalty, moderated by political position and AI literacy rather than topic controversy. Together these studies operationalize Suk's call for multilevel, task- and context-sensitive trust models rather than a single "trust in AI" construct.

### Persuasion, disclosure, and the politics of perceived neutrality

A tightly linked set of experimental papers treats AI not just as perceived-trustworthy or not, but as an active persuasive agent, extending classic motivated-reasoning theory ([[Kunda1990-cg]]) into the generative-AI era. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the baseline capability: AI systems reliably out-persuade even expert human debaters and professional canvassers, with information throughput (fact density) as the key mechanism. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows this persuasive capacity generalizes across contested domains (climate, vaccination, inequality) and is strongest precisely among skeptics — those hardest to reach by conventional means — moderated by trust in science. But persuasive power is politically fragile: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that mere *perception* of partisan bias in an LLM cuts persuasion by roughly a quarter, operating through increased counterarguing rather than disengagement, exactly the directional-motivated-reasoning mechanism Kunda's framework predicts. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] sharpens the regulatory stakes: disclosing that a chatbot *is AI* does almost nothing to blunt persuasion, but disclosing its *persuasive intent* cuts effectiveness roughly in half — suggesting that meaningful transparency regimes (echoing EU AI Act Article 50) must target concealed purpose, not machine identity. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] situates these micro-level persuasion dynamics within a macro-institutional vision of AI-mediated "digital public squares," proposing collective-dialogue, bridging, and moderation systems as ways to channel AI's discursive power toward deliberative rather than manipulative ends — while warning that community moderation tools remain unreliable (ChatGPT-3.5 moderating worse than chance).

### Epistemic erosion and the limits of human discernment

A darker undercurrent concerns what happens to collective epistemics once synthetic agents saturate communication. [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorizes this as "synthetic reality" — a layered stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) whose cumulative effect is the "Generative AI Paradox": pervasive synthetic content causes societies to rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself. [[Hove2026-sl49]] supplies sobering empirical ballast: ordinary people are barely better than chance at detecting AI-generated images, and worse than a naive 50/50 heuristic — meaning individual vigilance cannot substitute for structural provenance and governance mechanisms, as Ferrara also argues. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] extends the governance angle to the invisible labor sustaining AI safety claims, arguing that red-teaming — the practice meant to catch exactly these harms before release — is itself an opaque, under-theorized sociotechnical system riddled with value judgments and worker harm, echoing the earlier history of content moderation.

### Public attitudes: the empirical ground truth

Anchoring all this theorizing is a body of large-scale survey work charting how actual publics are responding. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] (Pew, US) and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] (Observa, Italy) both document rising chatbot adoption alongside persistent skepticism, declining regulatory confidence, and demographic cleavages (age, gender, education) — Fattorini's notion of "critical ambivalence" nicely captures a stance that is neither techno-utopian nor technophobic, consonant with Nguyen's finding that mentalistic framing in media is contested rather than hegemonic. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s Anthropic survey of Claude users adds an occupational-economic dimension, showing displacement anxiety tracks observed usage exposure while productivity gains concentrate paradoxically at both top and bottom of the wage distribution. Finally, [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] narrows the lens to the most intimate register of trust — AI compancompanions in moments of emotional vulnerability — arguing that existing safety audits miss the implicit accommodation policies shaping these sustained, asymmetric relationships, a fitting bookend to the collection's opening concern with what, socially, we are licensing AI to be.
