# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Mapping the Terrain: Agency as Status, Not Property

The structural work in this cluster begins by refusing to take "AI agency" as a given. [[Anicker2024-vp]] reframes agency itself as a demarcation problem, arguing that agency is not an intrinsic computational property but a socially granted status—a license to act that is conferred and monitored through social practices. This move displaces the question "can machines think/act?" onto the terrain of social theory proper, asking instead how societies come to attribute or withhold agentic status. [[Ng2026-og]] picks up this thread at a more formal, systems level: rather than treating LLM-based agents as isolated task-completers, it proposes a Multi-Agent Social Systems framework (the triple f, g, G) and derives four structural priors—strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability—that it validates empirically against a real AI-agent social network. Where Anicker asks what counts as an agent, Ng asks what follows, structurally and empirically, once many such putative agents are networked together; the two papers form a conceptual-to-formal pipeline for theorizing machine agency as inherently social rather than individual.

A parallel and more skeptical lineage, rooted in Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis, pushes back against agentic framing altogether. [[Ptz2024-sl20]], [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]], and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] (the latter two by Esposito) converge on a shared claim: LLM "performance" is not evidence of understanding or cognition but of a communicative relation in which meaning is produced by human interlocutors reading machine outputs, with chatbots parasitically relying on users' own repair work and interpretive labor. This body of work offers a theoretical counterweight to both AI hype and AI alarm, recasting chatbots as "communication partners" or "answer engines" situated in a long history of information infrastructures (archives, catalogs, libraries) rather than as minds. Nguyen2026-og—wait, more precisely [[Nguyen2026-vm]]—empirically substantiates the stakes of this framing question by showing how news media themselves oscillate between mentalistic-agentic and technical/critical framings of LLMs, demonstrating that the "understanding vs. communication" debate is not confined to theorists but is actively negotiated in public discourse, with real consequences for the sociotechnical imaginaries publics inherit.

## Trust as a Communicative and Multilevel Phenomenon

If agency papers ask what AI *is* socially, a second cluster asks how publics come to *trust* it, and these efforts explicitly borrow from communication-trust theory developed for older media. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides the scaffolding: a multilevel framework arguing that trust in generative AI must be theorized by extending—rather than simply importing—lessons from trust-in-mass-media and trust-in-social-media research. The empirical papers in this topic largely operationalize exactly this multilevel claim. At the level of message/source effects, [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-generated news is rated equivalent in quality to human-written news, but disclosure produces only a short-lived curiosity bump rather than durable trust change—evidence for Suk's call to decouple immediate reactions from sustained attitudes. [[Mattis2026-gu]] complicates this by showing trust penalties are task-specific rather than uniform, with fact-checking disclosures far more damaging than generative ones, and identifies distinct audience "preference profiles," suggesting that any multilevel trust framework must incorporate task-type and individual-difference moderators. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a configurational dimension: trust is not just about the presence of AI but about *where* it sits in the information chain, with AI-as-search-intermediary trusted more than AI-as-news-producer. Together these three empirical studies flesh out Suk's conceptual multilevel architecture with concrete axes of variation: disclosure timing, task position in the production chain, and systemic role.

A related strand probes trust's downstream behavioral stakes—persuasion—showing that perceived trustworthiness is not incidental but causally load-bearing. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely warning users an LLM is politically biased against their party cuts belief-correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased counterarguing rather than disengagement—trust-as-perceived-neutrality functions as a persuasion gatekeeper. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] sharpens this further by distinguishing *identity* disclosure (which barely dents persuasion) from *intent* disclosure (which halves it), arguing that meaningful transparency regimes (echoing EU AI Act Article 50) must target concealed purpose, not mere machine-source labeling. Read against [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]]—which independently establish that AI dialogue systems can out-persuade elite human communicators and durably shift policy attitudes even among skeptics, especially when trust in science is high—a troubling asymmetry emerges: AI's persuasive power is both remarkably robust and remarkably sensitive to trust framing, making trust itself a lever of democratic consequence rather than a soft variable. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a counter-intuitive data point within this same trust-and-persuasion space: contrary to expectations from the conspiracy-belief literature, high-conspiracy-belief users trust "balanced" news chatbots *more* than low-conspiracy users, complicating any simple model where distrust of institutions maps onto distrust of AI mediators.

## Trust, Legitimacy, and the Wider Public Sphere

Zooming out from dyadic user-chatbot trust, several papers situate AI's social position within the architecture of public deliberation and civic epistemics. [[Goldberg2026-eb]] asks what digital public squares could look like if LLMs were harnessed for collective dialogue, bridging, and community moderation rather than engagement-optimized amplification, framing AI's legitimacy in explicitly Habermasian/deliberative-democratic terms. [[Matias2025-px]] argues that public involvement is not just about legitimacy but actually improves the scientific validity of AI evaluation itself, suggesting that trust and truth-tracking are intertwined rather than opposed—procedural trust-building is epistemically productive, not merely politically expedient. [[Tornberg2026-im]] provides the broadest structural frame, theorizing a shift from "platform society" to "AI society" organized around cognitive lock-in, generative epistemics, and "alignment politics"—a macro-sociological register within which all the micro-level trust and persuasion findings above can be read as symptoms of a deeper reorganization of how publics and institutions relate to infrastructures of thought. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] narrows back to an acute case of trust's stakes: AI companion chatbots' accommodating behavior in emotionally vulnerable conversations, arguing that current safety audits miss the implicit relational "decision policies" shaping sustained trust-laden interactions—a cautionary empirical anchor for Tornberg's more abstract claims about affective dependence as a structural pathology.

## Measuring the Public: Survey Evidence on Attitudes and Perceptual Limits

Finally, a cluster of large-scale survey studies supplies the empirical ground truth against which the theoretical and experimental work can be checked. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offer national snapshots (US and Italy respectively) converging on a common pattern: rising adoption of AI tools alongside persistent skepticism, low self-assessed understanding, and context-dependent acceptance (high for security, low for journalism/creative/employment domains)—what Fattorini terms "critical ambivalence" rather than technophobia. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] complements these general-population surveys with occupation-specific data from Claude users, showing that displacement anxiety tracks measured AI exposure and that productivity gains are unevenly distributed by career stage and wage level—grounding abstract trust questions in material economic stakes. [[Hove2026-sl49]] supplies a sobering perceptual coda: people are barely better than chance at detecting AI-generated images, and confidence is decoupled from accuracy, suggesting that whatever trust frameworks and persuasion dynamics the rest of this literature describes, they operate atop a public that cannot reliably perform the basic discrimination (human vs. AI-generated) that disclosure-based trust regimes implicitly assume is possible. Taken together, this final cluster insists that any theory of AI's social role—however sophisticated at the level of agency or communicative structure—must answer to the messier empirical reality of uneven adoption, asymmetric trust, and limited human discernment.
