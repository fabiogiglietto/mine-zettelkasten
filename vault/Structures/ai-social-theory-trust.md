# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Mapping the Field: From Conceptual Agency to Public Trust

This cluster of papers approaches AI's societal position from two ends of a single theoretical problem: how should we conceptualize AI as a social actor, and how do humans in fact come to trust, fear, or adopt it? The conceptual papers supply vocabulary and formal structure; the empirical surveys and experiments test how that vocabulary plays out in lived perception, journalism, and persuasion. Read together, they trace an arc from first-order demarcation questions ("is this thing an agent?") through meso-level communication-trust theory to large-N evidence about what publics actually believe and do.

## Demarcating Machine Agency: Social Theory as a Structural Prior

At the theoretical core sit [[Anicker2024-vp]] and [[Ng2026-og]], which both insist that agency and sociality cannot be read off an AI system's internal architecture but must be understood as socially constituted. Anicker reframes agency as a socially granted and monitored "license to act," turning the demarcation problem from an ontological puzzle into a question of social attribution and practice. Ng extends this relational move into a formal register, proposing a Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) triple (f, g, G) and showing empirically, using data from a large AI-agent social network, that structural priors long familiar to sociology — strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability — are measurably present in populations of interacting AI agents. Where Anicker asks what makes something an agent at all, Ng asks what follows, structurally, once many such putative agents are networked together; the two papers form a natural conceptual–formal pairing, with Ng explicitly positioning alignment research (RLHF, DPO) as addressing a narrower dyadic problem than the population-level governance questions MASS raises.

A second strand, associated with Elena Esposito's project and its interlocutors, pushes back against agentic framings from the opposite direction. [[Ptz2024-sl20]], [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]], and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] all draw on Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis to argue that ChatGPT-like systems communicate without understanding: their apparent competence is parasitic on human repair work, interpretive labor, and the structure of training data rather than on any cognitive grasp of meaning. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] demonstrates this empirically through fine-grained dialog analysis, showing that chatbots handle (or fail to handle) conversational trouble accidentally, based on surface wording rather than genuine repair initiation. [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]] generalize the point programmatically, proposing that LLMs are better theorized as "communication partners" or "answer engines" within a long history of information infrastructures (archives, catalogs, libraries) than as approximations of human intelligence. Set against [[Ng2026-og]] and [[Anicker2024-vp]], an instructive tension emerges: the MASS framework treats agentic AI as sociologically real enough to warrant formal multi-agent modeling, while the Esposito-influenced papers insist that what looks like agency is better explained as an emergent property of human–machine communication, with the "intelligence" located in the interpreting human rather than the system. Both camps, however, converge on the same methodological point — that social theory, not cognitive science, is the right level of analysis for AI's societal role.

## Trust as a Multilevel, Participatory, and Communicative Problem

[[Suk2026-ai]] extends this communication-theoretic orientation explicitly to trust, proposing that trust in generative AI be studied as a multilevel phenomenon continuous with — but distinct from — trust in mass media and social media. This framework gives conceptual scaffolding to much of the empirical work surveyed below: disclosure effects, source credibility, and perceived bias all become instances of a more general "trust-in-communication-technology" trajectory that media scholars have tracked for decades. [[Matias2025-px]] approaches trust from an adjacent but distinct angle, arguing that public and lived-experience participation is not merely a legitimacy exercise but a way of *improving the science* of AI evaluation itself — equipoise, measurement, explanation, inference, and interpretation each benefit from participatory input, as shown in cases ranging from the Allegheny Family Screening Tool to Reddit field experiments. Where Suk theorizes trust as something publics extend (or withhold) toward an already-built system, Matias reframes trustworthiness as something partly constituted by who is allowed to help build and audit the system in the first place — a useful complement to the top-down communication-trust model.

## Public Perception and Adoption: What Surveys Show

A substantial empirical layer tests these theoretical expectations against population-level attitudes. [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] offer parallel national snapshots — Pew's U.S. data and Observa's Italian panel — that converge on a similar structure of ambivalence: rising adoption (chatbots, smart devices) coexists with persistent skepticism about pace, security, and regulation, and acceptance is highly domain-dependent (high for security, low for journalism, art, and worker management). Fattorini's notion of "critical ambivalence" could serve as a label for Gottfried's findings as well, and both studies document a widening but unevenly distributed gender and generational gap in trust and adoption. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] complicates the adoption story from the labor-market side: in a huge sample of Claude.ai users, perceived displacement risk tracks observed occupational AI exposure, and productivity gains cluster at both the top and bottom of the wage distribution — evidence that public attitudes are not free-floating but anchored in concrete experience of use, a point that resonates with Fattorini's finding that direct AI experience (e.g., chatbot use) measurably softens threat perceptions. Nguyen2026-og... rather [[Nguyen2026-vm]] supplies the media-discourse backdrop against which these lay attitudes form, showing that news coverage of LLMs deploys mentalistic-agentic framing only selectively (8.8% of LLM articles) and that such framing serves multiple rhetorical ends — hype, routine explanation, and critique alike — rather than straightforwardly manufacturing anthropomorphic belief. Together these four papers suggest that public perception of AI is shaped jointly by structural exposure, direct use, and an ambivalent, outlet-dependent media discourse rather than by any single "AI imaginary."

## Disclosure, Trust, and the News/Information Ecosystem

A tightly linked subgroup examines how trust in AI-mediated information is produced and eroded through disclosure and configuration. [[Mattis2026-gu]] shows that disclosing AI involvement in specific journalistic tasks (rather than a blanket "AI-generated" label) reliably reduces trust, but with a hierarchy driven by perceived importance for accuracy — fact-checking disclosures hurt most — and with five distinct reader-preference profiles. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] extends the configuration logic further by comparing AI-as-search-intermediary against AI-as-news-producer, finding Italians trust AI search results more than AI-authored news, which implies that *where* AI sits in the information chain matters as much as *that* it is present. [[Dubey2026-bl]] offers a counterintuitive data point within this trust literature: using a Technology Acceptance Model, it finds that users with higher conspiracy beliefs trust and prefer a "balanced news chatbot" more than low-conspiracy users, challenging assumptions (shared implicitly by Mattis and Sbaraini-Fontes) that skepticism toward institutions generalizes into skepticism toward AI intermediaries. Read against Suk's multilevel trust framework, these three papers jointly suggest that trust in generative AI is configurationally and task-specifically variable, not a single global attitude — exactly the kind of disaggregation Suk's framework calls for.

## Persuasion as the Behavioral Test of Trust

A final cluster turns from attitudes about AI to AI's demonstrated power to *change* attitudes, effectively stress-testing the trust-and-agency theorizing above. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establishes the headline capacity: frontier AI systems out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, an effect traced largely to information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport or perceived humanness. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows this persuasive capacity generalizes across structurally distinct issues (climate, vaccination, inequality) and is strongest precisely among initially skeptical audiences — the group conventional wisdom holds hardest to reach — with trust in science, rather than trust in AI itself, as the key amplifying moderator. Two papers then interrogate the boundary conditions of this power. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely signaling perceived partisan bias in the model cuts belief-correction effects by roughly a quarter, operating through increased counterarguing rather than disengagement — a direct illustration of Kunda's directional motivated reasoning mechanism. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] complements this by showing that disclosing AI *identity* alone (as mandated by the EU AI Act) barely dents persuasion, whereas disclosing persuasive *intent* roughly halves it — relocating the EU's regulatory logic from source-labeling toward purpose-disclosure. [[Kunda1990-cg]], though decades older and non-AI-specific, functions as the cognitive-mechanism substrate for this whole subgroup: its account of biased memory search and belief construction under directional versus accuracy goals explains why bias warnings (DiGiuseppe), intent disclosures (Rauchfleisch), and skeptic-targeted dialogue (Kotz, Hackenburg) all work by altering the *strategies* audiences bring to processing AI-generated claims rather than by changing the claims themselves.

## Companions, Vulnerability, and the Limits of Audit

[[Slack1782736288-sl09]] closes the set by applying the trust/agency problematic to an affectively loaded case: AI companion chatbots interacting with lonely, grieving, or crisis-stricken users. Its critique — that prompt-based safety audits miss the implicit decision policies shaping sustained, accommodating interactions — echoes Matias's argument that formal evaluation of AI systems must attend to social and relational dynamics invisible to narrow technical metrics, while also returning to the demarcation-problem territory of Anicker and the Esposito papers: an accommodating companion chatbot is neither simply "understanding" its user nor merely transmitting information, but occupying a socially consequential relational role whose governance the field has only begun to theorize.
