# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## The Question Beneath the Question: What Is AI, Socially?

The four papers filed here converge on a deceptively simple observation: AI systems do not exist in a social vacuum, and treating them as isolated technical artifacts — whether at the level of a single model's outputs or a single user's trust judgment — misses the structural reality of how AI is embedded in, shapes, and is shaped by social relations. But they approach this claim from strikingly different altitudes, from abstract theory of agency down to national survey percentages, and reading them together traces an arc from *ontology* (what kind of thing is an AI agent?) through *structure* (how do populations of such agents behave?) to *reception* (how do people actually trust and adopt them?).

### Agency as a Social Status, Not a System Property

[[Anicker2024-vp]] supplies the conceptual bedrock. Rather than asking what internal properties (reasoning, intentionality, autonomy) would qualify a machine as an agent, it reframes agency itself as a *socially granted license* — a status conferred, monitored, and revocable through social practice, not a fact read off a system's architecture. This is a deliberately anti-essentialist move: the "demarcation problem" of agent versus nonagent is resolved relationally, by looking at attribution and licensing practices rather than at the machine. This matters for the rest of the cluster because it reframes what the other papers are actually studying. When [[Ng2026-og]] models "agentic AI," or when [[Gottfried2026-ww]] surveys whether Americans trust AI's "impact," they are implicitly operating in the space Anicker demarcates: agency and trust are not properties to be measured in the machine but statuses and relations enacted by human (and, increasingly, machine) social practice.

### From Individual Agents to Structured Populations

[[Ng2026-og]] takes this relational premise and scales it up into a formal, population-level argument. Where Anicker asks how *an* agent's status is socially conferred, Ng et al. argue that once many such agents are licensed and interacting, their collective behavior is governed by structural regularities that no single-agent framing can capture — strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, distributional instability. The Multi-Agent Social Systems (MASS) triple (f, g, G) is essentially a formalization of the idea that social theory, not task performance, is the right "structural prior" for agentic AI — a direct extension of the demarcation-problem logic into a generative, empirically testable framework, evidenced with large-scale data from an actual AI social network. Crucially, the paper explicitly distinguishes this population-level governance problem from dyadic alignment techniques like RLHF or DPO, arguing that even a well-aligned individual agent says nothing about the emergent dynamics of many such agents in a network — a claim that resonates with Anicker's insistence that agency/status is not intrinsic to the individual system but constituted at the level of interaction.

### Trust as a Historically Layered, Multilevel Phenomenon

If Anicker and Ng theorize the *agent side* of the relation, [[Suk2026-ai]] theorizes the *audience side*: how publics come to trust (or distrust) these socially-positioned systems. Suk's multilevel framework explicitly borrows the trajectory of communication research on trust in mass media and then social media, treating generative AI as the next node in a lineage of trust-in-communication-technology scholarship rather than a wholly novel object. This is a conceptual companion to Ng's structural-prior argument: both insist that existing single-level or ahistorical treatments (individual trust judgments in isolation, individual-agent task performance in isolation) are insufficient, and both call for frameworks that operate across levels of analysis — individual, interpersonal, institutional, systemic for Suk; agent, dyad, network for Ng. Where Ng offers formal propositions and empirical validation via agent-network data, Suk's contribution is programmatic, laying groundwork for future empirical trust research rather than reporting it.

### The Empirical Ground Truth: What Publics Actually Feel

[[Gottfried2026-ww]] supplies the large-scale empirical texture that the more theoretical papers gesture toward but do not measure directly. Pew's 2026 survey shows a public that has rapidly adopted AI chatbots and ambient AI devices even while remaining broadly distrustful of AI's societal trajectory, skeptical of regulatory capacity, and split along partisan and demographic lines. This is precisely the kind of data that a multilevel trust framework like Suk's would need to explain: adoption and trust are clearly decoupled (usage is high, sentiment is negative), trust in institutions (government, companies) diverges sharply from personal use behavior, and group-level differences (age, race, gender, party) suggest that trust in generative AI is not a unitary attitude but a layered, socially structured one — exactly the multilevel architecture Suk calls for. It also offers indirect, real-world confirmation of Ng's "distributional instability" and "co-evolution" priors at the level of public opinion: attitudes shift over time (Democrats' regulatory confidence dropping sharply, Republicans' rising) in ways that look like population-level dynamics responding to a changing information/technology environment, not fixed individual dispositions.

### Reading the Cluster as a Single Argument

Taken together, the four papers stage a coherent research program: agency and trust in AI are not intrinsic properties of systems to be engineered or measured in isolation, but socially constituted, structurally patterned, and historically situated phenomena. [[Anicker2024-vp]] establishes the theoretical premise that agency is attributed rather than possessed; [[Ng2026-og]] formalizes what follows when many attributed agents interact at scale; [[Suk2026-ai]] extends the same relational, historically-grounded logic to the trust side of the human–AI relation; and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] grounds all of this in the messy, partisan, unevenly distributed reality of how actual publics are adopting and distrusting these systems right now. The open question the cluster leaves is integrative: no paper here yet connects the formal population dynamics of agentic AI systems (Ng) to the empirical structure of public trust and adoption (Gottfried) via the multilevel trust theory Suk calls for — suggesting that the next move in this line of inquiry is to test whether MASS-style structural priors (heterogeneity, network dependence, co-evolution, instability) also characterize the dynamics of public trust itself, not just AI agent populations.
