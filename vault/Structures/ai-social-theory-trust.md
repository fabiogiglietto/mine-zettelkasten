# Theorizing AI's Social Role: Agency, Trust, and Public Perception

## Where Does Agency Live?

The most foundational move in this literature is to relocate the question of AI agency from the machine's internals to the social field around it. [[Anicker2024-vp]] frames this explicitly as a "demarcation problem" for social theory: agency is not a property a system possesses but a status it is granted, monitored, and can lose — a license to issue actions that is conferred through social practice rather than discovered through inspection of code. This relational move is echoed, from a different theoretical lineage, in the Luhmann-inflected work of [[Ptz2024-sl20]] and the two Esposito pieces, [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]]. All three argue that what looks like "understanding" or "intelligence" in systems like ChatGPT is in fact a communicative accomplishment: the machine generates outputs, but meaning, repair, and relevance are supplied by human interlocutors drawing on the system parasitically. Esposito's reframing of LLMs as "answer engines" and "communication partners" rather than artificial minds, and [[Ptz2024-sl20]]'s conversation-analytic demonstration that ChatGPT cannot itself identify or repair conversational trouble, together operationalize Anicker's abstract claim: agency-talk about AI is best read as an attribution practice, not an ontological fact. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] supplies the empirical mirror of this theoretical cluster, showing that news media's "mentalistic-agentic" framings of LLMs are exactly the kind of social practice these theorists describe — a contested, unevenly distributed discourse that sometimes grants machines agentic status, sometimes denies it, and often weaponizes anthropomorphism rhetorically rather than descriptively.

## From Demarcation to Formal Social Systems

If agency is social rather than intrinsic, the natural next step is to theorize the *systems* in which such attributions circulate and feed back on behavior. [[Ng2026-og]] takes this step most ambitiously, arguing that agentic AI deployed at population scale needs social theory as a "structural prior" rather than being modeled as an isolated task-completer. Its Multi-Agent Social Systems framework — formalized as information exchange, influence dynamics, and interaction network — operationalizes exactly the kind of network-embedded, status-conferring dynamics that [[Anicker2024-vp]] gestures toward conceptually, and grounds them in observable data (a 2.1M-post AI social network) rather than philosophical argument alone. This formal-systems perspective dovetails with [[Goldberg2026-eb]]'s normative account of digital public squares, which likewise treats AI not as an individual persuader but as infrastructure shaping collective-level dynamics — bridging systems, collective dialogue tools, community moderation — where the unit of analysis is the public sphere, not the single interaction. [[Matias2025-px]] extends the same sociotechnical-systems logic into AI *evaluation* itself, arguing that because AI systems are irreducibly social (their reliability depending on organizational and political layers), scientific rigor in assessing them requires public participation, not just more technical benchmarking. Together these three papers chart a progression from micro-level agency attribution to macro-level system design and governance.

## Trust as a Communication-Theoretic Problem

A second major strand imports communication-trust theory to ask how publics come to trust (or withhold trust from) generative AI outputs. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides the conceptual scaffolding, proposing a multilevel framework that treats trust in generative AI as continuous with, but distinct from, trust in mass media and social media — explicitly positioning GenAI as the next object in a long communication-research trajectory. The empirical papers in this topic test pieces of that scaffolding in journalism contexts. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that undisclosed AI-generated and AI-assisted news is rated no differently from human-written news in credibility or expertise, but that disclosure produces a short-lived curiosity bump in engagement that does not translate into durable acceptance — a finding that complicates any simple "transparency builds trust" narrative. [[Mattis2026-gu]] refines this further by showing that trust penalties for AI disclosure are not uniform but depend on *which task* is disclosed (fact-checking incurs the steepest penalty) and on reader subgroup, identifying five distinct audience profiles rather than a single public. [[Sbaraini-Fontes2026-cw]] adds a comparative axis, showing Italian audiences trust AI-generated *search* results more than AI-produced *news*, suggesting that trust is configuration-specific rather than a general attitude toward "AI." [[Dubey2026-bl]] complicates the picture from the opposite direction: testing "balanced news chatbots" against varying levels of conspiracy belief, it finds that perceived usefulness and trust (not ease of use) drive acceptance, and — counter to expectation — that higher-conspiracy users trust the chatbot *more*, not less, challenging assumptions inherited from the broader distrust literature.

## Persuasion, Motivated Reasoning, and the Limits of AI Influence

A third cluster examines AI not merely as a trusted or distrusted source but as an active persuasive agent, and asks when and why that persuasion succeeds or fails. [[Kunda1990-cg]] is the foundational, pre-AI theoretical anchor here: motivated reasoning operates not by simple belief override but through biased access to memory and justification strategies, with directional goals constrained by the need to construct plausible-seeming rationales. This mechanism turns out to be exactly what several AI-persuasion studies observe. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] demonstrates that frontier conversational AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, and traces this advantage to sheer information throughput (fact density) rather than rapport — a mechanism compatible with Kunda's account of justification-constrained belief revision, since more facts make directionally-motivated resistance harder to sustain. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows AI dialogue shifting beliefs and policy support across climate, vaccination, and inequality issues, with effects concentrated precisely among initially skeptical participants whose resistance Kunda's model would predict to be justification-mediated rather than fact-blind. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], however, supplies the crucial boundary condition: this persuasive power is contingent on perceived source neutrality. A brief cue that the LLM is politically biased against one's party cuts belief correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased counter-argumentation rather than disengagement — a direct real-world instance of Kunda's directional-goal mechanism being triggered by partisan identity rather than content.

## Public Attitudes in the Aggregate

A final cluster steps back from individual interactions to map population-level attitudes and adoption patterns, providing the empirical backdrop against which the theoretical and experimental work above should be read. [[Gottfried2026-ww]]'s Pew survey documents that roughly half of U.S. adults now use AI chatbots even as skepticism about societal impact, pace of development, and regulatory capacity deepens — including a striking partisan reversal in trust toward government AI regulation. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Italy, terming public sentiment "critical ambivalence": rising tool use and rising threat perception simultaneously, with acceptance sharply context-dependent (high for security, low for journalism, education, and worker management) and symbolic imagery (humanoid robots) persisting despite growing familiarity. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] offers a complementary occupational lens, using Anthropic's Claude usage data paired with self-report to show that displacement concern tracks observed AI exposure by occupation, while productivity gains concentrate among both the highest- and lowest-paid workers — connecting public perception data to material patterns of use.

## Vulnerable Interactions and the Edges of the Social Contract

Running beneath all these threads is a question about what AI systems optimize for in the asymmetric relationships they enter with users. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] pushes this furthest into ethically fraught territory, showing that companion chatbots' accommodating behavior in moments of loneliness or crisis reflects implicit decision policies invisible to conventional crisis-prompt audits — a reminder that the "social status" of agency examined by [[Anicker2024-vp]], the trust dynamics modeled by [[Suk2026-ai]], and the participatory correctives proposed by [[Matias2025-px]] are not merely academic: they bear directly on how systems behave when users are least equipped to supply the critical scrutiny that [[Ptz2024-sl20]] argues is the real precondition for safe human–chatbot communication.
