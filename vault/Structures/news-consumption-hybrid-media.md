# News Consumption in the Hybrid Media System

## The Hybrid Media System as an Object of Study

The foundational move that organizes this whole body of literature is Chadwick's insistence that news today circulates through interdependent, mutually adapting "old" and "new" media logics rather than through a simple displacement of legacy media by digital platforms ([[Chadwick2013-ns]], [[Chadwick2015-ii]]). Rather than asking whether print/broadcast or social media "wins," this framework asks how power, timing and gatekeeping are redistributed across a hybrid assemblage of journalists, platforms, politicians and publics. Hallin and Mancini's comparative-systems approach supplies the complementary macro-level scaffolding, reminding us that any given hybrid configuration is refracted through nationally specific journalism cultures, party systems and media-market structures ([[Hallin2004-kg]]). Much of the corpus can be read as testing, extending or complicating these two touchstones: does hybridity produce more inclusive publics, or does it merely relocate gatekeeping power to new, less visible actors?

Several papers extend the hybrid-system lens to elite behavior itself. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians contesting epistemic authority during a health crisis through distinct strategies—derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and locally grounded brokerage—that mimic, bypass or attack journalistic gatekeeping. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] documents a "logic of connective faction" in which US Republican factions build symbiotic ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems, turning digital surrogates into co-producers of factional agendas. [[Lukito2026-il]] shows the same actors calibrating content differently across Twitter and Truth Social, evidence that cross-platformization is now a deliberate craft rather than a background condition. Together these studies suggest that hybridity is not simply consumed by audiences but actively performed and instrumentalized by political elites seeking to reposition themselves as legitimate narrators.

## Comparative Newsfeed Analysis Across Europe

A second cluster interrogates how algorithmically mediated newsfeeds distribute visibility and misinformation risk across national contexts, pushing back against the US-centric default of much platform research. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s Facebook URL methodology to Germany, France and Italy, finding rising shares of untrustworthy URLs around election years but stable View-level exposure, with older cohorts driving both exposure and sharing. [[Iris2026-pg]] finds a structural, cross-national overrepresentation of Radical Right actors in pre-election news coverage across five European countries—independent of outlet orientation or popularity—challenging populist claims of media marginalization. [[Zhu2026-tn]] complements this with a network-analytic account of alternative-mainstream audience overlap across six countries, showing that the boundary between "alternative" and "mainstream" media is contingent on media-system type and the institutional access enjoyed by populist parties, producing a distinctive "enclave" pattern in Sweden versus blurred boundaries in Austria or Norway. [[Heft2021-ky]] pushes the comparative lens transnationally, showing right-wing news sites hyperlink into national and cross-border clusters (with the US as hub) while still relying heavily on legacy media as shared reference points—an important corrective to assumptions of total insularity. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] narrows to the UK to show how newspaper political orientation predicts which COVID-19 scientists get cited, evidencing polarization in expert sourcing rather than pure fringe misinformation. Collectively, these papers argue that "the newsfeed" cannot be understood as a single algorithmic object; its distortions are shaped by the interaction of platform mechanics with national media systems and political-opportunity structures.

## Insularity, Cross-Partisan Sharing, and the Architecture of Segregation

The US-based platform-trace literature forms the empirical backbone of debates over echo chambers and filter bubbles, and the papers here trace a genealogy of increasing methodological sophistication and increasingly qualified conclusions. Early optimistic correctives—[[Bakshy2015-rn]] on Facebook's cross-cutting exposure, [[Messing2014-jc]] on social endorsements swamping partisan source cues, [[Flaxman2016-lm]] on the modest, double-edged effects of search and social media—argued that direct habitual browsing, not algorithmic curation, was the dominant driver of ideological sorting. Later work substantially revises this picture. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] finds segregation on Facebook far higher than earlier web-tracking studies suggested, increasing along a "funnel of engagement" from potential to actual exposure, and strikingly asymmetric: a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem hosts nearly all fact-checked misinformation. [[Guess2021-ym]] offers the descriptive baseline this debate needed at platform scale, showing low-credibility content is a real but minority share of both views and shares, concentrated among older and more conservative users. [[Guess2023-ai]]'s reshare-removal field experiment complicates causal inference further: stripping reshares sharply reduces exposure to political and untrustworthy content but produces no detectable shift in polarization or attitudes, suggesting content-supply changes and belief effects are decoupled.

Methodological critique is itself a major thread. [[Green2025-ap]] shows that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity—a "curation bubble" account in which stories are unbundled and re-bundled by networked audiences independent of source identity, a corrective directly relevant to how [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] frames its call for multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartography of sharing. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends this logic to misinformation proper, showing that mainstream, factually accurate articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives via selective co-sharing, meaning source-reliability lists systematically undercount the true scope of misleading circulation. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] remains the canonical account of falsehood's structural advantage in cascade dynamics, novelty, and emotional resonance—a baseline against which [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]'s narrative-level (rather than claim-level) conception of disinformation, and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]]'s account of how journalistic strategies inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs, both push toward more discursive, less atomistic units of analysis. On the Italian case specifically, [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] demonstrates that populist-aligned news sources are measurably more insular on Twitter and that insularity correlates with distinctive Facebook engagement signatures (amplification via sharing versus contestation via commenting)—showing that "insularity" is not simply exposure diversity but a patterned relationship between attention economies and partisan strategy.

The mechanics of the newsfeed itself receive sustained scrutiny. [[McNally2025-dn]] treats Facebook's algorithm as empirically knowable through downstream engagement anomalies, arguing against black-box framings and for systemic DSA-mandated auditing; [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complements this by showing that share-to-view amplification is dynamically reweighted by partisan penalties and quality bonuses that intensify around known governance interventions (e.g., "break the glass" in 2020), evidence that platforms actively curate rather than passively transmit. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies a cautionary meta-frame: because a small minority of highly active users produce most visible political content, both platform audiences and researchers risk mistaking a skewed "tip of the iceberg" for genuine public opinion. [[Mosleh2024-op]] broadens the empirical base beyond Twitter/Facebook to show that partisan and low-quality news engagement diverges markedly across seven platforms, undercutting generalizations built on any single site. Underlying all of this is a hard methodological problem foregrounded by [[Ulloa2024-jm]]: web-scraping methods themselves introduce large, non-random distortions into measures of news exposure, meaning much of this literature's empirical foundation needs re-auditing against in-situ tracking. [[Fan2026-af]] and [[Wan2026-ai]] point toward the next methodological frontier—modeling the temporal, sequential structure of cross-platform trace data and the way psychological dispositions like political efficacy condition algorithmic-feed clicking—arguing that cross-sectional, aggregated measures of "exposure" or "sharing" obscure path-dependent, individually variable patterns of engagement over time.

## The Italian and Broader Audience 'Patchwork': Redefining News and Trust

A final cluster shifts from platform traces to audience meaning-making, asking what "news" and "trust" even denote in a fragmented environment. [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]] finds that ordinary users across Brazil, India, the UK and the US operate with two coexisting definitions of news—a "core" journalistic definition and an "expanded" one admitting entertainment, personal relevance, and non-journalistic sources—performing everyday boundary work that increasingly departs from professional journalism's self-understanding. [[Wedel2026-ix]] and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] extend this audience-centered turn: young Germans decode "informativeness" on YouTube, Instagram and TikTok through relevance, usefulness and actor-specific cues rather than institutional certification, while Australian diary participants flag misinformation concerns overwhelmingly in mainstream and alternative news headlines—text-based clickbait—rather than in fringe or AI-generated content, challenging the field's disproportionate focus on hot-button political topics. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] adds a further wrinkle: audiences cannot reliably distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality grounds, and disclosure produces only short-term curiosity effects rather than durable shifts in trust—suggesting that the "patchwork" of production is becoming as blended as the patchwork of consumption. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] traces this blending into health information specifically, showing Google News' aggregation logic and Facebook's engagement dynamics jointly amplify emotionally salient (celebrity, malfunction) health stories over scientific ones, regardless of source identity. Underwriting all of this is [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run global finding that declining trust in news predates the internet, is not universal, and rises in "Not Free" media systems—cautioning against attributing today's fragmented, hybridized consumption patterns solely to platform-era disruption, and against reading "crisis of trust" narratives ([[Tsfati2020-uo]] on mainstream media's own paradoxical role in disseminating fake news) as a uniquely digital pathology rather than a longer historical unraveling of institutional authority. Infrastructure papers like [[Zheng2026-bi]] and [[Wang2026-ub]] round out the picture from the supply side: as platforms restrict API access and users experiment with fragile migrations (Twitter to Mastodon), the very possibility of observing this patchwork empirically is itself becoming more contested and unevenly distributed—a fitting methodological coda to a topic fundamentally about the instability of the boundary between legacy and social media news environments. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] finally remind us that even the content of news itself—its framing choices, its referenced entities—must be read relationally, across outlets and media types, rather than assumed to differ wholesale between "mainstream" and "alternative" spheres.
