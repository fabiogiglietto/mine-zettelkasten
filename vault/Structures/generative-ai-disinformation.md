# Generative AI & Synthetic Disinformation

## Capability: how persuasive is generative AI, and why

The starting empirical question this literature answers is deceptively simple: can conversational AI actually change minds, and if so, how? A tight cluster of large-scale field experiments converges on a surprising answer. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] tests 19 LLMs across tens of thousands of conversations and finds that persuasiveness is driven less by raw model scale or personalization than by post-training choices and, above all, by *information density*—the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a model can pack into a conversation. Crucially, the same levers that maximize persuasion also degrade factual accuracy, producing a built-in persuasion–accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this further by pitting frontier AI against elite human persuaders—professional canvassers and championship debaters—and finds that AI reliably out-persuades even coached experts, again traceable to information throughput rather than rapport or perceived humanness; strikingly, when AI is artificially constrained to human typing speed and message length, its advantage disappears entirely. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this logic to real elections across four countries, showing that brief AI dialogues shift voting intentions more than traditional political ads, again via facts and evidence rather than manipulative psychological tactics—though AI arguing right-leaning positions is measurably less accurate than AI arguing left-leaning ones, an asymmetry inherited from the information environment itself. [[Costello2024-bg]] rounds out this arc by showing the same mechanism can be turned toward epistemic repair: personalized, evidence-dense AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief, undercutting motivational accounts that treat such beliefs as immune to counter-evidence. Together these four papers establish a load-bearing finding for the whole topic: generative AI's persuasive power is not primarily about mimicking humanness or exploiting psychological vulnerability, but about weaponizing (or repairing) belief through scaled, cheaply produced argumentative density—a mechanism that is agnostic to whether the claims themselves are true.

## From text to image: engineering visual and coordinated persuasion

A second cluster shifts from what AI *says* to what it *shows*, and from individual persuasion to industrial-scale campaign production. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] provides the topic's central case study: an analysis of over 2,300 images from 223 coordinated Facebook gambling groups shows an exponential, structurally-broken jump in posting volume immediately after ChatGPT's public launch, with generative AI not replacing but intensifying pre-existing persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization) through hyper-real, surreal aesthetics that evade influencer-accountability mechanisms. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] generalizes this into a broader analytic category—Deceptive Information Operations—arguing that a pro-Kremlin propaganda network, the same gambling ecosystem studied by Giglietto, and a poorly-moderated adult-content network all share a common structural logic of affordance exploitation and simulated coordination, regardless of whether the content is technically "false." Both papers converge on the same institutional diagnosis: Meta's asymmetric governance—strict rules for paid ads, permissive rules for organic content—creates the regulatory grey zone these networks exploit, and platform reporting yields acknowledgment but little enforcement. [[Orlando2025-ul]] supplies a mechanistic complement from the production side, using generative agent-based modeling to show that LLM agents *spontaneously* reproduce the coordination signatures documented empirically in these Facebook networks—narrative convergence, synchronized amplification, rapid hashtag diffusion—once agents are merely made aware of their teammates, with no need for explicit central direction. [[Tang2026-gu]] closes this loop from a labor-and-platform-culture angle, documenting Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable output—benchmarking, testing formats, running matrix-account systems—showing that the industrialization Giglietto and Giada infer from network traces is also an explicit, self-aware creative practice on the production side.

## Reception: does synthetic persuasion actually land, and on whom?

A third strand asks whether audiences are actually moved by this synthetic content, and under what conditions—complicating any assumption that better fakes automatically mean more persuasion. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds that AI-generated visual disinformation does *not* uniformly outperform text; decontextualized real video only beats text when abundant authentic footage exists to lend it plausibility (as with the Ukraine war, but not the more epistemically ambiguous MH370 case), suggesting that low-tech recontextualization can rival high-tech synthesis in real-world persuasive power. [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a psychological aftereffect largely orthogonal to content type: difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and susceptibility toward the *next* piece of synthetic content encountered in the same format. [[Wack2026-bt]], working in Kenya with a large survey experiment on fabricated candidate-confession deepfakes, shows that a video's impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated: credulous comments amplify harm, skeptical comments partially restore support, and partisanship determines whose skepticism gets believed—evidence for treating deepfakes as flexible cues within a participatory disinformation ecosystem rather than freestanding artifacts. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] offers a partial counterpoint from platform-vernacular research: on Canadian TikTok, pro-immigration content substantially outweighs anti-immigration content, and generative AI appears only at the margins, used for racist "speculative worldbuilding"—a reminder that the affordances enabling synthetic persuasion can also, on the same platform, support inclusive framings, and that AI's presence in a discourse is not proof of its dominance.

## Systemic capture: states, platforms, and epistemic infrastructure

A fourth cluster raises the stakes from campaign-level manipulation to systemic epistemic risk. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state media control has already seeped into LLM training data itself: Chinese state-scripted content is memorized by commercial models, and pro-regime valence increases with language exposure tied to lower-media-freedom states—an indirect, largely invisible pathway of institutional influence distinct from direct model regulation. [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents a more direct case of elite capture, showing that Grokipedia is heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically over-cites unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics, with citation of the chatbot itself as evidence—a novel "auto-citogenesis" that exemplifies how a single actor's incentives can reshape a purportedly neutral knowledge infrastructure. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complicates any universalizing narrative about AI-driven authoritarian propaganda by comparing six democratically weakened states and finding that GenAI's propaganda uses are structurally contingent rather than uniform, cautioning against techno-deterministic readings of the other papers in this cluster. [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] both extrapolate from documented mechanisms toward frontier risk: Schroeder theorizes "malicious AI swarms"—agentic systems that fuse the coordination dynamics modeled empirically by Orlando2025-ul with persistent memory and cross-platform adaptation to manufacture consensus and poison training data, while Ferrara's "Generative AI Paradox" argues the terminal risk is not any single deepfake but a layered "synthetic reality" stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) that, once pervasive, licenses rational discounting of all digital evidence—an epistemic tax that benefits strategic deniers regardless of which side controls the technology.

## Governance and the political economy of scams

The final strand asks what should be done, situating disinformation and AI-scams within a broader political economy of platform capitalism. [[Swartz2026-zb]] reframes the entire topic at the level of genre: scams, she argues, are no longer peripheral criminal activity but a central organizing logic of platform economies, with the boundary between "legitimate" and "scam" itself a site of governance contestation, and anti-fraud infrastructure a vehicle for expanding behavioral surveillance—an argument that resonates with Giglietto's and Giada's findings about the profitability of regulatory grey zones. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] operationalizes this into policy, mapping the deepfake financial-fraud ecosystem across platforms, telecoms, and financial institutions and arguing that liability must shift from individual victims to gatekeepers; its comparative review of jurisdictional models (UK reimbursement mandates, Singapore's shared-responsibility framework, Taiwan's federated bank data-sharing) offers a concrete governance vocabulary for the platform-amplification problem that Giglietto and Giada diagnose empirically. Read together, this closing pair insists that the arc traced by the rest of the topic—from capability, to production, to reception, to systemic capture—cannot be countered by artifact-level detection alone; because scams and synthetic disinformation are now infrastructural features of the platform economy rather than exceptions to it, effective countermeasures must target the amplification and liability structures that make industrial-scale synthetic persuasion profitable in the first place.
