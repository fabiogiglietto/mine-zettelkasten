# Generative AI and Disinformation

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces a single, urgent question across very different methods and sites: what happens to persuasion, trust, and truth when generative AI makes synthetic content cheap, fluent, and infinitely scalable? The collection moves from a concrete empirical case of AI-generated visual persuasion in a commercial-criminal setting, outward to theoretical accounts of coordinated AI-driven influence, and finally to debates about whether audiences — and institutions — can still tell the difference.

## The anchor case: gambling, visuals, and industrial-scale persuasion

The empirical center of gravity is [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]], which documents how coordinated Facebook gambling networks adopted generative AI almost immediately after ChatGPT's late-2022 launch, producing an exponential, structurally-breaking surge in post volume. Its contribution is to show that generative AI does not invent new persuasion logics so much as *intensify* and *recombine* pre-existing visual drivers — aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization — through a hyper-real AI aesthetic that is cheaper to produce and harder to attribute to any single accountable actor. Crucially, the paper locates the enabling mechanism not in content per se but in Meta's asymmetric governance of paid versus organic content, anticipating a recurring theme across this literature: the problem is infrastructural and economic as much as technological. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends this logic to deepfake-enabled financial fraud more broadly, arguing that platforms, telecoms, and financial intermediaries — not individual victims — are the appropriate locus of liability, and cataloguing regulatory experiments (Singapore's COSMIC, Taiwan's federated-learning bank alliance, UK reimbursement mandates) that model a gatekeeper-centered response Giglietto's own regulatory-gap diagnosis implicitly calls for.

## From artifacts to swarms: how production scales

Several papers theorize the *production side* of this shift. [[Orlando2025-ul]] offers an agent-based simulation showing that LLM agents, given even minimal mutual awareness, spontaneously reproduce the coordination signatures of real information operations — network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — without being explicitly instructed to do so, suggesting that coordination inauthenticity may become an emergent rather than engineered property of agentic systems. [[Schroeder2026-im]] pushes this further into a governance register, arguing that "malicious AI swarms" constitute a qualitative break from botnet-era influence operations because they combine persistent memory, adaptive self-optimization, and human-mimicry that evades existing synchrony-based detectors; its proposed remedies (provenance infrastructure, an "AI Influence Observatory," commercial-incentive levers) echo Schiffrin's gatekeeper framing. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds these abstractions in a concrete labour-process account of Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable output via benchmarking and matrix-account systems — a vernacular, platform-level analogue to the gambling networks' industrialization strategy. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] supply the cultural-theoretical register for this same material, reframing "slop" not as failed content but as a *gimmick* (Ngai) that works simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," and as an affective "lure" optimized through RLHF alignment to attune to platform moods rather than to persuade propositionally — a useful corrective to accounts (including Giglietto's) that still largely read AI visuals as rational-persuasive devices. [[Crichton2026-kn]] documents a disturbing extension of this production logic into far-right propaganda, showing how commercial image models can "sanitize" explicit extremist imagery while preserving its underlying message, effectively laundering hate content back into circulation.

## Political capture: elections, populism, and state influence

A parallel strand examines generative AI's entry into electoral and state politics. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] document far-right and populist adoption of AI-generated multimedia across European and democratically weakened contexts respectively, with Beacken explicitly arguing against techno-deterministic readings: adoption patterns are structured by local political-economic conditions rather than following one global trajectory. [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Triedman2025-uy]] push the institutional-capture argument to its most systemic form, showing respectively that state-coordinated media is memorized into LLM training data and shapes model outputs along lines of national media freedom, and that Musk's Grokipedia systematically over-cites low-quality and ideologically aligned sources relative to Wikipedia on controversial topics. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this with a discourse-level view, mapping how national press systems themselves construct divergent utopian/dystopian "sociotechnical imaginaries" of generative AI — the symbolic terrain on which these institutional struggles over AI's meaning play out.

## Does it actually work? The persuasion evidence base

A crucial counter-current interrogates whether synthetic content delivers the persuasive power often assumed. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Lin2025-xp]] run large field-like experiments showing that conversational AI *can* shift political attitudes substantially, but primarily through information density and factual (if sometimes inaccurate) argumentation rather than psychological manipulation or microtargeting — complicating alarmist readings while surfacing a genuine persuasion-accuracy trade-off and partisan asymmetries in claim accuracy. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows the same mechanism can be turned toward correction: personalized AI dialogue durably reduces conspiracy belief, suggesting debunking failures were previously an evidence-depth problem, not evidence of immovable motivated cognition. On the visual side, [[Hameleers2026-mc]] is a critical corrective to the Giglietto-style emphasis on AI imagery: in controlled comparison, decontextualized *real* video outperformed both text and AI-generated images, and only for highly salient, high-footage-availability issues — implying that generative AI's visual advantage may be issue-bound rather than universal. [[Hove2026-sl49]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] study the perceptual side directly: people are barely better than chance at spotting AI images, confidence is decoupled from accuracy, and prior difficulty authenticating one deepfake carries over to shape (over)confidence toward subsequent ones in the same modality — jointly implying that individual vigilance is an inadequate safeguard. [[Wack2026-bt]] relocates the question from the individual to the social: in Kenya, a deepfake's political impact was shown to be negotiated through surrounding comment-section skepticism or credulity, reframing deepfake harm as a matter of "collective sensemaking" rather than fixed artifact properties — a finding with direct bearing on how platforms like those studied by Giglietto structure not just exposure but interpretive context.

## Epistemology and countermeasures

A final cluster steps back to ask what these developments do to the concept of "fact" and what can be done about it. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] and [[Emilio2026-ik]] both argue that existing epistemic and risk vocabularies are inadequate: Dierickx proposes "emergent facts" as a category between evidence-based, interpretative, and institutional truth, while Ferrara's "Generative AI Paradox" warns that ubiquitous synthetic media may rationally induce wholesale discounting of digital evidence, raising the "cost of truth" itself — a macro-diagnosis that frames the gambling, swarm, and slopaganda cases as symptoms of a single emerging condition of epistemic insecurity. [[Manovich2026-ih]] offers a deliberately counter-framing, insisting generative AI be studied as a creative *medium* with its own cognitive and stylistic logic rather than solely as a social harm — a useful friction against the otherwise uniformly alarmed tenor of this collection. On the countermeasures side, [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] audits how Google's AI Overviews handle conspiratorial queries, finding moderation uneven and sometimes self-defeating for newer narratives, while [[Gilardi2026-hw]] shows that disclosure of AI authorship in journalism does not damage perceived quality and can even boost short-term engagement — suggesting that transparency-based interventions, central to Schiffrin's and Schroeder's policy proposals, are at least not self-sabotaging from an audience-reception standpoint, even if their long-term efficacy against coordinated, swarm-like, and slop-driven production remains, across this entire body of work, an open and urgent question.
