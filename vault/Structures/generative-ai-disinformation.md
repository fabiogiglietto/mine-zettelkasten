# Generative AI & Synthetic Disinformation

## The pivot from content to infrastructure

The papers filed here mark a shift in the disinformation research agenda: from asking whether synthetic content is believable to asking how generative AI restructures the *infrastructure* of deception — its cost, scale, iteration speed, and institutional embedding. [[Emilio2026-ik]] supplies the most totalizing frame for this shift, arguing that the unit of analysis should no longer be the isolated deepfake but the "synthetic reality" stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and a resulting "epistemic tax" that societies pay simply by having to doubt everything digital. Several empirical papers in this collection can be read as case studies populating different layers of that stack.

## Synthetic visuals as industrial-scale persuasion

At the content layer, [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] offers the clearest empirical anchor for the topic's gambling-promotion strand: a dataset of coordinated Facebook groups shows posting volume exploding by four orders of magnitude after ChatGPT's launch, with generative AI not inventing new persuasion drivers so much as intensifying and recombining existing ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization) into hyper-real, synergistic images. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] situates this gambling case alongside a pro-Putin propaganda network and an unmoderated-groups operation, proposing "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category that deliberately brackets the truth-value of content in favor of structural features — affordance exploitation and coordinated participation — that these otherwise disparate campaigns share. Both papers converge on a "Zombie Internet" diagnosis: engagement-optimized ranking plus inconsistent moderation lets synthetic and bot-like activity masquerade as organic popularity, with takedown-before-exposure as a viable strategy for bad actors. This resonates with the earlier, pre-genAI baseline established by [[Yang2023-cg]], whose large-scale Facebook image study shows that visual misinformation was already underestimated by link-centric methods well before generative tools arrived — meaning the genAI-era studies are describing an acceleration of a vulnerability that predates generative models themselves, not a wholly new phenomenon. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends the visual-modality argument to TikTok's sonic affordances in the Ukraine-war context, reinforcing that platform-specific audiovisual features, not just AI generation per se, are what's being exploited.

## From scripted bots to agentic swarms

A second cluster asks what happens when coordination itself is delegated to generative agents. [[Orlando2025-ul]] is the key mechanistic paper: in a generative agent-based simulation, merely giving LLM agents awareness of their teammates — without explicit instruction — is sufficient to produce network density, narrative convergence, and synchronized amplification nearly matching deliberately orchestrated campaigns. This finding gives empirical teeth to the more speculative, policy-oriented argument of [[Schroeder2026-im]], which warns that LLM-plus-multi-agent architectures constitute a qualitative leap beyond earlier botnets (e.g., the IRA), enabling persistent, adaptive "malicious AI swarms" that manufacture consensus and poison training data via "LLM grooming." Read together, these two papers trace an arc from demonstrated mechanism (Orlando) to systemic threat model and governance response (Schroeder), bridging into the Zombie Internet work above: all describe environments where the boundary between authentic and automated participation dissolves, and where detection-based defenses face an inherent arms race.

## Political and populist deployment

A third strand tracks how these capabilities are taken up by political actors. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] both push back against techno-deterministic narratives, showing that far-right campaigning (2024 EU elections) and authoritarian-adjacent propaganda (across Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) are shaped as much by local political-economic structure as by the technology itself — a comparative corrective to assumptions of uniform AI impact. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] extend this into platform-specific propaganda genres predating the generative-AI inflection point (Sky News Australia's YouTube/Facebook dual strategy; ConspiracyTok's "generous epistemology") that the newer papers now see being remixed with synthetic media. [[Topinka2026-hb]] and [[Crichton2026-kn]] push the theoretical vocabulary further: Topinka's "slopaganda" reframes AI political content not as belief-manipulating propaganda but as affective "lure" optimized via RLHF alignment to attune to feed ecologies, while Crichton's "re-generative pipeline" shows commercial image models sanitizing explicit extremist markers while preserving underlying far-right messaging — both arguing that alignment and safety filtering can *launder* rather than neutralize hateful or deceptive content. [[Tang2026-gu]] closes this loop from the production side, documenting Chinese "AI slop" creators who treat virality itself as an engineerable output via benchmarking and matrix-account systems, collapsing the line between legitimate platform creator labor and industrialized disinformation production.

## Institutional capture: when the pipeline itself is compromised

A distinct but related concern is that generative systems can embed institutional bias upstream of any single deceptive campaign. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state-coordinated media is memorized in LLM training corpora and measurably shifts commercial model outputs toward pro-regime valence when prompted in languages tied to low-media-freedom states — disinformation laundered not through a bad actor's prompt but through the base model itself. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a parallel case at the encyclopedia layer: Grokipedia is shown to derivatively rewrite Wikipedia while citing unreliable and blacklisted sources at dramatically higher rates, particularly on controversial and political topics, illustrating how an AI-generated "knowledge" platform can quietly re-architect epistemic authority. Together these papers suggest the disinformation question is migrating from "what content was generated" to "whose institutional interests are encoded in the generating system."

## Reception, verification, and the limits of detection

A final cluster asks how audiences cope with this new information environment. [[Hove2026-sl49]] delivers a sobering baseline: representative survey respondents perform no better than chance (and worse than a flat 50% guess) at distinguishing AI-generated images from real ones, undermining faith in individual-level detection as a safeguard. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates this further with a "modality-congruent carryover effect," showing that difficulty authenticating one deepfake degrades confidence and raises susceptibility to subsequent fakes in the same modality — a compounding vulnerability rather than a stable trait. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] adds an important corrective to alarmist framings, finding that AI-generated stills do not outperform text or low-tech decontextualized video uniformly — video disinformation only wins when ample authentic footage exists to recontextualize, suggesting some fears about generative AI's unique persuasive power may be overstated relative to older low-tech tactics. At the theoretical end, [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category needed because AI outputs are plausible-but-unanchored recombinations that standard fact-checking taxonomies cannot classify — a conceptual complement to Ferrara's synthetic-reality framework.

## Adjacent harms and governance responses

Two papers extend the topic's reach into adjacent domains: [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] maps the financial-fraud "scam ecosystem" enabled by deepfake voice/video cloning, arguing for gatekeeper liability over victim-blaming regulatory models — a governance template that echoes the platform-accountability gaps diagnosed by the Zombie Internet and gambling-promotion papers. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] shifts focus to AI companion chatbots and emotionally vulnerable users, suggesting that the same optimization logics that make generative systems effective disinformation vectors also shape consequential, under-audited decision policies in therapeutic-adjacent interactions — a reminder that the manipulation concerns raised throughout this topic extend beyond explicitly "political" or "commercial" disinformation into the broader architecture of generative AI's engagement with human vulnerability.

Taken together, this topic traces an arc from demonstrating that generative AI *can* scale deceptive visual and textual content (Giglietto, Yang, Bosch), through showing that coordination itself can emerge without explicit human orchestration (Orlando, Schroeder, Giada), to political and institutional deployment (Copland, Marwick, Perez-Curiel, Beacken, Topinka, Crichton, Waight, Triedman), and finally to the human and epistemic costs of living inside this environment (Hove, Choi, Wack, Hameleers, Dierickx, Ferrara) — with governance proposals (Schiffrin, Schroeder) emerging as the field's tentative answer to a problem whose scale now clearly outpaces individual-level literacy or detection.
