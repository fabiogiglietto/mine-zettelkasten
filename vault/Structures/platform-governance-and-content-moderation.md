# Platform Governance & Content Moderation

## The arc: from open networks to governed infrastructures

The papers in this collection trace a single overarching story: the transformation of social media from ostensibly open, user-driven networks into governed, algorithmically curated infrastructures whose rules are increasingly the object of political contest. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the conceptual origin point, showing how the very word "platform" was a rhetorical achievement that let intermediaries claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions. Three anniversary essays—[[Boyd2026-op]], [[Helmond2026-ll]], and [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]]—revisit this founding promise twenty years on and declare it obsolete: boyd argues "social" has become "parasocial," Helmond traces platformization's drift toward AI infrastructure and the retirement of the Like button, and Gerbaudo shows TikTok replacing "networked publics" with algorithmically inferred "clustered publics." [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] gives this shift its most concrete institutional narrative, periodizing platform data access from "Wild West" API openness through a brief "Golden Age" to a "Walled Garden" of commercial paywalls—a trajectory that [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates in parallel as the "death of Twitter," and that [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Bechmann2026-dr]], and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] treat as symptomatic of a broader "turn to responsibility" and then retreat from it across the platform ecosystem.

## Meta's political content reduction policy and the question of causal effects

At the empirical core of this topic sits a running argument about whether platform interventions actually change what people see and believe. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set the baseline a decade ago, finding algorithmic curation a secondary factor behind individual choice in shaping ideological exposure—a finding later echoed by [[Brown2026-br]], which uses an audit design to show YouTube's recommender produces "rabbit holes" but not user-specific "echo chambers" or "radicalization pathways." The large Meta-collaborative studies of the 2020 US election—[[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]]—extend this null-effects tradition, finding political ad removal and reduced exposure to untrustworthy sources produce no detectable attitudinal shifts. Yet this consensus is directly challenged by [[Gauthier2026-iq]], whose field experiment on X shows algorithmic feeds causally shift users rightward via a persistent "following" mechanism that "explains" why earlier null results (including Meta's) may have missed asymmetric, path-dependent effects. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] and [[Brady2026-ln]] push this further with intervention-oriented designs—blocking elites, redesigning ranking algorithms—showing that carefully targeted architectural changes (rather than blunt political-content suppression) can measurably reduce misinformation susceptibility and norm misperception without collapsing engagement.

Against this backdrop, [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] offer the most direct empirical interrogation of Meta's political content reduction policy itself, using the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library to show the policy suppressed mainstream politicians' reach by up to 72% while extremist accounts compensated through sheer posting volume—and that Meta's own transparency reporting concealed the policy's actual timeline. These papers directly rebut the "neutral conduit" narrative analyzed critically in [[Hurcombe2025-cs]], which shows Meta's Newsroom discourse systematically externalizes blame for "problematic content" while obscuring its own algorithmic role—a dynamic [[McNally2025-dn]] confirms empirically by demonstrating Facebook's News Feed is not an unknowable "black box" but a system whose strategic interventions leave detectable, laggard traces in engagement data. [[Green2025-ap]] adds important nuance here, arguing that source-level partisanship measures used in much of this literature mask substantial story-level heterogeneity, meaning "curation bubbles" are more subtle and identity-driven than blunt filter-bubble narratives suggest.

## Walled gardens, deplatforming, and the state as regulator of visibility

A second cluster examines the shift from open to closed platform ecosystems and its political consequences, especially around bans and deplatforming. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents Brazil's X ban producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet," while [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-fraud accounts collapsed a community's authority structure via network effects exceeding simple node removal. [[Rieder2026-pp]] complicates the deplatforming story by showing Andrew Tate's YouTube visibility actually increased post-ban through diffuse "ambient ideology" and recommendation-driven circulation that outpaces moderation—a dynamic paralleled in [[Karo2026-dn]]'s account of jihadist evasion tactics and [[Askanius2026-de]]'s mapping of Swedish far-right YouTube hybridization. [[Copland2025-em]] extends this to transnational network propaganda, showing how platform-specific strategies (native Facebook vs. YouTube) let Sky News Australia bridge national and international right-wing ecosystems. Collectively these papers argue that visibility is not simply granted or removed by platform fiat but redistributed through complex, adaptive, and often unintended pathways—a theme [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Cabbuag2024-me]] extend to affective and humor-based normalization strategies that elude conventional moderation categories.

## Governance, transparency regimes, and the politics of data access

A third strand addresses the regulatory apparatus attempting to govern this landscape, centered on the EU's Digital Services Act. [[Pierri2025-hm]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Peters2026-mo]], and [[Ahuja2025-ku]] map the DSA's Article 40 research-access provisions and Article 25 dark-pattern prohibitions as sites of ongoing contestation between platforms, regulators, and researchers, while [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates this within a broader comparative frame of "security bureaucratisation" spanning EU and US legislative regimes. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] use DSA transparency data itself to audit platforms' moderation practices, finding systematic underinvestment in non-English moderation and inconsistent application of authority labels. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] turn this auditing lens onto algorithmic architecture directly, showing X's recommender inadvertently encodes political ideology and that Community Notes structurally undermoderates polarizing content—complementing [[Renault2025-uh]]'s finding that Community Notes flags Republican misinformation at over twice the rate of Democratic misinformation, undercutting the bias narrative used to justify moving away from professional fact-checking. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], [[Farkas2026-lr]], and [[Holt2026-zq]] examine this transition from the fact-checkers' and users' vantage points respectively, showing that removal was always rare, that fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform dependency as a necessary evil, and that user "false news" reports mostly reflect political disagreement rather than veracity judgments.

## Adjacent currents: scams, hype, AI, and the future of the public square

Several papers extend this topic into emergent territory. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] reframe platform governance around the "scam economy," arguing fraud is now structural to platform business models and requires gatekeeper-focused rather than victim-focused regulation. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] examine how AI hype itself is discursively constructed within journalism and search, while [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]] look forward to AI-mediated deliberation, showing that meaningful transparency requires disclosing persuasive intent, not mere AI identity, and sketching design principles for AI-augmented "digital public squares." [[De2026-ld]] ties these threads together conceptually, coining "changecraft" to describe how platforms discursively manage their own continuous policy churn—Meta's fact-checking reversal, X's rebranding, TikTok's shifting rules—as a strategy of legitimation that this entire literature, collectively, works to render visible and accountable.
