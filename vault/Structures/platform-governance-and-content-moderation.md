# Platform Governance & Content Moderation

## Platform Governance & Content Moderation

### From open networks to walled gardens

The historical arc running through this collection is one of enclosure. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodizes platform data access itself into a "Wild West," a brief "Golden Age," and a post-Musk paywall era, arguing that the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) attempts to convert what was once platform philanthropy into a regulatory right — with mixed and fragile success. [[Bruns2026-yv]] tells a parallel story about Twitter/X's public-sphere function, tracing how its "flat, open network" affordances for crisis reporting and hashtag activism were eroded by mismanagement, commercial pressure, and eventually Musk-era "enshittification," leaving successor platforms (Mastodon, Threads, Bluesky) to inherit rather than solve its governance failures. [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Boyd2026-op]], both anniversary reflections for *Social Media + Society*, extend this genealogy into the present: Helmond reframes "platformization" as an AI-centered transcoding of the web (illustrated by Meta's retirement of the Like button and Google's space-based AI infrastructure), while boyd argues the "social" in social media has been supplanted by parasocial, influencer-dominated consumption. Together these pieces suggest that walled-gardening is not merely an access problem for researchers but a structural transformation of what platforms *are* — a claim echoed by [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]], who theorizes TikTok's shift from "networked publics" to algorithmically inferred "clustered publics" as a second-generation social form now being copied ("TikTokified") across the ecosystem. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the conceptual prehistory: the very term "platform" was always a discursive device enabling companies to claim neutrality while making substantive editorial interventions, a tension that the rest of this literature shows has only intensified.

### Meta's political content reduction and the politics of reach

A cluster of papers interrogates Meta's specific policy of down-ranking political content, treating it as a paradigm case of opaque algorithmic governance. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] provides the empirical anchor: using the Meta Content Library, it detects that Italy's rollout began roughly ten months before Meta's announced global timeline, that re-elected MPs lost 72% of average reach at trough, and — crucially — that extremist accounts *offset* the per-post penalty by posting far more frequently, ultimately gaining aggregate visibility even as mainstream politicians lost it. This finding of policy effects diverging from stated intent recurs across the topic: [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom discursively frames "problematic content" as originating from outside actors while eliding platform affordances (e.g., Groups) and hyperpartisan verified accounts from its own accounts of harm, and [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into "changecraft" — a repertoire of discursive strategies (visible aesthetics, continuity-framing, patchworking) that platforms including Meta use to manage and legitimate policy change while consolidating power. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complements the causal-policy work with a longitudinal, share-to-view analysis showing Facebook's amplification function is not static but fluctuates with governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures, directly challenging treatments of platform curation as background noise. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] turns to the endpoint of Meta's retreat from fact-checking, empirically debunking Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing (removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases) while documenting fact-checkers' skepticism toward Community Notes as a replacement. [[Farkas2026-lr]] rounds out this cluster by examining how fact-checkers themselves rhetorically defend — via differentiation and transcendence — their dependency on platform funding, revealing an emergent professional field negotiating its legitimacy under conditions of platform capture.

### Algorithmic feeds, attitudes, and the limits of causal inference

A second major thread concerns whether feed algorithms causally shape political attitudes, and how to know. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s X field experiment finds real, asymmetric, persistence-driven conservative shifts from algorithmic (vs. chronological) feeds — explicitly reconciling its results with the null findings of the 2020 Meta studies ([[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Bakshy2015-rn]]) by showing that algorithms reshape *who users follow*, an effect chronological reversion cannot undo. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the meta-scientific verdict on that whole Meta2020 collaboration: methodologically impressive but structurally incapable of producing durable, generalizable knowledge given platforms' rapid, undisclosed mutability — a critique that anticipates [[Bechmann2026-dr]]'s call to move beyond individual causal-effects paradigms toward theorizing platform "collective behavior" as democratic infrastructure. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment operationalizes that pivot, showing engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and *distorts norm perceptions* (rather than directly shifting attitudes) — a mechanism a custom "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially correct. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] narrows the causal lens further, showing that blocking political elites specifically (not political content generally) reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing the problem as one of exposure architecture. [[Brown2026-br]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] supply adjacent algorithmic audits: the former disentangles user choice from algorithmic effect to find rabbit holes but not radicalization or echo chambers on YouTube (alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt); the latter finds pre-X Twitter's algorithmic advantage for right-leaning accounts is better explained by agitation and proximity to Musk than partisanship per se. Bouchaud2026-um adds a structural finding underlying all this: X's recommender system linearly encodes users' ideological position even without being designed to, raising profiling concerns addressed only partially by post-hoc embedding correction.

### Deplatforming, bans, and downstream political effects

Several papers examine coercive interventions — bans and account removals — and their often counterintuitive political consequences. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's 2024 X ban finds a durable rightward "sorting ratchet": conservative users circumvented the ban while liberal users went silent, an effect surviving the ban's lifting. [[Simeone2025-vo]] documents a comparable "network ripple effect" from Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-fraud accounts, which collapsed the right-leaning cluster's authority structure and shifted discourse from mobilization to meta-conspiracy about the ban itself — evidence for deplatforming's efficacy as a "network intervention," in tension with [[Rieder2026-pp]]'s finding that Andrew Tate's deplatforming from YouTube merely diffused his influence into an ambient, decentralized "Tate-space" that recommendation systems continue to amplify faster than moderators can remove it. [[Renault2025-uh]] intervenes in the Community Notes debate directly, showing Republicans are flagged 2.3x more than Democrats even under a crowdsourced bridging design, undercutting the stated rationale for replacing professional fact-checking; [[Bouchaud2026-np]] pushes further, arguing bridging-based systems structurally *undermoderate* polarizing content by design, with acute electoral risks. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] situates these dynamics historically, showing platforms' election policies moved through phases of protection, civic-integrity expansion, and — since 2022 — rollback ("platform illiberalism"), largely reactive to US election cycles rather than principled commitment.

### Moderation as governance infrastructure: labor, law, and evasion

A final thread treats moderation itself as contested infrastructure — legal, technical, and human. [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers a comparative framework showing EU/US legislation converges on "security bureaucratization" (transparency, auditing, reporting) despite divergent free-circulation, patrolling, and flow-optimization regimes. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] operationalizes DSA Article 25 by mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto autonomy violations, applying the framework live to X's verification redesign. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] extend content-moderation labor critique into AI red-teaming, warning against repeating commercial moderation's outsourcing and psychological-harm patterns; [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the resulting real-world "end of trust and safety" as a profession under political and economic siege. Evasion-focused papers — [[Karo2026-dn]] on jihadist TikTok vernaculars, [[Cabbuag2024-me]] on Filipino "dogshows," [[Nangle2026-yo]] on far-right affective softening on Instagram Reels — show moderation consistently outpaced by adversarial creativity and algorithmic amplification, a pattern also visible in coordinated-inauthenticity research: [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] both argue that Meta's narrowing definition of "coordinated inauthentic behavior" leaves a wide "Zombie Internet" of manufactured attention (gambling networks, hijacked groups, propaganda) structurally indistinguishable from legitimate coordination, while [[Appel2026-qr]] finds that most exposure to deceptive Facebook networks comes from *reshares by non-network accounts* rather than the networks themselves — again complicating attribution and hence governance. Data-access papers ([[Pierri2025-hm]], [[Peters2026-mo]], [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]], [[Tonneau2025-bv]], [[Bruns2026-pn]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Jurg2025-ur]]) close the loop by showing that the DSA's promise of transparency is itself unevenly and often superficially fulfilled — through clean-room restrictions, language-coverage blind spots, or vague removal notices — meaning the empirical study of platform governance remains hostage to the same walled-garden dynamics it seeks to illuminate.
