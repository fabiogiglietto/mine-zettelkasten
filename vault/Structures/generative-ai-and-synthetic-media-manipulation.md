# Generative AI & Synthetic Media Manipulation

## Mapping the Terrain: From Isolated Artifacts to Synthetic Realities

The papers gathered here trace an arc from early alarm about individual fake images and videos toward a more systemic diagnosis of what generative AI does to collective epistemics. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most ambitious framing, arguing that the real danger is not the deepfake artifact but the emergence of "synthetic realities" — layered fabrications of content, identity, interaction, and institutions — and coining the "Generative AI Paradox": as synthetic media saturates the information environment, societies may rationally discount *all* digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] approaches the same rupture from epistemology rather than policy, showing that classical fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot accommodate AI outputs that are plausible but ungrounded, and proposing "emergent facts" as a new category requiring relational rather than binary verification. Empirically, [[Hove2026-sl49]] supplies a sobering baseline for this epistemic crisis: ordinary people are barely better than chance — indeed worse than a flat 50% guess — at telling AI-generated images from real photographs, undermining any policy response that relies on individual vigilance. [[Choi2026-bz]] complicates this further by showing that the *experience* of struggling to authenticate one deepfake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to confidence and vulnerability when encountering the next one — suggesting that repeated exposure reshapes discernment capacity itself rather than leaving it static.

## The Mechanics of AI Persuasion

A tightly linked cluster of large-scale experiments interrogates *how* conversational AI persuades, cutting against both alarmist and dismissive priors. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] decomposes the "levers" of persuasion across 19 LLMs and tens of thousands of participants, finding that post-training and prompting strategy matter far more than raw scale or personalization, and that the single best predictor of persuasiveness is information density — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims — which comes at the cost of accuracy. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the comparison by pitting AI against elite human persuaders (professional canvassers, championship debaters) and showing that no human in the sample matched AI's persuasive edge, which again traces back to fact-density/throughput rather than rapport or personalization; strikingly, constraining AI to human writing speed erases its advantage entirely. [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates this logic in real electoral contexts across three countries, finding AI persuasion driven mainly by relevant facts rather than manipulative psychological tactics, but surfacing a partisan asymmetry — AI advocating for right-leaning candidates was reliably less accurate — that echoes broader information-environment imbalances. Together these three papers converge on a clear mechanism (information throughput) and a shared worry (the persuasion–accuracy trade-off), setting up [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] as a natural policy test: it shows that merely labeling a chatbot as AI (the EU AI Act Article 50 approach) does little to blunt persuasion, whereas disclosing its *persuasive intent* cuts effectiveness roughly in half — reframing transparency debates away from source-identity disclosure and toward purpose disclosure.

## Visual Persuasion, Platform Affordances, and Problematic Promotion

A second cluster turns from text-based dialogue to image- and video-based persuasion embedded in specific platform ecologies, directly addressing the brief's interest in gambling and coordinated promotional campaigns. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] is the anchor case study: an analysis of over 2,300 images from coordinated Facebook gambling networks shows generative AI did not invent new persuasive tropes so much as intensify and recombine established ones (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, celebrity endorsement, cultural localization), while enabling an explosive, ChatGPT-era scale-up that exploits the regulatory gap between tightly-governed paid ads and loosely-governed organic content. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] provides an important empirical corrective to assumptions embedded in that and related work: in a controlled comparison, AI-generated still images did not outperform text disinformation, and low-tech decontextualized *real* video was actually the most credible and engaging format — but only for a highly salient, polarized issue (the Ukraine war) where plausible authentic footage exists, suggesting that generative AI's persuasive "unprecedented" threat may be overstated relative to older manipulation techniques. [[Bosch2024-hj]] extends the platform-specificity point to an underexamined modality — sound — documenting how TikTok's audio affordances became a distinctive vector for Ukraine-war disinformation at huge scale, while Bastos2025-ol mines a terabyte-scale troll-farm dataset to show that even profile imagery carries visual "signatures" (gender, race, framing, sensuality) that could support attribution of influence operations by country of origin. [[Luceri2025-tr]] pushes platform-specificity further still, arguing that coordinated-inauthentic-behavior detection methods built for text-centric platforms like Twitter do not transfer cleanly to TikTok's video-first structure, calling for new network-based adaptations. On the explicitly political side, [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] documents far-right use of AI-generated multimedia in the 2024 European Parliament elections on Instagram and X, while [[Crichton2026-kn]] traces a disturbing "re-generative pipeline" in which extremist imagery harvested from Telegram and X is laundered through commercial image generators that sanitize explicit markers of hate while preserving its underlying messaging — a vivid illustration of how safety filters can be circumvented rather than defeated. Finally, [[Wack2026-bt]] shows that even within one artifact, impact is not fixed: in a large Kenyan survey experiment, the persuasive damage of a political deepfake is "socially negotiated" through surrounding comments, with credulous commentary deepening harm and skeptical commentary partially restoring it — a reminder that synthetic media's effects are inseparable from participatory platform context, not just production technique.

## Coordination at Machine Scale

Two papers model what happens when generative capability is married to multi-agent autonomy rather than single-shot content production. [[Orlando2025-ul]] uses generative agent-based modeling to show that LLM agents *spontaneously* reproduce real-world coordination signatures of information operations — network densification, narrative convergence, synchronized amplification — and that merely making agents aware of their teammates' identities produces coordination nearly as strong as explicit collective deliberation. [[Schroeder2026-im]] takes this threat model to its logical extreme, arguing that LLM-plus-multi-agent architectures enable "malicious AI swarms" capable of manufacturing consensus, infiltrating communities, and poisoning future training data ("LLM grooming") — a threat qualitatively different from earlier botnets because it is adaptive, persistent, and largely autonomous. Both papers share a sober conclusion: symmetric defenses (detection, counter-swarms) face an inherent arms-race disadvantage, pushing governance proposals toward provenance infrastructure, commercial-incentive levers, and independent oversight rather than content-level moderation alone.

## Institutional and Geopolitical Dimensions

A further set of papers moves the lens from individual campaigns to the institutions and political economies that shape — and are reshaped by — generative AI manipulation. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates a strikingly indirect pathway of influence: state-coordinated media in China is memorized by commercial LLM training corpora and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence, with a cross-national audit showing the effect scales with a country's degree of media unfreedom — meaning authoritarian influence over AI need not involve direct regulatory capture of the models themselves. [[Beacken2026-zb]] complements this with comparative fieldwork across six democratically weakened states, cautioning against techno-deterministic narratives: GenAI propaganda adoption varies enormously with local political, economic, and social structure rather than following a single global trajectory. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a concrete case of elite-driven epistemic capture through infrastructure rather than content moderation: Grokipedia is shown to be heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically skewed toward unreliable and ideologically aligned sources on controversial topics, illustrating how an "alternative" AI-generated knowledge base can quietly redraw the boundaries of credibility. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] shifts from information integrity to financial harm, mapping the "scam ecosystem" of deepfake-enabled fraud and arguing — in a move that rhymes with Giglietto's regulatory-gap critique — that liability must shift from individual victims to the platforms, telecoms, and financial intermediaries that structurally enable fraud at scale. Underpinning all of this governance machinery is human labor that is rarely discussed: [[Gillespie2026-aa]] reframes AI red-teaming as a sociotechnical practice freighted with the same value judgments, outsourcing, and psychological toll that characterized early content moderation, warning that automation narratives obscure rather than resolve the labor questions at stake.

## The Culture and Vernacular of "Slop"

A final, more interpretive cluster examines generative AI's aesthetic and cultural residue — the "slop" saturating feeds — treating it as a phenomenon in its own right rather than only a vector of harm. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes AI slop and "brainrot" through Sianne Ngai's concept of the gimmick, arguing these forms work simultaneously "too little" (deskilled, labor-saving) and "too hard" (straining for attention), and that this very contradiction may harbor frictional, resistant potential against platform enshittification. [[Tang2026-gu]] grounds this theoretical move empirically, showing how Chinese creators have industrialized virality itself, transforming it from unpredictable outcome into a systematically engineered production process via benchmarking and matrix-account systems. [[Topinka2026-hb]] extends the cultural analysis into explicitly political territory, proposing that "slopaganda" works not by deceiving viewers into mistaking fakes for reality (the deepfake logic) but by affectively attuning to the moods and virality logics of platforms — AI alignment itself, trained via RLHF, becomes a machine for rhetorical attunement that intensifies existing ideological formations rather than inventing new ones. [[Li2026-zv]] closes the loop by asking what ordinary users — rather than scholars or policymakers — actually mean by "slop," finding through Reddit analysis a contested folk category defined by constitutional defects, algorithmic overreach, resistance, and ambivalent enjoyment, and arguing that current moderation regimes (keyed to deception and explicit rule violations) structurally fail to capture this user-grounded definition.

## Search, Everyday Epistemics, and Open Threads

[[Kasianenko2026-tn]] bridges the epistemics and platform-governance strands by auditing how Google Search and its AI Overviews respond to conspiratorial versus general queries, finding that moderation and AI guardrails work reasonably for well-established conspiracies (chemtrails) but fail — sometimes actively legitimizing claims by framing them as open "debates" — for newer narratives (15-minute cities), underscoring that even the most heavily instrumented information gatekeepers remain uneven and exploitable.

Read together, these papers chart a field moving from artifact-level concern (can we detect the fake?) toward systemic diagnosis (what happens to trust, labor, institutions, and culture when fabrication becomes cheap, scalable, and ambient?) — precisely the arc a graduate course on generative AI and synthetic media manipulation would need to traverse: mechanism (persuasion studies), manifestation (platform-specific visual/audio propaganda and gambling promotion), infrastructure (coordination, institutions, labor), and reception (slop culture, folk epistemics, and the limits of individual discernment).
