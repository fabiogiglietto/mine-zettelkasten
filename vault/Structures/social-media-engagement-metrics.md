# Social Media Engagement & Audience Metrics

## From Likes to Reactions: early groundwork on audience metrics

Long before "fake news" and filter bubbles dominated the conversation, researchers were already asking whether social media metrics meant what they appeared to mean. [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]]'s study of the 2011 Italian elections found that Facebook Like counts bore little statistical relationship to vote share, an early caution against naively treating platform metrics as proxies for real-world support. Around the same time, [[Giglietto2015-320951c4]] examined a full season of Italian "social TV" to show that audience "willingness to speak up" on Twitter is genre-dependent: political content elicits more guarded, information-backed, joke-framed expression than entertainment, which draws spontaneous emotional reaction. Together these studies establish a methodological throughline for the field: raw engagement counts are not transparent windows onto attitudes or behavior but artifacts of genre, context, and platform affordance—a caution that recurs, in increasingly sophisticated form, throughout the papers below.

## Reactions, sentiment, and the architecture of affective engagement

Facebook's 2016 introduction of emoji "Reactions" gave researchers a direct, low-effort signal of audience emotion, and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of 64 studies synthesizes what a near-decade of work has found: lifestyle content skews positive, sociopolitical content draws a wider and angrier spectrum, and "Angry" reactions are a reliable engagement driver for populist and far-right communicators. Country-level studies bear this out and extend it temporally. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] shows across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections that populist parties harvest disproportionate "Angry" reactions and that anger—not love—predicts sharing, while [[Larsson2026-nl]]'s decade-long Norwegian panel finds negativity rising over time, driven more by incumbency status than ideology, though its amplifying effect on shares appears to be weakening—a hint that audiences may be adapting to negativity as a genre convention rather than reacting to it. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates the normative picture further by showing that fact-checking content itself, ostensibly the genre of dispassionate correction, is suffused with emotional language that shapes engagement and sentiment toward its targets. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] pushes the emotional-engagement literature toward audience toxicity, showing that influencer-sourced misinformation generates more frequent and more homogeneous hostile reaction than identical claims from ordinary users—toxicity as a function of source credibility and parasocial bonding, not just content. And [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] demonstrates the same content-over-source logic in health news: Facebook engagement with Google-aggregated Italian health stories tracks topical "popularity" (celebrity health scandals) rather than source authority, with shares vastly outnumbering comments—evidence that reaction-type itself (share vs. comment) carries interpretive weight, a theme [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] later formalizes at platform scale.

## The Meta/Facebook 2020 election research program

A distinct and highly influential cluster emerges from Meta's data partnerships around the 2020 US election, beginning conceptually with [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s earlier finding that individual click choices suppress cross-cutting exposure more than Facebook's algorithm does. The 2020-era studies refine and complicate this picture. [[Nyhan2023-gb]] and [[Guess2023-ai]] run large field experiments—reducing like-minded exposure and removing reshares, respectively—and find striking null results on polarization and most attitudes, despite substantially altering feed composition, suggesting social media's influence may run through identity expression rather than persuasion. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] use the same data infrastructure to map a funnel of engagement, showing segregation intensifying from potential to actual exposure and misinformation concentrated almost entirely within a homogeneously conservative information corner, while [[Yang2023-cg]] argues that link-based misinformation measures—including those underlying much of this program—miss an enormous volume of image-based misinformation that is similarly right-skewed. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] closes this strand with a parallel finding to Nyhan and Guess: exposure to untrustworthy sources is concentrated but produces no detectable attitudinal shift. [[Malhotra2026-vl]] extends the program beyond exposure to relationship dynamics, finding that cross-cutting Facebook friendships are disproportionately severed around flashpoints like January 6th, and validating self-reported unfriending against real behavioral data. Across this cluster, a consistent and almost counterintuitive pattern holds: platform-level interventions reliably reshape what users see, yet rarely move the needle on attitudes—precisely the tension that [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] revisits by showing that Facebook's reach-granting mechanisms (partisan penalties, journalistic-quality rewards) are not stable background conditions but fluctuate sharply with governance interventions like 2020's "break the glass" measures, implying that the "null effects" literature may be observing outcomes already shaped by invisible platform intervention.

## Beyond Meta: feed experiments on X and Bluesky

A newer wave of field experiments tests similar questions off Facebook, often with sharper causal designs. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that switching X users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed shifts political attitudes rightward and does so asymmetrically—switching back off does not reverse the effect, because the algorithm has already changed who users follow. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] offers a complementary account of the same platform in the same period, showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage is largely explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk rather than partisanship per se, with Musk's account becoming disproportionately central to the network. [[Brady2026-ln]] takes the experimental logic further with a custom "diversified extremity" feed on Bluesky, showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, toxic content and distorts perceived social norms—reframing the harm pathway as misperception rather than direct persuasion—while a redesigned algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] isolates a specific structural lever, elite visibility, showing that blocking political elites' posts reduces exposure to low-credibility content and improves factual knowledge without affecting polarization. [[Wan2026-ai]] rounds out this cluster by examining a fully algorithmic, non-political feed (Google Discover), asking how political efficacy conditions which users opt into political content at all—underscoring that algorithmic effects are inseparable from user-level selection dispositions.

## Algorithms as active governors, not neutral conduits

Running beneath these empirical studies is a theoretical argument about what platforms are. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] reframes content moderation around "reduction"—the largely invisible demotion of content short of removal—arguing that this practice, not deletion, is where most platform power over visibility now resides. [[McNally2025-dn]] operationalizes this empirically, using time-series methods to detect lagged, differential algorithmic treatment of hard news versus other Guardian content, arguing against the "black box" framing of recommender systems. [[Bucher2017-qh]] and [[Cotter2019-js]] approach the same governance relationship from the user side: Bucher's "algorithmic imaginary" shows ordinary users developing affectively charged folk theories of Facebook's algorithm that recursively shape their behavior, while Cotter's "visibility game" documents Instagram influencers instrumentally interpreting and gaming algorithmic rules, producing a cultural, not merely technical, economy of visibility that can entrench existing privilege.

## Selective exposure, curation, and the geometry of polarization

A parallel thread interrogates the mechanisms of ideological sorting itself, independent of any single platform intervention. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] and [[Messing2014-jc]] represent an earlier, more sanguine moment, finding that direct browsing dominates news consumption and that social endorsements can override partisan source cues, diluting selective exposure. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates this with the concept of "curation bubbles," showing that domain-level partisanship measures systematically obscure story-level heterogeneity, especially for nominally moderate outlets—meaning conventional measures understate how networked curation re-sorts content along partisan lines. [[Schulz2026-ts]] pushes the argument further into perception itself, showing that online discourse looks more polarized than underlying attitudes warrant because ideologically stronger users self-select into posting, producing "false polarization" as an artifact of who speaks rather than what anyone believes.

## Measuring reach: misinformation, narratives, and audience concentration

A final cluster turns to the scale, structure, and durability of problematic content once in circulation. [[Vosoughi2018-at]], the foundational study here, shows false political news traveling farther, faster, and deeper than truth on Twitter, driven by human novelty-seeking rather than bots. [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Guess2021-ym]] narrow this to Facebook sharing behavior, finding fake-news sharing rare overall but concentrated among older, more conservative users—a pattern [[Lyons2026-ca]] replicates almost exactly in the health domain, showing low-credibility health content exposure concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded users and propagated through cross-domain low-credibility referral chains rather than social media or search. [[Goel2025-iq]] and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] argue that source-level and claim-level measures of misinformation are both insufficient: Goel shows mainstream news is routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives at roughly twice the reach of outright fake news, while Suau Martinez shows belief accumulates through repeated exposure to recurring narratives across the hybrid media ecosystem rather than discrete falsehoods—a claim [[Prochaska2025-ef]] substantiates qualitatively, showing election-fraud "deep stories" sustained collaboratively by influencers and audiences through style and allusion as much as explicit claims. [[Di_Marco2026-xu]] shifts attention from detecting such coordinated narrative work to quantifying its causal influence on diffusion, while [[Kim2026-wg]] demonstrates a starkly consequential case of targeted reach: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 were associated with meaningfully depressed turnout among their intended targets, a finding obscured by average-treatment-effect designs. [[Mosleh2024-op]] reminds the field that these dynamics vary substantially once analysis moves beyond Twitter and Facebook to the fragmented seven-platform landscape users actually inhabit.

## Incivility, influencers, and the performed self

A smaller but thematically important set of papers examines how engagement metrics interact with self-presentation and platform-specific cultures of incivility. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] supplies the foundational account of "context collapse" and the imagined audience, showing how users strategically perform authenticity for an unknowable networked public—a dynamic [[Cotter2019-js]] (above) extends into algorithmically mediated influencer labor. [[Cabbuag2024-me]] documents a platform- and culturally-specific instance of this performance turning corrosive, as Filipino Gen Z TikTok influencers normalize "legal-but-harmful" humor that blurs into cyberbullying. [[Tiggemann2020-sl44]] offers a partial counterweight, showing that "Instagram vs reality" posts can reduce body dissatisfaction by neutralizing upward appearance comparison, suggesting audience metrics and affective harm are not unidirectional. Finally, [[Choi2026-bz]] extends the engagement-and-cognition literature into the deepfake era, showing that difficulty authenticating one (deep)fake carries over, in a modality-congruent way, to shape confidence and susceptibility toward the next—a fitting coda to a topic whose throughline, from Likes-as-votes to algorithmic reach, is that metrics of engagement are never simply descriptive: they are always already shaping the audiences and beliefs they purport to measure.
