# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The shifting terrain of polarization research

The papers in this collection converge on an uneasy consensus: polarization is simultaneously real, measurable, and poorly theorized. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] names the field's central problem directly—fragmented across disciplines and skewed toward a handful of (mostly American) contexts—while [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows that even within that narrow empirical base, the leap from diagnosing polarization to prescribing depolarization is largely unmoored from evidence, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] voices the normative urgency behind this literature, calling on scientists to treat depolarization as a civic obligation, even as [[Esau2025-tf]] tries to give that impulse conceptual ballast by distinguishing *destructive* polarization from the normatively healthy disagreement democracies need, proposing deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as correctives.

A parallel strand interrogates the foundational metaphors of the field. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] argues that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" should give way to a feedback-loop framework capable of explaining why self-reinforcing dynamics rarely produce runaway radicalization. [[Guo2026-fi]] pushes further upstream, showing that the very boundary between "political" and "non-political"—on which polarization research depends—is itself a contested, relationally constructed artifact of disciplinary convention rather than a natural kind. Together these pieces suggest that much confusion in polarization scholarship stems from importing folk concepts (echo chambers, bubbles, "the political") without examining their analytic load-bearing capacity.

## Does social media cause polarization? The null-results turn

A striking cluster of large-scale, high-powered studies—mostly from the Meta/Facebook research collaborations and allied experimental work—pushes back hard against deterministic claims that social media *produces* polarization. [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], and [[Guess2023-ai]] each find that algorithmic curation and resharing shape what people see far less than individual choice does, and that even substantial experimental reductions in like-minded or reshared content fail to move measured polarization. [[Bail2018-fk]] delivers the more unsettling twist: exposure to the *opposing* side can backfire, intensifying attitudes rather than moderating them, especially among conservatives. [[Schulz2026-ts]] reframes the entire puzzle as one of perception rather than attitude—discourse looks more polarized than citizens actually are, because the already-polarized disproportionately self-select into posting. [[Messing2014-jc]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] (with its companion ideal-point methodology paper [[Barbera2015-je]]) complicate the picture further by showing that polarization is topic-contingent and that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues entirely. Read together, these papers constitute a methodological corrective to journalistic and some academic common sense: the architecture of attention matters, but mostly by amplifying pre-existing dispositions rather than manufacturing new ones.

Yet this consensus is itself contested on measurement grounds. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] finds, using population-scale Facebook data, that segregation is asymmetric and far higher than browsing-based studies suggested—especially in a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem that also hosts most misinformation. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis explains why: the magnitude (though not direction) of polarization findings is substantially an artifact of how media partisanship and polarization are operationalized, meaning the field's contradictory results may reflect methodological variance as much as substantive disagreement. [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a partial resolution by proposing a unified sharing-based ideology measure spanning elites and publics, showing that reduced electoral competition—not platform design per se—predicts more polarized elite sharing.

## Asymmetry and the radical-right thesis

A second major throughline insists that polarization is not symmetric, and that treating it as a two-sided phenomenon obscures a specifically right-wing institutional pathology. [[Benkler2018-lw]] is the foundational statement here: the American "epistemic crisis" stems from an insulated, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem lacking the self-correcting mechanisms present elsewhere in the media system. [[Bennett2018-ys]] extends this into a "disinformation order" thesis, arguing that populism-centered frameworks in communication research obscure deeper anti-democratic agendas, while [[Bennett2025-xs]] (with [[Knupfer2025-vt]]) develops an organizational account—digital "surrogate" networks and a "logic of connective faction"—explaining how hyperpartisan media and digitally networked elites pull parties toward illiberalism, using CRT-adoption among Republicans as a diagnostic case.

Empirically, this asymmetry thesis finds robust cross-national support. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] shows across 26 countries that misinformation sharing is driven specifically by *radical-right populism*—not populism or right-ideology alone—while [[Renault2025-uh]] demonstrates that even a crowdsourced, bias-resistant moderation system (Community Notes) flags Republican accounts 2.3 times more often than Democratic ones. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for the Radical Right across the 2024 European elections, and [[Darius2026-xl]] finds that populist radical-right (and, to a lesser degree, far-left populist) parties receive outsized audience support online, especially on TikTok, through emotionally charged, anti-elite communicative styles rather than sheer posting volume. [[Heft2021-ky]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] trace the infrastructural correlate of this asymmetry: transnational hyperlink and audience networks among right-wing outlets, with the U.S. (Fox News, Breitbart) functioning as a central hub radiating influence into European and Anglophone far-right media ecosystems. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows the same transnational, centrally coordinated logic operating in anti-gender movements via CitizenGO, and [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that German far-right networks take structurally distinct shapes across platforms depending on each platform's affordances.

## Affective polarization, identity, and narrative

Where the structural literature asks whether platforms sort people, a parallel psychological and interpretive literature asks what polarization *feels like* and how it is narratively constructed. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] remains the touchstone statement that affective polarization—partisan animus as social identity—is distinct from and more consequential than ideological disagreement, with effects spilling into dating, hiring, and economic behavior. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] extends this to online behavior, showing that out-party hatred, not ignorance, drives fake-news sharing. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect"—a partisan-identity-driven perception that one's own side is targeted more by misinformation—paralleling the classic hostile media effect, while [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows that mere media *narratives* about election interference, independent of their factual basis, can themselves deepen perceived and affective polarization.

A newer vein insists polarization must be studied at the level of narrative and identity-work rather than opinion alone. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reconceives disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story* rather than discrete false claims. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes this insight computationally, introducing "narrative polarisation" to show that video content on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict is sharply polarized even where audience comments converge on the surface. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] examines how "Great Reset" conspiracy communities construct a collective "we" through boundary work, reclaiming stigma and transmuting personal grievance into solidarity—explicitly rejecting platform-centric explanations in favor of relational identity formation. [[Marino2026-slef]] complements this by showing empirically that a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro Facebook community's affective engagement is far from stable, shifting dramatically around Lula's inauguration and the January 2023 coup attempt—unsettling the assumption that hyperpartisan communities are static echo chambers.

## National and transnational case studies: Brazil, Italy, Germany, and beyond

The Brazilian context is especially richly represented, forming something of a sub-corpus on populism, platform governance, and parliamentary communication. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] both examine how Brazilian parliamentarians navigate platform incentives—respectively, contesting epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID crisis and adapting communicative strategy to the "democratic cost" of virality, which rewards antagonism over substantive legislative content. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents the asymmetric partisan fallout of Brazil's 2024 X ban, showing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's political ecosystem. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] situates these dynamics within a broader critique of platform privatization and "data colonialism" disadvantaging the Global South, using Brazil's failed Fake News Bill as its central illustration. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends the connective-action framework to Russian "connective counteraction," showing how authoritarian regimes can mimic grassroots digital mobilization to suppress protest—a theme echoed in [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]]'s account of Serbia's SNS party simulating authentic grassroots support through coordinated inauthentic behavior that evades standard bot-detection heuristics, and in [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s theorization of dual-audience wartime propaganda in Russia.

The Italian case contributes both a classic study of populist news insularity—[[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows that Five Star Movement and League supporters cluster around especially insular news sources and that M5S uniquely weaponizes the Facebook attention economy to amplify favorable coverage—and more recent work on discourse and legitimation: [[Mosca2026-yh]] proposes a "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model for how Italian political actors wield fake-news accusations as negative-campaigning weapons, while [[Paci2025-ag]] demonstrates that even advanced LLMs struggle to interpret the manipulative implicatures and presuppositions pervasive in Italian political speech. German-context work, meanwhile, centers on far-right network structure ([[Kakavand2026-kt]]) and audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media ([[Zhu2026-tn]]), the latter showing that institutionalized political access for far-right parties (as in Austria) blurs the alternative/mainstream boundary, while marginalization (as in Sweden) produces isolated alternative enclaves—a reminder that "alternative media" is a relational, not fixed, category.

## Platform architecture, method, and the limits of detection

A final throughline concerns how the very tools used to study polarization shape what we find. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] argues TikTok represents a structural break from earlier "networked publics" toward algorithmically "clustered publics," a shift with consequences for depersonalization and fragmentation that several papers here implicitly presuppose (e.g., [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] on Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored visual securitization, [[Divon2026-wx]] on influencers as crisis-time brokers of state narratives on TikTok/Douyin). [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Green2025-ap]] both challenge single-platform and domain-level methodologies: the former shows engagement with partisan/low-quality news diverges across seven platforms, the latter demonstrates that "curation bubbles" at the story level are masked by domain-level audience scores, especially for nominally moderate sources. [[Lee2026-je]], [[De_Leon2025-qn]], and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] push methodological innovation further—LLM-based inference of political alignment from nonpolitical text, a "networked exposure" concept capturing how ordinary content gets weaponized through circulation, and unsupervised computational framing-detection, respectively—while [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] examine the tactical mechanics of hashtag hijacking and evidence-frame rumor construction during contested elections. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] perhaps best synthesizes this methodological self-scrutiny, showing on Reddit that polarization is not a discourse effect, a platform-design effect, or a moderation effect alone, but an irreducible "entanglement" of all three—a fitting closing image for a field whose object keeps dissolving under closer methodological inspection, and whose next task, per [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], is to test whether any of these American- and European-forged frameworks travel to the rest of the world at all.
