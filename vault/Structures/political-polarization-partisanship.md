# Political Polarization & Partisan Media Ecosystems

## The Long Arc: From Filter Bubbles to Feedback Loops

The foundational empirical claim in this literature—[[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s landmark finding that Facebook friend networks contain substantial cross-cutting ties, and that individual choice suppresses cross-cutting exposure more than the News Feed algorithm—has functioned less as a settled conclusion than as a provocation that the field has spent a decade arguing with. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers the most direct theoretical reckoning: echo chambers and filter bubbles are empirically contested but conceptually seductive metaphors, and should be replaced by a feedback-loop framework that explains why reinforcing dynamics stabilize toward equilibria rather than producing runaway radicalization. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis: much of the field's disagreement is an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent underlying reality. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this empirically at scale, running a specification-curve analysis across 504 ways of measuring media partisanship and polarization outcomes and showing that methodological choices swing coefficient magnitudes by nearly sevenfold even as direction holds steady. Together these pieces mount a sustained methodological critique: the polarization literature's contradictions are often manufactured by measurement, not by the world.

This meta-level skepticism is echoed by algorithm-audit studies that keep failing to find the dramatic effects the "rabbit hole" narrative predicts. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user choice on YouTube and finds only mild, user-driven echo chambers, strong content-stickiness ("rabbit holes"), but no radicalization pathway—though the platform exhibits a uniform conservative tilt for everyone. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X complicates the null-results consensus established by Meta's 2020 collaboration: switching users onto the algorithmic feed does shift attitudes rightward, but the mechanism is subtle and asymmetric—the algorithm changes who users choose to follow, and those follows persist even after the algorithm is switched off, explaining why prior "switch it off" designs found nothing. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment during the 2024 election reframes the causal pathway again: engagement-based feeds amplify intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distort perceived social norms about political dialogue, and a "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially repair this without denting platform enjoyment—normative misperception, not direct persuasion, is proposed as the operative mechanism. Read together with [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s finding that Brazil's ban on X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately circumvented it, this cluster suggests that platform-level interventions (algorithmic or regulatory) have real but often unpredictable, asymmetric, and persistent partisan consequences.

## Insularity, Affect, and the Architecture of Attention

A parallel strand, closer to the topic's original empirical core, tracks how partisan communities structure attention and emotion within and across platforms. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the key construct of "insularity" in the Italian 2018 election, showing that populist-favored news sources are more insular on Twitter and that insularity inversely predicts Facebook's comment-to-share ratio—insular sources get amplified via sharing, cross-partisan sources get contested via commenting. This share/comment distinction becomes a durable analytic lens. [[Marino2026-slef]] revisits it longitudinally inside a single pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network from 2021–2023, overturning the assumption of stable echo chambers: affective engagement (love versus anger, share-dominance versus comment-dominance) swings sharply around discrete political shocks like the January 2023 coup attempt, and even suggests that opposing partisans can deliberately "brigade" into a community's comment section—turning boundary permeability into a strategic act rather than an accidental leak. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] extends the affective-affordance lens comparatively across Scandinavian elections, finding that anger reliably predicts sharing while love suppresses it, and that populist parties are disproportionately effective at cultivating anger—a pattern [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of Facebook Reactions research confirms as a broad regularity across the literature. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] pushes the insularity thesis furthest, showing across sixteen years of Reddit data that cross-ideological overlap in users, sources, and cross-posting remains minimal even between discursively similar subreddits, arguing that polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than an emergent outcome of discourse alone—directly contesting any residual hope, à la Bakshy, that a platform space like r/politics functions as a genuine town square.

[[Green2025-ap]] complicates the very metrics this insularity research relies on, showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity—a nominally "moderate" outlet like the Wall Street Journal contains stories with sharply divergent partisan audiences—so that "curation bubbles" form through networked re-bundling of individual stories rather than wholesale source selection. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Eady2025-vm]] extend the measurement conversation outward: the former shows partisan/low-quality news engagement diverges meaningfully across seven platforms (undercutting Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations), while the latter builds a joint ideology-estimation model from URL-sharing behavior across media, politicians, and the public, finding that ideologically extreme politicians dominate the shared information environment and that electoral non-competitiveness predicts more polarized sharing—linking online insularity back to institutional design.

## Platforms in Transition: TikTok, Algorithmic Publics, and Transnational Media

[[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] provides the clearest conceptual pivot away from the "networked publics" paradigm that undergirds Bakshy-style analysis, arguing TikTok's item-centric, implicit-signal clustering produces "clustered publics" rather than the interpersonal, friend-based networks that defined first-generation platforms—with consequences of depersonalization, opacity, and "silosociality." This matters for the topic's throughline because it suggests the insularity/cross-cutting-exposure debate, largely theorized around Facebook and Twitter, may not transfer cleanly to a TikTokified media ecosystem, where visibility no longer depends on interpersonal ties at all. [[Groebner2026-pc]] shows a related but distinct dynamic on YouTube: far-right hyperpartisan channels across four countries share substantial, sustained commenter overlap, with the US (Fox News) as the central hub—evidence that insularity within national partisan camps coexists with vigorous transnational integration among ideologically aligned actors, an axis largely missing from single-country Facebook/Twitter studies. [[Zhu2026-tn]]'s cross-country network analysis of alternative-mainstream audience overlap in Europe adds further nuance: audience overlap is generally low, but where far-right populist parties have institutionalized political access, the alternative/mainstream boundary blurs—insularity, in other words, is not a fixed platform property but a function of a country's media system and party system.

## Narrative, Identity, and the Turn Away from Facts

A significant recent cluster reframes polarization and disinformation less as a problem of exposure or algorithmic sorting and more as one of narrative and identity construction. [[Marwick2026-qd]] argues disinformation is best understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative rather than discrete false claims—a framework extended by [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], which shows survey evidence that cumulative cross-media exposure to recurring narratives (not isolated claims) drives belief, and by [[Elfes2026-jb]], which operationalizes "narrative polarisation" via computational actantial analysis of YouTube content on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, finding that videos are sharply polarized in how they cast actors while comments paradoxically converge—suggesting narrative and audience ideology can partially decouple. [[Marwick2025-vx]] and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] push this narrative/identity turn into radicalization and conspiracy research specifically: redpilling is typically a gradual socialization process, not a sudden conversion, in which disinformation functions as "evidence" bolstering a self-image of rational skepticism; and Great Reset conspiracy communities cohere less through shared ideology than through symbolic boundary work, reclaiming stigma ("we, the conspiracy theorists") and converting personal grievance into collective identity. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows a cognate dynamic at the level of political vocabulary: "fake news" itself has become a weaponized label used by both left- and right-wing hyperpartisan outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance, further eroding shared epistemic ground. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents elites doing similar epistemic-authority work directly, with Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus COVID crisis pursuing three distinct legitimation strategies (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity-based brokerage) that map onto, and complicate, the opposition/government insularity split found elsewhere in this corpus.

This narrative-and-identity turn dovetails with an emerging institutional/elite-network literature that asks how such narratives get organizationally amplified. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how the Critical Race Theory issue was adopted disproportionately by more ideologically extreme Republicans who are also more densely tied to right-wing digital media, with hyperpartisan attention functioning as a reward mechanism that reinforces factional radicalization. [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes this into a "communication-as-organization" framework, arguing that individual-level disinformation-effects research (the dominant, well-funded paradigm) misses how digitally networked "surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal, Proud Boys) organizationally bind together the Republican Party's radicalization—explicitly building on Bennett and Segerberg's connective-action theory and directly complementing Knupfer's finding. These pieces collectively suggest that the "insularity" observed at the level of aggregate engagement data (Giglietto, Marino) has an organizational and elite-strategic substrate that content-based or attitude-based measures alone cannot capture.

## Depolarization, Measurement Reflexivity, and Open Problems

The corpus circles back, finally, to interrogate its own normative and geographic foundations. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review is damning on this front: depolarization recommendations in the literature are almost never grounded in the studies' own evidence, resting instead on unexamined commitments to deliberative-democratic ideals—a finding that should give pause to normatively-oriented pieces in this same set, including [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theoretical case for "deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening" as an antidote to destructive polarization, and the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] urging scientists toward applied depolarization work via "proximate" framing. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] names the discipline's other besetting weakness directly: polarization research is fragmented across disciplines and overwhelmingly concentrated on the US, calling for integrated, globally equitable study—a call [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] partially answer by testing polarization dynamics via survey experiments in the UK, Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland respectively, finding that even discourse *about* interference and misinformation (rather than misinformation itself) can asymmetrically inflame affective polarization, and that citizens exhibit a robust "hostile misinformation effect" mirroring the classic hostile-media-effect literature. Smaller case studies—[[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] on partisan citation of rival COVID-19 scientist camps in UK newspapers, [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] on the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag, [[Lieu2025-nl]] on which categories of climate misinformation are most polarizing—serve as domain-specific instantiations of the same insularity-and-affect dynamics traced at scale elsewhere. Methodologically emergent work such as [[Sarmiento2025-as]]'s unsupervised framing-detection pipeline, [[Zhao2025-ny]]'s account of hashtag hijacking as strategic attention capture, and [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s inquiry into whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization gesture toward where the field is heading: increasingly computational, increasingly skeptical of blunt echo-chamber metaphors, and increasingly attentive—following [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s foundational insight about context collapse and the imagined audience—to the fact that partisan media ecosystems are not simply exposure regimes but performative, identity-constituting spaces whose audiences, algorithms, and elites co-construct polarization in ways no single metric fully captures.
