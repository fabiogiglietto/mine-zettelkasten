# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## Political Polarization & Partisanship: A Structure Note

### Redefining what "polarization" means

The largest fault line running through this literature is conceptual: what, exactly, are we measuring when we say a society or discourse is "polarized"? [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] opens with the diagnosis that the field itself is fragmented across disciplines and geographically skewed toward the US, a critique that [[van-Eck2026-xg]] sharpens by showing that even the empirical study of polarization is lopsided—overwhelmingly focused on ideological rather than affective polarization, and, more damningly, that the depolarization *recommendations* scholars issue are almost never grounded in their own evidence but instead smuggle in unexamined normative commitments (typically deliberative-democratic ones). [[Hartmann2025-px]] extends this meta-critique to the specific case of echo chambers and filter bubbles, arguing that scholarly disagreement about whether these phenomena even exist stems largely from incommensurable operationalizations rather than divergent realities. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers a constructive alternative: replace the static, binary metaphor of "bubbles" with a feedback-loop framework that can explain why self-reinforcing dynamics exist without producing runaway radicalization—directly answering the empirical puzzle that motivates [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s foundational finding that individual click choices, not algorithms, are the primary suppressors of cross-cutting exposure on Facebook. [[Elfes2026-jb]] pushes the redefinition furthest, proposing "narrative polarization"—divergence in how actors are cast in stories—as a dimension partly orthogonal to opinion-based polarization, since audiences (via comments) can converge even when the content they consume is sharply polarized. Together these works insist that polarization is not one thing but a family of related-but-distinct phenomena (ideological, affective, narrative, perceived, discursive) that require correspondingly distinct measures—an insistence borne out methodologically by [[Schemer2026-mh]], who shows with a specification-curve analysis of 504 model variants that conclusions about US media-partisanship effects swing wildly in magnitude (though not direction) depending on how "partisan slant" and "polarization" are operationalized.

### Measurement artifacts and the "false polarization" problem

A second cluster interrogates whether visible online polarization is even a faithful signal of underlying attitudes. [[Oswald2025-km]] and [[Schulz2026-ts]] converge on a "production gap" thesis: because posting behavior correlates with attitude extremity, the vocal minority who post distort the visible discourse into something more polarized than the silent majority's actual views—a finding with obvious implications for any study (including many in this collection) that infers polarization from platform trace data. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates the measurement question from a different angle, showing that domain-level partisan-audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity in outlets scored as moderate; "curation bubbles" are constructed by networked re-sharing of individual stories rather than by consuming whole sources. [[Eady2025-vm]] builds a complementary joint-estimation model of media, elite, and mass ideology from shared URLs, finding that ideologically extreme politicians dominate the visible news-sharing ecosystem—a structural mechanism that helps explain why online discourse looks more polarized than the electorate. Gaisbauer2025-px (cataloged here as a methods intervention) argues explicitly that prior news-sharing research has been too outlet-level and too geographically narrow, calling for multi-level "political cartography." [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" adds a perceptual layer: citizens believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation, a bias that itself erodes trust in media and elections independent of any actual asymmetry—closely related to [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s finding that mere media *narratives* about election interference can heighten affective polarization even when the underlying interference is inert, suggesting that talk about polarization can become a polarizing act in itself.

### Platform architecture, algorithms, and affect

A substantial subset examines how platform design and affective affordances shape partisan engagement. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] provides causal field-experimental evidence that X's algorithmic feed shifts US users rightward and that this shift persists asymmetrically because algorithmic exposure changes who users choose to follow—reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies and complicating simple "filter bubble" stories. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes a parallel but distinct transformation on TikTok, arguing that "networked publics" built on explicit social ties are being supplanted by algorithmically inferred "clustered publics," with depersonalization and fragmentation as consequences—a structural argument that dovetails with [[Kristensen2025-ni]]'s question of whether alternative-platform architecture itself drives discursive polarization. Affective engagement is examined at a more granular level by [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Anwar2024-34dba628]], both showing that anger-eliciting content—especially from populist actors—disproportionately drives Facebook sharing, while love/positive reactions suppress it. [[Marino2026-slef]] extends this into a longitudinal, actor-by-time analysis of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network, showing that affective engagement is not the stable echo-chamber signature theory predicts but fluctuates dramatically around political shocks (Lula's inauguration, the January 2023 coup attempt), with in-group symbols like the Armed Forces showing the most volatile reception—directly challenging static models of hyperpartisan community stability. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's 2024 X ban shows platform-level shocks can themselves become polarizing events: conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberal users exited, producing a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's political information environment—an institutional-level echo of the individual-level dynamics [[Gauthier2026-iq]] documents.

### Insularity, cross-partisan contestation, and the Italian/Brazilian axis

The Italian and Brazilian cases anchoring this topic converge on a shared insight: partisan communities are neither fully insular nor fully permeable, but strategically differentiated in how they engage across lines. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]'s study of the 2018 Italian election is foundational here, showing that populist-aligned news sources (M5S, League) are more insular on Twitter, and—crucially—that insularity correlates with an amplification-vs-contestation dynamic on Facebook: insular sources generate more shares (echoing agreement), cross-partisan sources generate more comments (contesting or reframing). M5S alone exhibits a strategically asymmetric pattern, mobilizing shares for favorable coverage and comments to contest unfavorable coverage, suggesting a sophisticated "cyber party" capacity to manage attention. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] extends this logic to Brazil's hybrid media environment, showing how parliamentarians during the Manaus COVID crisis pursued distinct epistemic-authority strategies—opposition "derivative reporting" that borrows journalistic credibility, pro-government "direct authority" claims that sever ties with the press, and non-polarized "proximity brokerage" by local Amazonas deputies—demonstrating that polarized elite communication is not monolithic but stratified by structural position. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] (Reddit) generalizes the insularity finding beyond Twitter/Facebook, showing minimal cross-ideological overlap even between *ideologically similar* subreddits, arguing polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than simply an emergent discursive outcome—a structural claim that resonates with [[Zhu2026-tn]]'s cross-country audience-overlap analysis, which finds that the alternative/mainstream media boundary is not fixed but contingent on national media systems and the institutional access of populist actors (contrast Sweden's isolated alternative enclave with Austria's blurred boundaries).

### Elite radicalization, connective action, and transnational diffusion

A distinct thread connects partisan online dynamics to organizational and elite-level radicalization. [[Bennett2025-xs]] argues that disinformation-centric ("technocentric") explanations of democratic backsliding must be integrated with institutionalist accounts of party capture, proposing that "digital surrogate organizations" pull conservative parties toward illiberalism—a framework [[Knupfer2025-vt]] operationalizes empirically through the Critical Race Theory case, showing how a "logic of connective faction" rewards ideologically extreme Republicans with disproportionate hyperpartisan-media attention and engagement, radicalizing intra-party dynamics from within. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends connective-action theory to authoritarian counter-mobilization, showing pro-Navalny and pro-government Russian Twitter networks exhibit distinct coordination signatures (centralized vs. modular), while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] documents a related but more covert Serbian variant of coordinated inauthentic behavior that mimics grassroots support. [[Groebner2026-pc]] internationalizes the elite-media axis, showing sustained transnational audience overlap among English-language far-right YouTube channels with the US far right as central hub—complementing [[Iris2026-pg]]'s finding of systematically disproportionate radical-right media visibility across the 2024 European Parliament elections, itself a structural (not merely audience-driven) contributor to normalization.

### Narrative, identity, and the discursive construction of "truth"

Running beneath the platform- and elite-level analyses is a persistent argument that polarization is sustained less by information than by identity-affirming narrative and symbolic boundary-work. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation itself as cathartic, identity-affirming cultural narrative rather than discrete false claims, explaining why fact-checking fails to counter stories that "fit" partisan moral frames. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] shows empirically how "Great Reset" conspiracy communities construct collective identity through reclaiming stigma and affective solidarity across six platforms, cutting across ideological lines to form "diagonal" coalitions united by anti-institutionalism rather than content. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show "fake news" itself has become a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization rather than a descriptor of falsehood—used symmetrically by left and right outlets in the US and strategically amplified by Italian media—while [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces how social-justice discourse (from #StayWoke to "culture wars") is politicized both within and across platforms. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] grounds these dynamics empirically in UK science journalism, showing partisan press selectively amplifies scientists aligned with their editorial line even among mainstream (non-fringe) researchers—science itself becoming a resource for narrative-level polarization rather than a corrective to it. [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s earlier theorization of "context collapse" and the imagined audience underlies much of this: strategic self-presentation on platforms where audiences cannot be known precedes and enables the identity-performance dynamics later theorized as narrative polarization and symbolic boundary work.

### Interventions, normative stakes, and the Global South

Finally, several papers turn from diagnosis to remedy and normative stakes. [[Esau2025-tf]] and the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] both call for deliberate depolarization strategies (reciprocity, inclusive listening, proximate framing of policy), echoing [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s warning that such prescriptions must be evidence-grounded rather than merely aspirational. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] and [[Lieu2025-nl]] test this empirically in the climate domain, finding messaging effects are typically small, non-partisan-differentiated, and behaviorally inert—cautionary evidence against overconfident claims about targeted depolarizing communication. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] closes the loop by relocating these debates in the Global South, arguing that the "privatization of public discourse" by transnational platforms compounds polarization risks asymmetrically in contexts—Brazil and Latin America especially—lacking regulatory infrastructure, a framing that ties the topic's Italian and Brazilian case studies back to the broader call, voiced by [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], for a genuinely global and integrated understanding of polarization rather than one modeled chiefly on the US experience. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s long-run global data on trust in news underscores the same point: decline in trust is not universal, is contingent on regime type and media freedom, and the US—so often treated as paradigmatic in polarization research—is in fact the outlier.
