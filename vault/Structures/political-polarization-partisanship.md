# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The echo chamber debate, revisited and complicated

A substantial share of this literature continues to litigate the foundational question of digital-era polarization research: do platforms sort citizens into ideologically sealed enclaves, or is this metaphor overblown? The methodologically landmark studies—[[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Barbera2015-fw]], [[Barbera2015-je]], [[Flaxman2016-lm]], [[Messing2014-jc]], and later [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], [[Guess2023-ur]], and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]—form a cumulative, often self-correcting research program on Facebook and Twitter news exposure. Early work found meaningful cross-cutting exposure and attributed most segregation to individual choice rather than algorithms ([[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Barbera2015-fw]]), and even showed that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues ([[Messing2014-jc]]). The massive Meta-collaboration studies pushed further: reverse-chronological feeds altered engagement and content mix but left polarization and knowledge outcomes untouched ([[Guess2023-ur]]), and reducing like-minded exposure by a third produced no measurable attitudinal change ([[Nyhan2023-gb]]), while [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicated this picture by showing that segregation increases along the "funnel of engagement" and is markedly asymmetric, concentrated in a homogeneously conservative corner where most fact-checked misinformation also resides. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s field experiment delivers the starkest complication: exposing partisans to opposing views did not depolarize but produced backfire effects, especially among Republicans. [[Brown2026-br]] extends the adjudication to YouTube, formally distinguishing echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways and finding algorithmic rabbit-hole effects but no independent radicalization push. Taken together, this cluster suggests a field moving from binary "is it a bubble or not" framing toward conditional, mechanism-specific answers—a shift crystallized theoretically by [[Trilling2024-slc8]]'s proposal to replace the bubble metaphor with a typology of communicative feedback loops that can reinforce without producing runaway radicalization, and empirically reinforced by [[Dehghan2026-sy]]'s Reddit study, which finds structural "sedimentation" of polarization irreducible to discourse, platform design, or moderation alone, and by [[Schulz2026-ts]]'s cross-national demonstration that much apparent polarization is an artifact of self-selected posting rather than underlying attitude divergence. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] frames the entire enterprise's limitation: polarization research remains disciplinarily fragmented and geographically skewed toward a handful of (mostly US) contexts, a critique this very topic cluster—spanning Italy, Brazil, Scandinavia, and the EU—implicitly answers.

## Affective polarization: identity, animus, and its behavioral traces

A second strand shifts from exposure to *affect*, treating partisanship as social identity rather than ideological position. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] supplies the canonical synthesis: affective polarization is distinct from issue polarization, driven by out-party animus, and spills into non-political life. This identity lens reframes subsequent findings across the corpus. [[Malhotra2026-vl]] shows Facebook users disproportionately sever cross-cutting rather than aligned ties, with dramatic spikes around Election Day and January 6th, validating survey self-reports against actual behavior. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends the hostile-media-effect tradition into misinformation perception, finding a "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans believe their in-group is disproportionately targeted—amplified by political interest, identity strength, and right-wing orientation. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates the same animus at the root of fake-news sharing, finding partisan hatred—not ignorance or trolling—the dominant motivator, with clear asymmetry favoring Republican sharing. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows how elite fraud claims erode election confidence asymmetrically along partisan lines, with fact-checks failing to repair the damage. Several Scandinavian and Brazilian studies operationalize affect at the level of platform engagement metrics: [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Larsson2026-ro]], and [[Larsson2026-nl]] trace how "Love" and "Angry" Facebook reactions and negative sentiment drive sharing differently across parties, leadership eras, and incumbency status, while [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review synthesizes this reaction-based literature more broadly. [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates assumptions of echo-chamber stability directly within a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network, showing affective engagement is highly volatile and event-contingent rather than fixed, and Karlsnes2025-hd (filed here as Karlsson2026-hd) demonstrates experimentally that even discourse *about* election interference—rather than interference itself—can asymmetrically deepen affective polarization toward out-partisans.

## Partisan news ecosystems: measuring slant, curation, and asymmetry

A methodologically dense cluster interrogates how we measure "partisan media" in the first place. [[Benkler2018-lw]] remains the architectural reference point, arguing the US crisis is asymmetric and institutional—a radicalized right-wing media ecosystem insulated from truth-correcting norms—rather than a symmetric, technology-driven phenomenon. [[Eady2025-vm]] operationalizes ideology jointly for media, politicians, and publics via shared URLs, finding polarized elites share disproportionately more news and that electoral non-competitiveness predicts more extreme sharing. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis is a crucial methodological corrective, showing that measurement choices for both media slant and polarization outcomes substantially shift coefficient magnitude (though not direction), with audience-based scores systematically inflating estimates. [[Green2025-ap]] pushes this further by showing that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, proposing "curation bubbles" as a better description of networked re-bundling of content than the classic echo-chamber image. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] both critique the field's over-reliance on unidimensional, outlet-level, single-platform (usually Twitter) measures, calling for story-level, cross-platform, cross-national approaches. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Balluff2026-ev]] bring this to the alternative-media question specifically: audience overlap between alternative and mainstream outlets is generally low but context-dependent on media systems and populist parties' institutional access, while entity-network analysis of German coverage finds alternative media converge with mainstream media in *which* actors they reference even as they diverge in *how* they relationally organize them. [[Heft2021-ky]] extends this spatially, showing right-wing news sites build national and transnational hyperlink ecologies with the US as central hub, yet paradoxically rely most heavily on legacy media as shared reference points. [[Chadwick2015-ii]]'s hybrid media systems framework underwrites much of this cluster theoretically, insisting that older and newer media logics coevolve rather than one simply displacing the other—a frame explicitly invoked by [[Rodarte2026-dk]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]].

## Populist attention economies: Italy and Brazil as strategic cases

The Italian and Brazilian materials ground these general dynamics in specific "polarized pluralist" and hyper-presidentialist contexts. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] is foundational here: during the 2018 Italian campaign, populist-aligned news sources (especially M5S's) show higher insularity, and the Five Star Movement uniquely exploits asymmetric sharing/commenting patterns—amplifying favorable coverage while contesting unfavorable coverage—demonstrating that populist success is inseparable from strategic command of the social media attention economy. The Brazilian cluster extends this logic to crisis communication and platform governance. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis through distinct narrative strategies (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity brokerage), while [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] demonstrates the downstream "democratic cost" of these dynamics: Brazilian legislators believe (correctly, per the computational analysis) that antagonistic, news-cycle-aligned content is rewarded while substantive legislative communication is penalized. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a striking natural experiment in platform governance: Brazil's 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet," as conservative users disproportionately circumvented the ban while liberals went silent. [[Marino2026-slef]], discussed above, completes this cluster by showing the internal volatility of a major Bolsonarista Facebook community. [[Darius2026-xl]]'s EU-wide study of the 2024 European Parliament election adds a comparative populist dimension, finding populist radical-right (and some far-left populist) parties receive disproportionate audience support—particularly on TikTok—driven by communicative style and Euroscepticism rather than sheer activity volume.

## Elite misinformation, crowd moderation, and partisan asymmetry in sanctioning

A closely related set of papers asks whether misinformation-sharing and its sanctioning are symmetric across the partisan divide. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] both interrogate X's Community Notes system: Republicans are flagged roughly twice as often as Democrats among helpful notes, an asymmetry the authors argue cannot be explained by base rates or contributor imbalance, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]]'s cross-national replication shows the system's cross-ideological agreement requirement means polarizing content—especially around elections—is systematically undermoderated by design, a structural rather than incidental feature. [[Tai2026-qk]] examines how institutional role and ideology condition elected officials' own misinformation sharing, complementing [[Osmundsen2021-et]]'s individual-level account. [[Guess2020-rr]] remains an important counterweight, showing untrustworthy-site exposure in 2016 was concentrated in a narrow but deep conservative-leaning subgroup rather than broadly diffused. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes the entire misinformation paradigm, arguing successful disinformation functions as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic narrative rather than discrete false claims—a reframing that resonates with [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s "evidence-frame" analysis of 2022 election-integrity rumors, where misleading narratives arise from framing of largely accurate evidence rather than fabrication outright, and with [[Yoo2026-ev]]'s finding that "fake news" itself functions as a weaponized, content-free rhetorical label in hyperpartisan outlets. [[Lieu2025-nl]] brings this to climate misinformation specifically, finding content category (not rhetorical fallacy) drives both perceived veracity and political polarization.

## Algorithmic amplification, platform affordances, and the new publics

A cluster theorizes how platform architecture itself reshapes partisan visibility beyond the classical network-based echo chamber. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] offers the most ambitious reframing, arguing TikTok-style "clustered publics"—item-centric, opaque, behaviorally inferred—are supplanting Facebook-style "networked publics," with consequences for depersonalization and subcultural fragmentation that bear directly on polarization dynamics. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] examine affective and identity-based mechanisms of extremist normalization on Instagram and across six platforms respectively: far-right Instagram content relies on humor and positive affect for "multimodal softening," while Great Reset conspiracists construct collective identity through symbolic boundary work (reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor) that cuts across platform affordances and ideological camps. [[Divon2026-wx]] shows lifestyle influencers becoming situational political brokers of state narratives during crises in both democratic (Israel) and authoritarian (China) contexts. [[Lukito2026-il]] and [[Much2026-gu]] examine strategic cross-platform curation: right-leaning outlets differentiate content between Twitter and Truth Social, while Trump's 2024 podcast strategy targeted "gendered media spaces" rather than ideological ones, incidentally exposing young men to conservative content. [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] traces how social-justice discourse is politicized both within and synergistically across Twitter and YouTube into "culture wars" framing. [[Wan2026-ai]] returns to the individual level, showing political efficacy conditions how users select political content within algorithmic feeds like Google Discover. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines "hashtag hijacking" as a strategic attention-capture tactic involving bots and narrative substitution. Bridging the technocentric and institutionalist literatures, [[Bennett2025-xs]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] argue that the real story is organizational: digitally networked elites and "digital surrogate" media ecosystems pull parties toward illiberalism and intra-party radicalization (the Critical Race Theory case), a diagnosis that complements rather than replaces the technocentric literature above.

## Narrative, discourse, and the conceptual/normative turn

Finally, a set of papers steps back to interrogate the concepts and norms underwriting this entire field. [[Elfes2026-jb]] introduces "narrative polarisation"—divergence in how partisan groups cast actors in contested stories—using an LLM-operationalized Greimasian framework on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube discourse, finding that comments substantially de-polarize surface narratives even as deeper motifs persist. [[Hartley2006-oi]]'s much earlier theorization of a "cultural public sphere," developed through the Cronulla Beach riots, anticipates this narrative-identity turn, showing how affective, non-rational cultural-political tensions irrupt into mainstream visibility via extreme actors—a dynamic clearly echoed in [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Divon2026-wx]]. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] contribute methodological and conceptual groundwork for unsupervised framing analysis and for assessing whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offer bounded case studies—politicized scientist-citation patterns in UK COVID coverage, and counter-hashtag dissent after Charlie Hebdo—illustrating polarization dynamics in adjacent domains of science communication and crisis discourse. Most explicitly normative are [[van-Eck2026-xg]], [[Esau2025-tf]], and [[Unknown2026-sl3d]]: the systematic review finds depolarization recommendations are largely disconnected from the evidence base and instead reflect implicit deliberative-democratic ideals, a diagnosis [[Esau2025-tf]] responds to by theorizing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as explicit normative antidotes to "destructive polarization," while the Nature editorial ([[Unknown2026-sl3d]]) calls on researchers to take applied responsibility for designing and testing depolarization interventions—closing the loop between this topic's descriptive abundance and its persistent normative uncertainty.
