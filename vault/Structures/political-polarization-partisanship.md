# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The contested empirics of echo chambers and filter bubbles

The most persistent thread running through this collection is a slow-burning revision of the "echo chamber" thesis. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set an early benchmark by showing that Facebook's algorithm modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, but that users' own clicking behavior does so more — a finding that complicates any simple platform-blames-all story. Three decades of subsequent research have not settled the matter so much as multiplied its terms: [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 studies argues that disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of incompatible conceptualizations and operationalizations rather than divergent realities. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] pushes further, proposing that the whole metaphor be replaced by a feedback-loop framework capable of explaining why reinforcing dynamics exist without producing runaway radicalization — a theoretical move that dovetails with [[Green2025-ap]]'s empirical demonstration that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning "moderate" sources routinely serve highly partisan stories to segregated audiences. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] extends this skepticism to Reddit, showing that even discursively similar political subreddits share almost no users, links, or cross-posts, and proposing that polarization is "sedimented" into platform structure rather than emergent from discourse alone. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] internationalize the question, the former showing that alternative-mainstream media audience overlap is generally low but strongly conditioned by whether radical-right parties have institutionalized political access (Sweden's isolated alternative enclave versus Austria's blurred boundaries), the latter probing whether alternative platforms themselves drive discursive polarization. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] steps back to indict the field's US-centrism and disciplinary fragmentation, a critique this very topic cluster — spanning Italy, Brazil, Germany, Korea, Serbia, and Scandinavia — implicitly answers.

## Algorithms as political actors

A second cluster treats recommender systems not as neutral conduits but as active political agents. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X shows that switching users onto the algorithmic feed causally shifts attitudes rightward on several issues, with asymmetric persistence explained by algorithm-induced following of conservative accounts — reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates the "algorithm favors the right" narrative by showing that most of the apparent right-leaning boost in post-Musk Twitter is explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk himself rather than ideology per se. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding robust "rabbit holes" but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization pathway, alongside a subtle platform-wide conservative tilt. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' Left-Right position, raising privacy and profiling concerns addressed partly through a debiasing method. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment shows engagement-based feeds amplify moralized, emotional content and distort perceived social norms of political discourse — and that norm-misperception, not direct persuasion, is the likely mechanism linking algorithms to perceived partisan animosity — while a "diversified extremity" algorithm mitigates these effects without harming enjoyment. [[Ventura2026-yc]] shows how platform governance itself can polarize: Brazil's ban on X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately evaded the ban while liberals departed.

## Partisan communities, affective engagement, and insularity: Italy, Brazil, Scandinavia

A substantial empirical core documents how partisan communities actually behave on social platforms. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] shows that Italian populist-aligned news sources (Five Star Movement, League) are markedly more insular on Twitter, and that insularity predicts a share-heavy, amplification-oriented Facebook engagement style, with M5S uniquely weaponizing both shares and comments depending on valence. [[Marino2026-slef]] revisits this insularity thesis longitudinally in a Brazilian pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network, finding that affective engagement is far less stable than echo-chamber theory predicts — shifting dramatically around Lula's 2023 inauguration and the January coup attempt — and proposing that engagement depends jointly on which actor is mentioned and when, including evidence of deliberate cross-partisan "brigading." Scandinavian evidence from [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] shows populist parties (Sweden Democrats, Norway's Progress Party) systematically eliciting more "Angry" reactions and gaining more shares from negative content, a pattern synthesized at scale by [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research, which finds anger reliably driving engagement for sociopolitical and far-right content across contexts. Together these studies suggest affective engagement, not stable ideological sorting, may be the more proximate driver of visibility within partisan networks.

## Elites, parties, and the strategic supply of misinformation

Rather than treating misinformation as an undifferentiated pathology of the information environment, several papers relocate it as an elite, strategic practice. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country analysis of parliamentary tweets finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts low-factuality sharing — only their interaction, radical-right populism, does. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] complements this with a "logic of connective faction" account of how the Critical Race Theory issue let ideologically extreme US Republicans forge ties to hyper-partisan media, rewarded with disproportionate engagement and attention. [[Bennett2025-xs]] situates both findings within a broader argument that "technocentric" disinformation research must be integrated with an "institutionalist" account of digital surrogate organizations pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism — a framework explicitly extended to European contexts including the AfD and Orbán. [[Tai2026-qk]] asks whether institutional roles and ideology function as checks on elected officials' misinformation sharing, while [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians contesting journalistic gatekeeping itself during the Manaus health crisis through distinct strategies of derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and local brokerage — a vivid case of elites becoming media producers rather than merely sources.

## Narrative, rhetoric, and the weaponization of "fake news"

A parallel strand treats "misinformation" less as a factual than a rhetorical category. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows that both left- and right-leaning US alternative outlets deploy "fake news" primarily as a delegitimizing label rather than a descriptor, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance. [[Mosca2026-yh]] formalizes this insight into a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model for Italian newspapers. [[Elfes2026-jb]] proposes "narrative polarisation" as a distinct dimension beyond opinion- or interaction-based measures, using an LLM-operationalized Actantial Model on Israeli-Palestinian YouTube content to show that videos are sharply polarized while comments surprisingly converge on surface narratives even as deeper motifs remain partisan. On election integrity specifically, [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] jointly develop a sensemaking account in which misleading rumors emerge from the interaction of (often true) evidence and politically charged frames, with quote-tweet structures and "deep stories" allowing influencers to imply fraud narratives without explicit claims — a dynamic that shifted from explicit story-building in 2020 to implicit, audience-completed framing by 2022. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] extends the meaning-making lens to conspiracy culture, showing "Great Reset" communities across six platforms constructing collective identity through symbolic boundary work — reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor — rather than through platform affordances or shared ideology per se. Methodological innovations for detecting such framing at scale are contributed by [[Arora2025-tx]] (multi-modal framing analysis) and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] (unsupervised frame discovery).

## Cross-national and comparative perspectives: radical right visibility and multi-platform ecologies

Several papers push the topic beyond single-country, single-platform designs. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate media visibility for radical-right parties across five European countries in the 2024 EP elections, a structural bias present even where radical-right parties lack parliamentary representation (Ireland), undercutting claims of anti-radical-right media censorship. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring visual securitizing discourse platform-by-platform, prioritizing memetic TikTok content over their larger Facebook/X followings to target younger voters, often using non-Swedish footage to manufacture crisis. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] compares German far-right networks across five platforms, finding that affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability) produce structurally distinct networks — Twitter's loose international broadcast arena versus Facebook's hierarchical, AfD-dominated replication structure — arguing affordances are relational rather than fixed. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance directly to voters' disinformation concerns across EU member states. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] both push methodologically toward multi-platform, multi-level (story/outlet/content) analysis of partisan news circulation, arguing single-platform, single-dimension studies of political leaning have produced an incomplete picture.

## Coordinated inauthenticity and information operations beyond the West

A distinct set of papers examines manipulation and mobilization in explicitly authoritarian or hybrid regimes. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] argues that Serbia's ruling SNS party runs decentralized, mimetic coordinated inauthentic behavior that evades detection tools built for centralized bot farms. [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extends Bennett and Segerberg's connective action framework to "connective counteraction," showing pro-Navalny networks were more centralized and voluminous while pro-government counter-mobilization was more modular and preventive — evidence of astroturfed digital repression. [[Kim2026-br]] applies an explainable ML pipeline to two decades of Naver News comments, finding suspected foreign-linked trolls rely more on morally condemning rhetoric ("Condemning Korea") targeting domestic politicians across the spectrum than on overt pro-foreign messaging, with condemnation driving disproportionate engagement. [[Kim2026-wg]] returns to the 2016 US election to document geo-racially targeted voter suppression advertising, showing average-treatment-effect designs obscure severe turnout effects concentrated among targeted minorities in battleground states. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a related tactic of attention capture and narrative substitution, often bot-amplified.

## Consequences: trust, tolerance, and democratic backsliding

A cluster of Brazilian and European survey studies traces downstream democratic harms. [[Rossini2026-jn]] finds belief in Brazilian electoral misinformation predicts rising political intolerance over an election cycle, with messaging-app news use working indirectly through misinformation beliefs; [[Rossini2026-mj]] shows the mirror-image effect on institutional trust, with mainstream news bolstering and misinformation belief eroding trust in the 2022 election results, moderated by WhatsApp network composition. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a "hostile misinformation effect" — a partisan-motivated perception that one's in-group is disproportionately targeted by misinformation — replicated across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland and linked to lower media trust. [[Renault2025-uh]] shows that even crowdsourced, cross-partisan Community Notes flag Republican posts 2.3 times more often than Democratic ones, challenging claims that fact-checking bias explains partisan asymmetries in sanctioned misinformation. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] demonstrates that media narratives about election interference can themselves increase perceived societal division and affective polarization when the "wrong" party is perceived to benefit — a discourse-level harm independent of whether interference is actually effective. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] shows social bots functioning as agenda-builders that most influence campaign sentiment (especially negative tone) while leaving the press comparatively insulated, and [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrates that brief human-AI dialogues can shift voter preferences more than traditional ads, with right-leaning AI outputs systematically less accurate — connecting persuasion technology directly to partisan informational asymmetries. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offer earlier case studies of politicized science coverage and counter-hashtag dissent, respectively, illustrating how polarization dynamics recur across very different controversies.

## Measuring polarization: methods, LLMs, and metascientific critique

A growing subset of this literature is explicitly methodological, often using large language models to solve longstanding measurement problems. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text via subtle lexical and cultural cues, raising privacy concerns around micro-targeting. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes prompting-and-averaging LLMs to scale political texts across languages and document types. [[Eady2025-vm]] introduces a joint measurement model estimating the ideology of media, politicians, and the public from shared URLs alone, revealing that ideologically extreme politicians dominate the shared information ecosystem and that electoral competitiveness predicts polarized sharing. [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis of 504 operationalizations shows that measurement choices for media partisanship and polarization substantially affect estimated relationship strength (though not direction), cautioning against overreading any single study's conclusions. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] provides an early conceptual anchor for this methodological turn, arguing that big data and networked publics blur organizational boundaries and undermine traditional exposure-based methods altogether.

## Toward depolarization? Normative debates and interventions

Finally, several papers step back to interrogate the field's normative commitments. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review finds that depolarization recommendations are rarely grounded in the studies' own evidence, resting instead on implicit ideals of deliberative democracy — a critique that resonates with [[Esau2025-tf]]'s theoretical proposal of "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to destructive polarization, and with [[Unknown2026-sl3d]]'s Nature editorial calling on scientists to test proximate, personalized framings as depolarizing interventions. Read together with [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s call for a globally equitable, integrated polarization science, this cluster suggests the field is entering a self-reflexive phase: having spent a decade documenting insularity, affective engagement, elite strategy, and algorithmic amplification across Italian, Brazilian, German, Scandinavian, and other contexts, researchers are now asking not only what drives polarization, but whether — and how responsibly — anything can be done about it.
