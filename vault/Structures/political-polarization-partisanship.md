# Political Polarization and Partisanship

## The arc of inquiry

The papers filed here trace a movement from *who controls political narratives* to *how platforms and communities metabolize those narratives affectively*, and finally to *what, if anything, can be done about the resulting divisions*. Read together, they resist a single master theory of polarization, instead assembling a set of complementary lenses — elite strategy, narrative and cultural analysis, platform architecture, and measurement critique — that converge on Brazil and Italy as especially generative cases while insisting that findings from the US cannot simply be exported.

## Elites as narrators, not just sources

A cluster of papers reframes political actors as active producers of media reality rather than passive subjects of coverage. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis through three distinct postures — derivative reporting that borrows journalistic credibility, direct state-aligned claims that bypass the press, and localized brokerage narratives that sidestep polarization altogether. Its companion piece [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] extends this logic to everyday parliamentary communication, finding that platform incentives systematically punish substantive legislative content and reward antagonism ("lacração"), producing a genuine democratic cost as politicians internalize media logic. In the US context, [[Knupfer2025-vt]] formalizes a parallel dynamic as the "logic of connective faction": intra-party factions (exemplified by Republicans who adopted the Critical Race Theory issue) cultivate ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems that reward ideological extremity with engagement and attention, radicalizing the party from within. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this argument up theoretically, arguing that technocentric disinformation research and institutionalist party theory must be integrated via a "communication-as-organization" framework in which digitally networked publics become "digital surrogate organizations" pulling conservative parties toward illiberalism — with echoes in European radical-right cases. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows the discursive weapon these actors wield most often: "fake news" itself, deployed symmetrically by left and right hyperpartisan outlets to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy rather than to describe actual falsehood. [[Tai2026-qk]] adds a US-focused elite-behavior study of how institutional role and ideology condition officials' own misinformation-sharing.

## Brazil as a laboratory of democratic strain

Brazil recurs as the topic's most intensively studied non-US case, allowing the collection to test whether "misinformation causes polarization" claims travel. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a pair built on the same three-wave panel around the 2022 election: belief in electoral fraud misinformation predicts rising intolerance and declining trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) operating as an indirect pathway, while mainstream news use acts as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters. [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates the "stable echo chamber" assumption at the community level, showing that affective engagement within a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network was highly volatile — shifting from love- to anger-dominated register and from sharing to commenting around Lula's 2023 inauguration and the January coup attempt — suggesting hyperpartisan communities are contested, event-driven spaces rather than sealed enclaves. [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents a structural shock to this ecosystem: Brazil's 2024 ban of X produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" as conservative users disproportionately evaded the ban while liberals went silent, showing how state intervention against misinformation can itself deepen partisan asymmetry. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] zooms out to the regulatory failure behind these dynamics, arguing that platform "privatization of public discourse" and data colonialism disproportionately disable Global South states like Brazil from correcting these ecosystems, using the defeated Fake News Bill (PL 2630) as evidence.

## Disinformation reconceived as narrative, not fact

A second throughline reconceptualizes disinformation itself. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest theoretical move, arguing disinformation should be understood as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative* — with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes — rather than discrete false claims, which explains why domain-level fact-checking fails to contain it. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this narrative view in a US survey, showing cumulative exposure to recurring disinformation narratives (rather than single claims) drives belief. [[Elfes2026-jb]] pushes the narrative approach into computational territory, using an LLM-operationalized Actantial Model to show that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict propagate sharply polarized narratives about victims and perpetrators, even as user comments paradoxically converge — with deeper "narrative motifs" still encoding partisan difference beneath surface harmony. [[Starbird2025-jj]] offers a complementary "evidence-frame" framework showing that election-integrity rumors on Twitter emerge from partisan framing operations (adding, echoing, escalating) layered onto often-accurate evidence, with influential accounts often supplying evidence and letting partisan audiences supply the frame. [[Zhao2025-ny]] and [[De_Leon2025-qn]] extend the same logic to tactics and objects of exposure: hashtag hijacking as a strategic contest over attention and framing (amplified by bots), and "networked exposure" as a corrective concept capturing how ordinary content (news, Wikipedia) becomes politically weaponized simply by its circulation within extremist networks. At the more classically empirical end, [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] examines how journalistic strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs, while [[Lieu2025-nl]] experimentally isolates *which* categories of climate misinformation (solution-attacking claims) are most polarizing regardless of the underlying logical fallacy — a useful bridge to intervention research. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] look at the perception side of the same coin: Community Notes data show Republicans are flagged for misinformation at over twice the rate of Democrats even under crowdsourced, bias-resistant moderation, while a three-country panel finds a robust "hostile misinformation effect" whereby partisans of all stripes believe their own side is disproportionately targeted by misinformation — a self-reinforcing perceptual asymmetry that itself corrodes trust.

## Platforms, algorithms, and amplification

Several papers isolate the platform layer as an independent causal force. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X is the collection's cleanest causal evidence that an algorithmic feed (versus chronological) shifts attitudes rightward by reshaping *who users follow*, not just what they see, with asymmetric and persistent effects. [[Kristensen2025-ni]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] extend the platform-comparative lens, the former asking whether alternative platforms drive discursive polarization and the latter documenting that engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges meaningfully across seven platforms, cautioning against Twitter/Facebook-centric generalizations. [[Eady2025-vm]] contributes a methodological infrastructure for this comparative work, jointly estimating ideology for media, politicians, and the public from shared URLs, and finding that electorally uncompetitive districts breed more polarized elite sharing — linking platform dynamics back to institutional design. [[Groebner2026-pc]] shows algorithmic and audience dynamics operating transnationally: English-language far-right hyperpartisan channels (Fox, Sky News Australia, Rebel News, GB News) share a substantially overlapping commenter base anchored by US content, evidencing a genuinely transnational far-right public. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]] extend the platform-affect argument to Instagram and the Twitter/YouTube pairing respectively: far-right normalization proceeds through positive affect, humor, and algorithmic proximity rather than hostility, while social-justice discourse (from #StayWoke to "culture wars") is politicized both within and across platforms synergistically.

## Contesting the echo-chamber paradigm

A distinct, more skeptical vein interrogates the foundational metaphors of the field. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] remains the touchstone null/limited-effect finding — Facebook's algorithm modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, but users' own clicking behavior reduces it more. [[Hartmann2025-px]] systematizes 129 studies to show that the field's apparent disagreements about whether echo chambers exist are substantially artifacts of divergent conceptualization and measurement rather than divergent reality — a conclusion sharpened methodologically by [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve analysis showing that measurement choices for media slant and polarization change coefficient magnitude (though not direction) dramatically, and by [[Gaisbauer2025-by]]'s call for multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartography beyond crude domain-level partisanship scores. [[Green2025-ap]] operationalizes exactly this critique empirically, showing that "curation bubbles" emerge at the *story* level through networked re-bundling, such that source-level partisanship scores mask enormous within-domain heterogeneity — especially for nominally moderate outlets. [[Dehghan2026-sy]] complicates the picture further at the platform-structural level: cross-ideological interaction on political Reddit is minimal even between discursively similar subreddits, suggesting polarization is "sedimented" as a precondition of platform architecture and moderation rather than purely a discursive outcome — a materialist counterpoint to purely narrative accounts. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] offers the collection's most ambitious conceptual reset, proposing that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" metaphors be replaced by a feedback-loop framework that explains why self-reinforcing dynamics don't inevitably produce runaway radicalization. [[Schulz2026-ts]] delivers a related but distinct diagnosis: much apparent online polarization is "false polarization" produced by the self-selection of ideologically extreme users into posting, distorting the visible opinion landscape relative to underlying attitudes. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] contributes a methodological pipeline (unsupervised, unsupervised framing detection) designed to surface exactly the latent framing dynamics these debates presuppose.

## Affective engagement and everyday polarized communication

Beneath these structural debates sits a body of work on the affective texture of partisan engagement. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review of Facebook Reactions research establishes the empirical baseline: anger reliably drives engagement with political and especially populist/far-right content, while lifestyle content skews positive — a mechanism visible concretely in [[Larsson2026-ro]]'s decade-long study of Norway's Progress Party, where increasing negativity under a new leader tracks with sharply higher shares and comments (though not likes, which track positivity). [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] extends this logic into the Italian "polarized pluralist" context, showing that populist parties' supporters (especially M5S) cultivate more insular news ecosystems and that M5S uniquely mobilizes asymmetric sharing/commenting patterns to amplify favorable and contest unfavorable coverage — a finding that resonates with [[Marino2026-slef]]'s Brazilian instability findings and [[Nangle2026-yo]]'s far-right Instagram softening strategies. [[Esau2025-tf]] steps back to theorize what these affective dynamics threaten, introducing "destructive polarization" and proposing deliberative reciprocity and inclusive listening as normative correctives.

## Comparative reach, Italian cases, and the limits of generalization

The Italian material anchors the topic's comparative ambitions beyond Brazil and the US. [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] examines the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag as an early case of dissenting discursive strategy within a moment of enforced consensus, while [[Paci2025-ag]] tests whether LLMs can even parse the implicit, presupposition-laden pragmatics characteristic of Italian political speech — finding current models fall well short of expert human interpretation, a sobering note for any computational polarization research relying on automated text analysis (a concern equally relevant to [[Elfes2026-jb]]'s LLM-based narrative coding). [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] supplies a UK case showing that political polarization extends even into which *scientists* newspapers cite on COVID-19, with left- and right-leaning outlets and scientific networks forming separate bubbles. [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] push the comparative frame further: audience overlap between alternative and mainstream media across six European countries is generally low but strongly conditioned by whether far-right populists have institutionalized political access, while a UK survey experiment shows that mere media *narratives* about election interference — independent of whether interference is real or effective — can themselves raise perceived division and affective polarization toward opponents, a self-fulfilling-discourse effect that rhymes with [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s hostile misinformation effect. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes explicit the meta-critique underlying all this comparative work: polarization scholarship remains disciplinarily fragmented and geographically skewed toward the US, a call this very topic collection partially answers by foregrounding Brazil and Italy.

## From diagnosis to intervention

A final small cluster asks what, if anything, follows normatively and practically. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review delivers a bracing verdict: depolarization recommendations in the literature are largely disconnected from the empirical evidence generated by the same studies, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democratic ideals — a caution that should temper enthusiasm elsewhere in this collection for interventionist claims. [[Dubey2026-bl]] tests one such intervention directly, finding that "balanced news chatbots" are, counterintuitively, trusted *more* by high-conspiracy-belief users than low-belief users, complicating assumptions about who resists cross-cutting exposure. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] closes the loop with a call to action, urging scientists to treat depolarization as a civic responsibility while acknowledging, consistent with [[van-Eck2026-xg]], that the evidence base for effective interventions remains embryonic. Together, this final group suggests that the topic's overall arc — from elite narrative contests, through platform architecture and affective communities, to conceptual critique of the echo-chamber paradigm — ends not in consensus about solutions but in a disciplined humility about how far current evidence can support them.
