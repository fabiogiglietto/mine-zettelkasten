# Political Polarization & Partisanship

## The contested foundations: echo chambers, filter bubbles, and measurement disputes

Much of this literature begins by relitigating a foundational empirical claim: that social media sorts citizens into ideologically closed enclaves. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set an early benchmark, showing that Facebook's algorithm modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure but that individual clicking behavior does more to narrow diets than the platform itself — a finding that recurs as a touchstone across the corpus. [[Hartmann2025-px]] synthesizes 129 studies and concludes that disagreement about whether echo chambers exist is substantially a methodological artifact: conceptualization and operationalization choices, not just empirical reality, drive divergent conclusions. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] pushes further, arguing the entire metaphor should be retired in favor of a feedback-loop framework that explains why reinforcing dynamics rarely produce runaway radicalization. This methodological skepticism is echoed empirically: [[Brown2026-br]] finds YouTube's algorithm produces strong content "rabbit holes" but no radicalization pathway or independent echo-chamber effect, [[Dehghan2026-sy]] shows Reddit's political subreddits exhibit a "sedimentation" of polarization irreducible to discourse or platform alone, and [[Green2025-ap]] demonstrates that domain-level partisan-audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning conventional source-based echo-chamber measures may simply be wrong. [[Schemer2026-mh]] makes this measurement problem explicit for polarization outcomes themselves, showing across 504 specifications that operationalizing media partisanship and polarization differently changes coefficient magnitude, though rarely direction. [[Oswald2025-km]] and Gaisbauer2025-px-adjacent [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] add that the visible discourse researchers analyze is itself a skewed "tip of the iceberg," produced by a small vocal minority — a warning that recurs whenever this body of work draws inferences about "public opinion" from platform traces.

## Platform infrastructure and algorithmic causation

A second cluster asks whether platform design causally drives polarization, moving past the correlational echo-chamber debate toward experimental and quasi-experimental designs. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching users onto the algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward via a persistence mechanism — users follow new conservative accounts that outlast the intervention — reconciling earlier null results from the 2020 Meta studies. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky registered report similarly shows engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup, moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms, while a "diversified extremity" algorithm redesign can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment. [[Allen2025-ot]] contextualizes Piccardi et al.'s platform-independent browser-extension method as a necessary workaround given closing API access, while [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a sharp meta-critique of the entire Meta2020 partnership, arguing that one-off platform experiments lack "temporal validity" and that only proactive regulation—not reactive science—can keep pace with platforms. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] extends this into a call to reorient the field around platform *collective* behavior as democratic infrastructure under the EU's DSA, rather than isolated causal effects. Complementing these algorithmic studies, [[Ventura2026-yc]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] show that platform-level interventions — a national ban, targeted suppression advertising — have asymmetric partisan consequences, while [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] probe the opacity of ad-delivery and moderation algorithms (Community Notes) respectively, the latter arguing that bridging-based moderation structurally undermoderates polarizing content with electoral risks — a claim empirically reinforced by [[Renault2025-uh]]'s finding of a 2.3:1 partisan asymmetry in flagged misinformation.

## Elites, parties, and the strategic production of division

A third strand relocates polarization's engine from audiences to elites. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] introduces the "logic of connective faction" to show how Republican factions build ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems, using the Critical Race Theory episode to demonstrate that connectivity clusters among the most extreme actors and is rewarded with engagement. [[Bennett2025-xs]] generalizes this into a broader institutionalist-technocentric synthesis, arguing that "digital surrogate organizations" (QAnon, Stop the Steal) pull parties toward illiberalism in ways that individual-level disinformation research misses entirely. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] provides cross-national confirmation at scale: across 26 countries, neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts elite misinformation sharing, but their interaction—radical-right populism—does. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this by showing institutional role and ideology function as "checks" on officials' misinformation sharing, while [[Marino2026-slef]] and the Brazilian pair [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] show empirically that hyperpartisan communities and parliamentary narrators are far more volatile, event-driven, and strategically self-aware than the static echo-chamber model implies — Bolsonarist networks shift affective registers around coups and inaugurations, and Brazilian deputies pursue distinct epistemic-authority strategies (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, brokerage) during crisis. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] extend the elite-capture lens to media systems themselves, documenting "journalism for sale" dynamics in Austria and disproportionate radical-right visibility across the 2024 European elections, while [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance directly to voters' disinformation concerns.

## Disinformation reconceived as narrative and identity work

A recurring theoretical move across this topic is to reject "fake news" as discrete false claims in favor of narrative, cultural, and identity-based framings. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] lays early groundwork, using second-order cybernetics to argue that propagation dynamics—not creator intent—should anchor analysis. [[Marwick2026-qd]] crystallizes this into a full theory of disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative*, tracing how catchphrases and visual shorthand carry entire stories across genres. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], and [[Starbird2025-jj]] apply cognate frameworks to U.S. election denial, showing that meaning accrues through "deep stories," evidence-frame interactions, and collective sensemaking rather than isolated false facts—with [[Prochaska2025-ef]] documenting a shift between 2020 and 2022 from explicit fraud narration toward implicit, audience-completed allusion. [[Elfes2026-jb]] operationalizes a parallel "narrative polarisation" concept computationally via Greimas's actantial model, finding that YouTube videos on the Israeli-Palestinian conflict are sharply polarized while comments converge on the surface even as deeper narrative motifs remain partisan. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] takes the identity angle furthest, showing "Great Reset" conspiracy communities cohere less through ideological content than through symbolic boundary work—reclaiming the "conspiracy theorist" label and converting personal grievance into collective solidarity. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows a related weaponization dynamic: hyperpartisan outlets deploy the term "fake news" itself as a delegitimizing label rather than a descriptive category, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] rounds out this cluster by asking how journalistic strategies themselves can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs.

## Emotion, affect, and engagement mechanics

Several papers isolate the specific affective mechanics that translate content into engagement and, potentially, division. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] and [[Larsson2026-ro]] both find that anger-eliciting, negative content drives shares and comments more than positive content across Scandinavian and Norwegian populist Facebook pages, while "likes" follow a distinct, more positive logic—a pattern generalized in the systematic review [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]. [[Nangle2026-yo]] complicates the assumption that far-right content must be hostile to spread, showing Instagram far-right ecologies instead deploy positive affect, humor, and memetic softening to normalize extremism. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] finds a similar affordance-driven but ideologically symmetric pattern on TikTok around immigration discourse, where pro- and anti-immigration users deploy the same platform vernacular. [[Esau2025-tf]] theorizes normatively what these empirical patterns imply, proposing "deliberative reciprocity" and "inclusive listening" as antidotes to what it terms destructive polarization, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" shows that partisans' *perception* of being targeted by misinformation is itself identity-driven and consequential for media trust, closing the loop between affective polarization and misinformation belief.

## AI and LLMs: new instruments, new political actors

A fast-growing subset examines large language models both as measurement tools and as political agents in their own right. Methodologically, [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Lai2024-to]] extend ideology-scaling techniques using LLM prompting and Reddit-sharing networks respectively, while [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Sarmiento2025-as]] push automated framing analysis beyond fixed categories into multi-modal and unsupervised territory. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates LLMs can infer political alignment even from nonpolitical text, raising the privacy and microtargeting stakes explored earlier by [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]. On persuasion, [[Lin2025-xp]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] show AI dialogue can out-persuade both ordinary humans and expert debaters/canvassers across multiple national elections, largely through information throughput rather than manipulation—though [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows this persuasive power collapses by roughly a quarter once users merely believe the model is politically biased against them, and pro-right-leaning AI advocacy is measurably less accurate than pro-left advocacy in [[Lin2025-xp]]. [[Dubey2026-bl]] finds counterintuitively that higher-conspiracy-belief users trust "balanced" chatbots *more* than low-conspiracy users, complicating assumptions about reactance. Sounding a cautionary note against this wave of adoption, [[Balluff2026-if]] catalogs the reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs of unreflective LLM use in communication research itself.

## Depolarization: evidence, norms, and unresolved tensions

Running beneath all of this is the normative question of what, if anything, should be done. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s systematic review is the field's most direct reckoning: depolarization recommendations are overwhelmingly untethered from the evidence base scholars themselves produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democracy ideals. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] voices the applied counterpart—a call for scientists to test proximate, personalized framings as depolarizing tools—while [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy offers a sobering empirical check: even well-cited persuasive strategies move attitudes only marginally and rarely change costly behavior, with little partisan heterogeneity. Karlsnes2026-hd (Karlsson2026-hd) and [[Lieu2025-nl]] extend this skepticism to interference narratives and fallacy-based misinformation respectively, showing that *how* an issue is framed—by beneficiary party, or by content category rather than logical structure—matters more than blunt exposure. Read together with [[Budak2024-ef]]'s argument that public discourse about online harms has drifted from what the evidence actually shows, this cluster suggests the field's central unresolved task is less discovering new mechanisms of division than disciplining its own inferential leap from measurement to prescription—an arc traceable from [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s early empirical modesty through [[Marwick2011-sl02]]'s account of audience imagination and context collapse, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]'s early hybrid-media case study, and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]'s demonstrations that politicized science and dissenting hashtags long predate the platform-algorithm debates now dominating the literature, up to today's LLM-mediated frontier and its still-unsettled implications for democratic discourse, addressed comparatively by [[Zhu2026-tn]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Kristensen2025-ni]] across national and platform contexts.
