# Generative AI and Media

## The Sociotechnical Terrain of Generative AI in Media

Generative AI has arrived in media not as a discrete technology but as a systemic reconfiguration of production, distribution, and epistemic authority. The papers filed here approach this transformation from complementary angles — labour, discourse, persuasion, disinformation, governance — and together they trace an argument about how synthetic media is remaking public communication.

### Imaginaries, Hype, and the Discursive Construction of AI

A first cluster maps how AI is *narrated* into being. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs in global news coverage are widespread but not monolithic, varying with editorial style and often serving critique as well as hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] extend this comparatively, revealing that Global North outlets skew toward dystopian, risk-oriented framings while Global South and Asian outlets emphasize opportunity and development. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes such coverage as *AI hype* — a systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources — and [[Stanusch2026-ec]] shows how industry actors deploy "premediation" and "preclusion" during controversies like the Altman firing to occupy the future discursively. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by locating Silicon Valley's "quiet futuring" in physical spaces and pioneer communities, while [[Richter2026-bt]] traces longer stakeholder trajectories showing US discourse individualising around tech influencers versus German institutionalisation. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] pushes the critique furthest, arguing that most AI critiques function as "decoys" distracting from the political economy of the Project of AI. Together these papers frame AI discourse itself as an object of contested political work.

### The Persuasion Question

A second, tightly-linked cluster asks whether generative AI actually *changes minds*. [[Costello2024-bg]] launched this line by showing durable, ~20% reductions in conspiracy belief through tailored GPT-4 dialogues. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] then dissected the mechanism: information density (fact-checkable claims per conversation) explains most of the persuasive effect, with a troubling accuracy trade-off. [[Lin2025-xp]] extends this across three national elections, finding effects that dwarf traditional political ads and — importantly — a systematic right-leaning inaccuracy asymmetry across models. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the stakes further: AI now out-persuades even elite competitive debaters and professional canvassers, again mediated by throughput. But [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] identifies a crucial constraint: perceived neutrality is itself a lever — brief partisan bias warnings cut persuasion by roughly 25%. This body of work converges on a picture of AI persuasion as real, mechanistically driven by fact-density rather than manipulation, but politically contingent.

### Disinformation, Synthetic Realities, and Coordinated Harm

Where persuasion research examines direct dialogue, another cluster examines synthetic content circulating through media systems. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most ambitious framing with the concept of "synthetic realities" — coherent stacks of fabricated content, identity, interaction, and institutions — and the Generative AI Paradox: as fakes proliferate, rational actors discount all digital evidence. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] provides an important corrective by showing that AI-generated images do not universally outperform textual or decontextualised video disinformation, though [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies subtler modality-congruent carryover effects on truth-discernment confidence. [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Orlando2025-ul]] extend this to *coordination*: malicious AI swarms and networked LLM agents can produce emergent coordinated behaviour with only minimal shared awareness, threatening democratic epistemics. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] grounds these concerns empirically, documenting a 13,242% post-volume increase in coordinated gambling promotion after ChatGPT, with generative AI intensifying — not replacing — established persuasion drivers. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] then shifts the frame to regulation, arguing that deepfake financial fraud requires shifting liability from victims to gatekeepers. [[Waight2026-ts]] adds a geopolitical dimension: state-controlled media is already laundered into commercial LLM outputs, meaning the disinformation problem operates through training data itself.

### The Post-Social Media Environment

[[Tornberg2026-lc]] provides a synthesising frame: the "social media" paradigm is dissolving into three post-social formations — algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, and AI-mediated communication. [[Baym2026-tr]] reads this trajectory as the intensification of threats identified a decade ago, with generative AI now extracting user data at unprecedented scale. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] documents one concrete instance — Google's AI Overviews reconfiguring search from indexical gatekeeping to generative epistemic authority — while [[Triedman2025-uy]] shows how Grokipedia embodies the same shift toward AI-mediated knowledge, but tilted toward lower-quality and ideologically aligned sources. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] gives this an epistemological grounding through the concept of "emergent facts": AI outputs that are plausible but lack the ontological referents assumed by traditional fact-checking.

### Journalism, Labour, and Public Perception

Media production itself is being reshaped. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds that audiences cannot distinguish AI-generated from human-written news on quality, but disclosure produces short-term curiosity engagement. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] examine red-teaming as an emerging sociotechnical labour practice replicating patterns from content moderation. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] surfaces workers' own perspectives, showing displacement concerns concentrated where AI exposure is highest. Public opinion research from [[Gottfried2026-ww]] and [[Fattorini2026-bo]] documents "critical ambivalence": rising adoption paired with skepticism, negative sentiment, and demands for regulation. [[Suk2026-ai]] provides a conceptual scaffold for studying trust across these levels.

### Scholarly Practice and Methodological Reflexivity

Finally, several papers turn the lens on scholarship itself. [[Balluff2026-if]] warns against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research, emphasising trade-offs in reproducibility, bias, and environmental cost. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] reframes chatbot performance through Luhmannian systems theory: LLMs generate without understanding, parasitising human repair work — an argument that undercuts anthropomorphic framings across many of the papers above. [[Manovich2026-ih]] gestures toward the broader claim that generative AI constitutes "a medium that thinks," raising the question of what media cognition becomes when the medium itself produces meaning.

### The Arc

Read together, this collection traces a coherent line of inquiry: from how AI is *imagined* and *narrated*, through how it *persuades* and *deceives*, to how it *restructures* the media environments in which publics deliberate — and finally to how scholarship must adapt its methods, its concepts of trust and factuality, and its political-economic gaze to remain adequate to its object. The recurring tension throughout is between the technology's genuine capacities (persuasion via fact-density, industrial-scale coordination, synthetic realism) and the discursive inflation that both hypes and obscures them. The most productive papers refuse both alarmism and dismissal, insisting that generative AI's media significance lies less in any single artefact than in the sociotechnical infrastructures — training data, platform governance, labour arrangements, epistemic norms — through which it is embedded.
