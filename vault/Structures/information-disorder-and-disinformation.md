# Information Disorder & Disinformation

## The shape of the field: from "false facts" to narrative, source, and system

The corpus assembled here is defined less by a single object of study than by a slow-motion argument about what disinformation *is*. A recurring move, visible across nearly a third of these papers, is the rejection of the "discrete false claim" as the right unit of analysis. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] opens this line by arguing, via second-order cybernetics, that fake news is better understood through the chain of propagator judgments than through creator intent; truth-value is observer-dependent, and cascades are emergent rather than authored. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes this further into cultural theory, reframing disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *narrative*—stories with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes that travel through memes, sketch comedy, and Kickstarter campaigns, not just news articles. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical scaffolding this move needs, arguing that "narrative" has been used naively in the field and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological toolkit (Ricoeur, Phelan) for assessing content that is referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false. This narrative turn is operationalized empirically in several places: [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] show, through CSCW-style analysis of election-integrity tweets, that misleading rumors emerge from the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan *frames* and "deep stories," with influencers often supplying evidence and letting audiences supply the frame. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Waight2025-al]] extend the narrative framework to measurement, the latter offering an LLM-based method for quantifying "narrative similarity" across languages and demonstrating that lexical-overlap methods miss most of the diffusion of Russian "biolabs" narratives into mainstream U.S. outlets. [[Goel2025-iq]] delivers perhaps the sharpest empirical payoff of this reorientation: mainstream, factually accurate articles are disproportionately co-shared with fake news specifically because they contain reusable misleading narrative structures—meaning source-reliability blocklists systematically undercount the real scope of the problem. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from inside a fact-checking organization (Maldita.es) during the 2024 Valencia floods, translates this narrative-centrism into practice, proposing "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" as necessary supplements to claim-by-claim fact-checking.

## Rhetorical weaponization: "fake news" as a political tool

A second cluster treats "fake news" and "disinformation" not as a phenomenon to be corrected but as a *label wielded* in political conflict. [[Yoo2026-ev]] shows that both left- and right-wing U.S. alternative outlets deploy "fake news" almost entirely as a delegitimizing weapon rather than a description of fabricated content, reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus and deviance. [[Mosca2026-yh]] generalizes this into the "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model for Italian newspapers, arguing fake-news accusations belong to the study of negative campaigning. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] pivot the lens toward the institutions caught in the crossfire: fact-checkers defending platform partnerships via rhetorical apologia, and fact-checkers navigating Zuckerberg's post-2025 "censorship" framing by showing that removal is rare and that Community Notes is viewed with professional skepticism. Renault2026—rather, [[Renault2025-uh]]—complicates the "biased fact-checker" narrative empirically, showing that Republicans are flagged 2.3x more than Democrats even under crowdsourced Community Notes, undermining the rationale for abandoning professional verification. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] closes the loop by identifying a "hostile misinformation effect": citizens perceive their own political side as more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, a bias amplified by partisanship and right-wing orientation, suggesting that *perceptions* of disinformation are themselves a politically patterned phenomenon deserving study alongside disinformation itself.

## Elite and party-level drivers: who spreads it, and why

Several papers push the field toward elite-centered, comparative political explanations rather than audience psychology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]], analyzing 32 million tweets from parliamentarians in 26 countries, finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation-sharing—only their interaction, radical-right populism, does, tying the phenomenon to institutional legitimacy crises and clickbait media symbiosis rather than a generalized "post-truth" condition. [[Tai2026-qk]] complements this with a U.S.-focused account of institutional and ideological checks on elected officials' sharing behavior. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a parallel, and possibly reinforcing, phenomenon at the level of *media attention* rather than deliberate falsehood: European news coverage of the 2024 EP elections disproportionately amplified Radical Right actors relative to their electoral strength, a structural pattern independent of outlet orientation. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] asks the reverse question—whether far-right electoral performance itself shapes voters' disinformation concerns—linking party systems and public perception. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] and [[Gaw2025-ru]] descend from party-level dynamics to the organizational infrastructure of manipulation: the former's OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg IRA reframes troll farms as ordinary, precarious media-labor organizations embedded in national job markets rather than exotic Kremlin cells, while the latter reconceives Philippine influence operations as a form of political brokerage mediating between clients, platforms, and voters—both papers insisting that disinformation supply chains are best understood organizationally and economically, not just rhetorically.

## Platform ecologies and measurement: where and how much

A substantial empirical seam concerns the platform-specific texture of circulation and the sheer difficulty of measuring it well. The vera.ai/PROMPT-adjacent monitoring effort [[Vincent_undated-re]] tracks rising TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence and a persistent "low-credibility interaction advantage" across major EU platforms, while [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s Facebook methodology to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in election years but stable exposure, concentrated overwhelmingly among older users. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] scales this to nearly a billion reshared Facebook posts during the U.S. 2020 election, isolating misinformation's distinctive diffusion signature. [[Scalco2026-bd]] innovates methodologically by operationalizing "information voids"—demand-supply imbalances between social platforms and Wikipedia/Google Trends—showing that voids around vaccine milestones coincide with lower-credibility content, giving Golebiewski and boyd's concept empirical teeth. [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] and [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] turn to coordination detection itself, the former proposing real-time bot detection on Bluesky, the latter mapping coordinated pro-government amplification on Russian/Belarusian Telegram. Platform-specific affordances are foregrounded by [[Bosch2024-hj]] (sound as a distinctive TikTok propaganda technique around the Ukraine invasion), [[Copland2025-em]] (Sky News Australia's dual Facebook/YouTube strategy bridging Australian and U.S. right-wing ecosystems as "network propaganda"), and [[Righetti2025-slf9]] (CitizenGO's centrally coordinated multilingual anti-gender network functioning as transnational "symbolic glue"). [[Rothut2026-or]] similarly maps how the Querdenken protest movement became embedded, via Telegram outlinking, in far-right and international conspiracist communities—a case of movement infrastructure enabling mainstreaming rather than radicalizing individuals directly. [[Rogers2026-cy]], explicitly a vera.ai output, offers a taxonomy-and-mapping method for locating "post-truth spaces" among four adjacent categories of problematic information, tested on Moldovan Facebook ahead of the 2024 elections—arguably the collection's most direct methodological contribution to the EU monitoring infrastructure this topic is built around.

## Encrypted and Global South circulation

A cluster of Brazil- and Global-South-focused papers insists that feed-based platform studies miss where much real-world harm occurs. [[Ventura2025-sw]]'s WhatsApp deactivation experiment and [[Kansaon2025-id]]'s Rapid-Spread-Network analysis of 13 million Brazilian WhatsApp messages both show that encrypted messaging—largely invisible to platform transparency regimes—is a decisive vector, in the latter case linking coordinated messaging directly to real-world mobilization (Supreme Court doxxing, January 8 unrest). [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] jointly develop the downstream political consequences in the Brazilian case: belief in electoral misinformation increases both political intolerance and depresses trust in election results, while mainstream news use (even among Bolsonaro voters) acts as a stabilizing counterweight—together arguing that disinformation's damage runs through erosion of democratic norms (tolerance, trust) rather than mere factual confusion. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan deepfake experiment extends this Global South emphasis, showing that a deepfake's persuasive impact is not fixed at production but socially negotiated through surrounding comments—a "collective sensemaking" argument that resonates strongly with [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]]'s U.S. election-rumor work, suggesting a convergent theoretical finding across very different national contexts: audience commentary and framing, not just content, determine belief effects.

## Audience psychology, correction, and the limits of debunking

A parallel strand keeps closer to individual-level cognition and intervention design. [[Adam2026-tz]]'s panel-plus-web-tracking study of COVID conspiracy beliefs in Germany/Switzerland finds both "contagion" (alternative media) and "mitigation" (mainstream media) effects, mediated by populist selective exposure and backfire—directly complementing [[Rohrbach2026-rc]]'s claim that journalistic strategies can inadvertently *generate*, not just diffuse, conspiracy beliefs. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s Australian diary study widens the aperture beyond "hot button" topics, finding most everyday misinformation is textual, mainstream-attributed, and tied to clickbait rather than fringe sources—an important corrective to alarmism about AI and audiovisual content. On the intervention side, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that brief Instagram prebunking ads durably improve fearmongering detection at scale, while [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] show that personalized LLM dialogue can durably shift even entrenched conspiracy beliefs and policy attitudes across structurally different issues (climate, vaccines, inequality), particularly among skeptics—jointly making a strong case that depth and personalization, not just factual correction, are what previous debunking approaches lacked. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates the normative self-image of fact-checking by showing it is itself saturated with emotional language, echoing [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]]'s finding that misinformation *source* (influencer vs. regular user) drives distinct patterns of online toxicity independent of content—both papers pointing toward affect and source credibility as underexamined mechanisms.

## Visual, synthetic, and AI-mediated disinformation

The newest frontier concerns generative AI and multimodality. [[Emilio2026-ik]] offers the most totalizing framing, arguing that GenAI's real danger is "synthetic realities"—a four-layer stack of fabricated content, identity, interaction, and institutions—that induces a "Generative AI Paradox" wherein societies may rationally discount all digital evidence. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] empirically tempers alarm about this frontier: AI-generated images and decontextualized video are not uniformly more persuasive than text, with video only outperforming when authentic footage already exists to be recontextualized (Ukraine) but not when facts are inherently uncertain (MH370). [[Choi2026-bz]] identifies a "modality-congruent carryover effect" whereby difficulty authenticating one deepfake shapes susceptibility to subsequent same-format fakes. [[Gardam2025-er]] argues methodologically that visual-first, rather than text-centric, analysis is needed for Instagram climate-denial discourse, complementing [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s experimental finding that among climate misinformation, content category (attacks on climate solutions) matters far more than logical-fallacy type for perceived veracity and polarization. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] and [[Triedman2025-uy]] extend the AI theme to knowledge infrastructure itself: LLMs remain poor fact-checkers without curated retrieval, and Grokipedia—Musk's AI-generated Wikipedia rival—is shown to be Wikipedia-derivative but systematically skewed toward unreliable and blacklisted sources on controversial topics, illustrating how generative AI can quietly re-inject fringe sourcing into ostensibly authoritative reference infrastructure.

## Standpoint, socialization, and the far-right pipeline

Finally, a set of ethnographic and qualitative papers insists that belief formation in conspiratorial and far-right milieus is gradual, socially embedded, and often self-consciously "rational" rather than credulous. [[Marwick2025-ov]]'s study of ConspiracyTok upends the stereotype of the white male conspiracist, showing young, non-white, and female creators building "populist knowledge production" under a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously standpoint-empowering and disinformation-vulnerable. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends this with "processual redpilling," arguing far-right belief adoption is rarely a single conversion but a socialization process in which disinformation is marshaled as "evidence" to sustain a self-image of rational inquiry—directly complementing [[Rothut2026-or]]'s meso-level account of protest-facilitated mainstreaming and [[Righetti2025-slf9]]'s transnational anti-gender networks, together suggesting that mainstreaming operates simultaneously at the level of individual narrative-work, movement infrastructure, and organized network amplification. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] closes this arc with a reflexive twist: even *discourse about* election interference (rather than interference itself) can independently amplify perceived societal division and affective polarization, suggesting that both disinformation and the anxious discourse surrounding it are co-productive of the democratic harms researchers set out to measure—a fitting final note for a field increasingly aware that its own object of study is entangled with how it is narrated, monitored, and fought over.
