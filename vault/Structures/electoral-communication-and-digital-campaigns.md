# Electoral Communication & Digital Campaigns

## Mapping a Fragmented Terrain

The papers gathered here document a single underlying transformation—national elections increasingly unfold as contests fought across platforms, algorithms, and data infrastructures—refracted through radically different national contexts and methodological lenses. Read together, they trace an arc from early, often skeptical, attempts to measure whether social media "effects" exist at all, through increasingly sophisticated detection of coordinated manipulation, to a present moment defined by platform governance, shrinking data access, and the integration of generative AI into campaign communication.

## Do Platforms Actually Change Minds? The Persistent Null-Effects Puzzle

A recurring thread, anchored especially in US 2016 and 2020 election research, is the surprising resilience of null or minimal-effect findings against popular narratives of algorithmic manipulation. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] established early that fake news exposure, while real and partisan-skewed, was modest relative to mainstream media consumption and heavily concentrated among small user subsets. [[Guess2019-ym]] extended this to sharing behavior, finding age—not just ideology—as the dominant predictor. This skepticism deepened with the large Meta-collaboration studies: [[Guess2023-ur]] showed chronological feeds dramatically altered on-platform experience without moving polarization or knowledge; [[Allcott2025-jb]] found ad removal similarly inert; [[Eady2023-xg]] showed Russian IRA exposure was dwarfed by domestic political content and uncorrelated with attitude change; and [[Appel2026-qr]] found associations between deceptive-network exposure and downstream beliefs vanished after controlling for pre-exposure characteristics. Collectively these studies, largely enabled by unusual researcher-platform collaborations, push back hard against alarmist diffusion narratives—while [[Kim2026-wg]] complicates the picture by showing that *average* null effects can mask substantial *heterogeneous* harms, with geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads producing large turnout declines among specifically targeted subpopulations. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] shows a related asymmetry: elite fraud claims measurably erode confidence in elections among receptive partisans even when they don't shift support for democracy itself, and fact-checks fail to repair the damage.

## Detecting Coordination: From Bots to Behavior

A parallel methodological lineage concerns how to detect inauthentic or coordinated activity once "it's just bots" proved too simple. [[Keller2019-nk]] and [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] independently establish that coordination and automation are orthogonal—astroturfing campaigns can be run by humans, and coordination exists on a continuum rather than a binary. Giglietto and colleagues build an influential Italian research program on this insight: [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] develops coordinated link-sharing detection across the 2018/2019 elections, [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] extends this into a continuously updating workflow for the 2022 snap election, uncovering ideologically, commercially, and religiously motivated networks alike, and [[Mannocci2025-ig]] formalizes the field's methodological trade-offs by comparing five ways of operationalizing *multimodal* coordination. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] extends the coordination lens outside Western contexts, showing Serbia's SNS deploys a more mimetic, harder-to-detect mode of digital authoritarianism, while [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] documents a related but distinct phenomenon in Russia: state-aligned "connective counteraction" against Navalny protest mobilization. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations entirely as political brokerage in the Philippine context, emphasizing organizational infrastructure over individual bad actors. Deplatforming's downstream effects receive attention too: [[Simeone2025-vo]] shows targeted suspensions can "flip" an entire community's authority structure, while [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents how Brazil's platform-level ban on X produced a durable partisan "sorting ratchet," with conservative users disproportionately circumventing the ban.

## National Cases: Italy, Germany, Brazil, Scandinavia, and Beyond

The topic's comparative ambition is most visible in country-specific case studies that each illuminate distinct political-media configurations. The Italian strand—[[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]], [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]], [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]—builds a longitudinal portrait running from early skepticism about Facebook-Likes-as-votes, through the TV/Twitter hybridity of the 2013 "permanent campaign," to the M5S's exploitation of insular hyperpartisan networks, and finally to sophisticated LLM-assisted pipelines auditing how Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently suppressed mainstream politicians' reach while extremist accounts adapted by posting more. German election research clusters around methodological innovation under data constraints: [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs for visual campaign analysis on Instagram, [[Schulte2026-df]] documents the sheer infrastructural difficulty of multi-platform analysis during the 2025 federal election, and [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] uses sock-puppet audits to show AfD's outsized incidental visibility on TikTok stems from supporter volume rather than overt platform bias. Brazilian cases foreground the entanglement of misinformation with democratic erosion and affective polarization: [[Rodarte2026-dk]] on parliamentary epistemic authority during the Manaus crisis, [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] on how electoral misinformation corrodes tolerance and trust, [[Marino2026-slef]] on the volatility (not stability) of hyperpartisan Facebook engagement around Bolsonaro, [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] on the "democratic cost" of virality-chasing by parliamentarians, and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] on crowdsourced ad auditing. Scandinavian comparative work ([[Kalsnes2025-zb]], [[Larsson2026-ro]]) shows how emotional "Reactions" architectures shape sharing differently even among similar multiparty systems, while [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] and [[Darius2026-xl]] extend the populist-amplification thesis to the 2024 EU elections, showing radical-right and far-left populist parties alike benefit disproportionately from platform-specific engagement dynamics, especially on TikTok.

## Theorizing the Information Environment: Hybridity, Framing, and Narrative

Underpinning these empirical cases is a cluster of theoretical contributions concerned with how meaning is made collaboratively rather than simply transmitted. [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]] furnish the foundational "hybrid media system" vocabulary—adaptation, interdependence, political information cycles—that many later papers invoke, while [[Hallin2004-kg]] supplies the comparative media-systems scaffolding (Liberal, Democratic Corporatist, Polarized Pluralist) used to situate national cases like Italy's. A distinct sensemaking tradition, developed by Starbird, Prochaska and colleagues ([[Starbird2025-jj]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], [[Prochaska2026-pr]]), reconceives election misinformation not as discrete false claims but as evidence-frame interactions, "deep stories," and recurring narrative "grammars" collaboratively performed by influencers, elites, and audiences. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] extends connective action theory to intra-party factionalism via the CRT case, and [[Tornberg2025-ir]] offers a cross-national corrective: misinformation sharing by elites is best explained not by populism or ideology alone but by their *interaction* in radical-right populism specifically. [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] both reframe "fake news"/strategic lying as rhetorical weapons of negative campaigning rather than epistemic failures per se.

## Platform Governance, Data Access, and the Post-API Predicament

A final, increasingly urgent cluster addresses the infrastructural conditions of this entire research program. Bastos2025-ya, [[Lukito2026-nb]], and [[Philipp2026-tl]] each chart the collapse and partial reconstitution of researcher data access after Twitter's closure and CrowdTangle's shutdown, with the EU Digital Services Act emerging as a contested new regulatory anchor; [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] shows platforms' election policies themselves cycling through protection, expansion, and backlash phases largely reactive to US election cycles. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content-moderation transparency commitments and finds them inconsistently implemented, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] identifies a structural flaw in X's Community Notes bridging algorithm that leaves polarizing content systematically undermoderated. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] gesture toward adjacent concerns—ad-delivery algorithms and disinformation-concern formation—that remain underdeveloped empirically but point toward where the field is heading: toward AI-generated campaign content ([[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]]), algorithmically-mediated norm perception ([[Brady2026-ln]]), and gendered media ecosystems ([[Much2026-gu]]) as the next frontiers of electoral communication research, several explicitly designed, like [[Gerard2025-br]] and [[Lukito2026-il]], to track narratives and content as they migrate across an increasingly fragmented, cross-platform terrain.
