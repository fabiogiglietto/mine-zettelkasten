# Social TV & Second-Screen Engagement

## Hybrid Media, Narrow Publics: Second Screens as Political Talk-Show Companions

This strand begins with a single but data-rich inquiry into how Twitter functioned as a second screen during Italian political talk shows in the run-up to the 2013 general elections, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]]. As the sole entry filed here, it nonetheless establishes several methodological and conceptual commitments that recur across the researcher's later work: large-scale hashtag-based tweet collection tied to broadcast schedules, mixed quantitative-content-analytic treatment of audience talk, and a theoretical insistence on treating social media not as an autonomous political arena but as one node in a broader "hybrid media system."

The paper's central move is to reframe the relationship between television and Twitter as one of interdependence rather than competition or substitution. Rather than asking whether Twitter is displacing or democratizing political talk, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] asks how the two media co-produce a single hybrid space — one where broadcast agendas, event calendars, and party mobilization still largely dictate the rhythm of Twitter activity, even as audiences carve out a parallel, only loosely tethered stream of commentary. Drawing on Chadwick's hybrid media systems framework and Couldry's practice-based approach to media, the study insists on tracking practices "in" versus "through" the media: does Twitter participation shape the substantive agenda of political discourse, or does it merely accompany and comment on an agenda set elsewhere? The empirical answer is unambiguous — participation is overwhelmingly "through" rather than "in" the media. Across a coded sample of over 8,000 tweets tied to broadcast peaks, opinion/comment and requests for interaction dominate, while the introduction of new issues, angles, or sources registers as a residual category (2.4%).

This finding does double duty. Substantively, it complicates celebratory narratives of networked political participation by showing that even at massive scale (nearly 2.5 million tweets, ~187,000 users), the population engaging in second-screen talk-show practices is a narrow, self-selected slice of the electorate — and that broadcasters and politicians largely fail to reciprocate or structurally integrate this audience voice. Only a handful of shows displayed tweets on air; official party and leader accounts were nearly silent; M5S and Beppe Grillo, despite their reputation for digital-native politics, are notably absent from the talk-show-linked conversation. Methodologically, the paper models an approach the researcher will carry forward: complete hashtag-based corpus collection anchored to broadcast events, combined with manual content coding to classify the *pragmatic function* of tweets (opinion, information, interaction-seeking, agenda-shaping) rather than treating tweet volume or sentiment alone as the unit of analysis.

Because this is the only paper currently filed under the topic, the "arc" here is more programmatic than developmental — it lays the groundwork (concepts of hybridity, "in"/"through" participation, event-synchronized Twitter corpora, talk-show second-screening) that a wider Social TV research program would later build on or specialize from, whether toward media events beyond politics, cross-national comparison, or more automated computational methods. As a foundational node, it stakes out the position that Twitter data about broadcast audiences must always be read against the broadcast logic it is entangled with — a methodological caution that anticipates the researcher's subsequent, more computationally ambitious work on social media and political communication.
