# Disinformation Narratives Monitoring

## Narrative, not fact: reframing the object of study

A cluster of foundational papers in this collection converge on a single methodological turn: disinformation is best studied as *narrative* rather than as a discrete inventory of false claims. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] lays early groundwork by shifting attention from creators' intent to the observer-dependent judgments of propagators, arguing that "fake news" cascades are composite, multi-actor phenomena that resist any single true/false typology. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes this further, showing through US case studies (Parkland "crisis actors," the "pee tape," "lottery manure") that disinformation succeeds as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story* — complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes — which is precisely why domain-blocklist and fact-checking paradigms struggle to contain it. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical vocabulary to formalize this, importing Ricoeur's hermeneutic realism and Phelan's narrative ethics to show that content can be referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false — a point demonstrated on pro-Russian framings of NATO's role in the Ukraine invasion. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] give this reframing empirical teeth: the former shows survey respondents' belief tracking *cumulative* exposure to recurring narratives rather than single claims, while the latter's "evidence-frame" framework demonstrates, via 2022 Arizona midterm tweets, that misleading election rumors typically combine accurate evidence with distorted partisan frames — the locus of manipulation is the *framing act*, not the fact. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this into "deep storytelling," showing how the *style* of telling (allusion, implicit cueing, reliance on a shared 2020 "deep story") becomes as constitutive of meaning as content when 2022 election-fraud rumors are compared to 2020 predecessors. [[Goel2025-iq]] closes the loop by showing that source-level trust measures miss a large phenomenon: users repurposing genuinely factual mainstream articles to support misleading narratives — mainstream co-shared content actually reaches roughly twice the audience of "fake news" proper.

## Tools for measuring narrative circulation

This narrative turn motivates a parallel methodological program aimed squarely at the PROMPT-style tooling problem: how to detect and track narratives, rather than claims, at scale and across languages/platforms. [[Waight2025-al]] operationalizes "narrative similarity" via LLM-based claim distillation, validating it against text-reuse and topic-modeling baselines and applying it to trace the Russian "Ukrainian biolabs" story's diffusion into mainstream and low-quality US outlets — revealing cross-source narrative sharing invisible to lexical methods. [[Gerard2025-br]] tackles the cross-platform dimension directly, introducing a discourse-cluster embedding (CANE/t-CANE) that reconstructs user networks from shared narrative participation rather than platform-specific interaction signals, identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed most narrative migration between Truth Social and X. [[Rogers2026-cy]], built explicitly for fact-checkers under the EU's vera.ai project, proposes mapping "post-truth spaces" — clusters of mutually referencing, hard-to-verify sources — and demonstrates the technique on Moldovan Facebook discourse around the war and the 2024 referendum, showing that "borderline" clusters straddling post-truth and mainstream zones carry more real influence than the fringe clusters themselves. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] evaluates whether LLMs can serve as automated fact-checking backends, finding that raw model knowledge and even web search are unreliable, and that only curated retrieval-augmented context (à la PolitiFact summaries) yields usable accuracy — with a caution that citation patterns skew toward left-leaning outlets.

## Monitoring the health of the platform ecosystem

A complementary strand measures the sheer scale and distribution of mis/disinformation exposure across Europe and the US, the empirical bedrock for any dashboard. [[Vincent_undated-re]] reports that TikTok's exposure-weighted mis/disinformation share rose from ~20% to ~25% between measurement rounds, with low-credibility accounts retaining an engagement advantage nearly everywhere. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US methodology to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares around national elections even as views from untrustworthy sources stay comparatively stable — and that older users drive both exposure and sharing. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Bosch2024-hj]] add platform-specific texture: Facebook diffusion trees from the 2020 US election show misinformation's distinctive viral signature, while TikTok's sonic affordances emerge as an under-studied propaganda vector for Ukraine-war content, reaching tens of billions of views under #Ukraine. [[Nenno2025-xa]] broadens the lens globally, showing that news-value patterns (especially conflict) distinguish flagged from unflagged content across 24 countries, with the US and Brazil as polarized outliers — a caution against WEIRD-centric assumptions baked into monitoring tools.

## The war in Ukraine as a narrative battleground

Several papers converge specifically on Ukraine-war disinformation, one of PROMPT's core sensitive topics. [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs case and [[Sadler2025-vu]]'s NATO-blame analysis show narrative migration and rhetorical framing operating on the same underlying conflict from opposite methodological angles (computational vs. hermeneutic). [[Hameleers2026-mc]] experimentally isolates *modality* effects, finding that decontextualized video disinformation about the invasion outperforms text and AI-generated images in credibility and engagement — but only because ample authentic Ukraine-war footage exists to be repurposed, a caution against overweighting generative-AI threats relative to low-tech recontextualization. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes the supply side, arguing that regimes like Russia's run dual-track propaganda — reassuring domestic audiences of low costs while reshaping foreign publics' sense of stake in the war. [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[Rogers2026-cy]]'s Moldova case study further anchor the war's narrative diffusion in platform-specific and geographically peripheral contexts.

## Elections as the other great pressure point

A parallel body of work applies the narrative lens to electoral integrity across contexts. In the US, [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] dissect the mechanics of 2020–2022 fraud rumors, while [[Appel2026-qr]] uses Meta-provided data to show that deceptive 2020-election networks reached tens of millions largely through *reshares by unaffiliated accounts* rather than direct network posting, with correlational effects on beliefs vanishing once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] shift focus to elite behavior: the former's 26-country parliamentary tweet analysis finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing, but their conjunction (radical-right populism) does; the latter examines how institutional role and ideology check or enable politicians' own misinformation sharing. Outside the US, [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians contesting epistemic authority itself during the Manaus COVID collapse, while [[Kansaon2025-id]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] track Brazil's WhatsApp-centric ecosystem — coordinated groups mobilizing real-world protest around the 2022 election, and belief in electoral misinformation eroding trust even as mainstream news use bolsters it. [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s UK survey experiment adds a reflexive twist: media *narratives about* election interference can themselves increase perceived societal division and affective polarization, regardless of whether the underlying interference was effective — a caution relevant to how monitoring dashboards frame their own outputs.

## Mainstreaming, identity, and the LGBTQI+/anti-gender front

The collection's attention to LGBTQI+ rights as a sensitive topic surfaces most directly in [[Righetti2025-slf9]], which maps a decade of CitizenGO activity across four platforms, showing a centrally coordinated, multilingual anti-gender network in which "gender" narratives function as transnational symbolic glue, amplified by a homophilic international network of religious and right-wing accounts exhibiting coordinated, near-astroturf link-sharing. This sits alongside a broader mainstreaming literature: [[Rothut2026-or]] shows how the German Querdenken protest movement became structurally embedded in far-right and conspiracist Telegram communities through shared anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology; [[Marwick2025-ov]] documents how young, diverse "ConspiracyTok" creators construct a "generous epistemology" that both empowers marginalized standpoints and disseminates antisemitic and transphobic tropes; and [[Marwick2025-vx]] reframes far-right "redpilling" as gradual socialization through accumulated pseudo-evidence rather than sudden conversion. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] adds a consumer-behavior angle, showing influencer-sourced misinformation generates more toxic, echo-chamber-like audience reactions than identical claims from ordinary users, especially on sociopolitical topics.

## Infrastructures of coordinated propaganda

Underpinning many of these narratives are identifiable organizational actors. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] offers a rare organizational history of the St Petersburg Internet Research Agency via leaked employee CVs, showing it recruited young, inexperienced staff well before the 2016 US election (peaking around the Crimea annexation) and operated as an ordinary media-labor-market entity rather than a covert intelligence unit. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] documents coordinated forwarding among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus, while [[Copland2025-em]] traces how Sky News Australia became a node in transnational right-wing "network propaganda," running parochial Facebook content domestically while feeding conspiratorial material into the US YouTube pipeline. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] generalizes the pattern, arguing that manipulation campaigns persist by opportunistically appropriating emerging events rather than fading after their original topic loses salience.

## Fact-checkers, platforms, and the practice of countering disinformation

The final arc turns to the institutional response this whole ecosystem provokes — directly relevant to PROMPT's fact-checker-facing tools. [[Farkas2026-lr]] shows European fact-checkers rhetorically defending their platform-funding partnerships through differentiation and transcendence strategies, revealing an emergent and contested professional boundary. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically dismantles Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing of Meta's fact-checking program, finding removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases and that fact-checkers themselves favor transparency and counter-speech over deletion, while expressing skepticism toward Community Notes. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] complicates the picture further by asking whether journalistic reporting strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines the strategic use of emotionality within fact-checking content itself, and [[Ramos2026-qo]] — written by Maldita.es fact-checkers reflecting on the 2024 Valencia floods conspiracy — makes the collection's practical case most explicit: claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with evolving, emotionally resonant narratives, motivating real-time narrative monitoring and argument-checking as necessary complements, precisely the synthesis this whole body of research points toward for dashboard and LLM tools serving journalists and fact-checkers across Europe.
