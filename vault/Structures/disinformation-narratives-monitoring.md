# Disinformation Narratives Monitoring

## From discrete falsehoods to circulating narratives

The papers gathered here trace a conceptual migration away from treating disinformation as a set of verifiable or falsifiable claims, toward treating it as *narrative* — a cultural form that moves across platforms, genres, and time. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this case most forcefully: successful disinformation behaves like story, with characters, settings, plots, and catchphrases ("pee tape," "crisis actor") that evoke whole narratives in shorthand, and it spreads because it satisfies desire, affirms partisan identity, and delivers catharsis rather than because audiences are duped by false facts. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes this empirically, showing in a U.S. survey that *repeated* cross-outlet exposure to a narrative — not exposure to any single claim — predicts belief, with the substantive content of the story itself mattering as much as dose. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the theoretical stakes further, arguing that disinformation studies has used "narrative" loosely and intuitively; drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan, he proposes a hermeneutic-realist and narrative-ethical framework for assessing content that is referentially slippery (neither clearly true nor false) but ethically monological, using pro-Russian Venezuelan Twitter discourse on NATO and Ukraine as a case. This dovetails with [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], an earlier but foundational piece arguing that fixating on creator *intent* (the classic disinformation/misinformation distinction) misses how false information actually cycles through propagators' situated judgments in a hybrid media system — a cybernetic, propagation-centred alternative to typological definitions.

The CSCW/sensemaking tradition arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from election-integrity data. [[Starbird2025-jj]] shows that election rumors on Twitter are less about fabricated facts than about politically charged *frames* applied to largely accurate evidence, with quote-tweet threads revealing how influencers supply evidence while audiences supply the frame. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this into a theory of "deep storytelling," showing how the *style* of telling — alluding to "Box 3," "Sharpie," "Dominion" — carries as much meaning as content, and how 2020's elaborated voter-fraud narrative became interpretive scaffolding that let 2022 audiences read ambiguous events (Maricopa tabulators, USPS delays) through an established deep story without any new explicit claims. [[Goel2025-iq]] demonstrates the same dynamic from the supply side: narrative, not source unreliability, is the better predictor of misleading content, since mainstream articles co-shared with fake news are measurably more likely to contain the narrative structures found in debunked claims — meaning domain-blocklist approaches to measuring misinformation systematically undercount its true reach by missing how true content is repurposed into misleading stories.

## Measuring and modeling narrative propagation

This conceptual turn creates a methodological problem that several papers address directly: how do you *measure* something as diffuse as a narrative at scale? [[Waight2025-al]] is the most explicit methodological contribution, arguing that narrative similarity is a distinct "estimand" from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and building an LLM pipeline (claim/subject distillation plus SBERT-guided pairwise comparison) that outperforms exact-text-reuse, topic modeling, and Relatio baselines; applied to the Ukrainian "biolabs" story, it reveals narrative diffusion into mainstream U.S. outlets invisible to n-gram-based detection. [[Scalco2026-bd]] tackles a complementary measurement problem — not narrative content but the structural conditions (information voids, where demand outstrips credible supply) that let misinformation flourish — formalizing "void," "lack," "balance," "abundance," and "overabundance" regimes from Facebook, Twitter, and search-trend data around COVID-19 vaccines, and showing voids correlate with lower-credibility content shares. [[Rogers2026-cy]], developed for the EU vera.ai project, offers a third measurement register aimed squarely at practitioners: mapping "post-truth spaces" as network clusters resistant to verification, and showing via a Moldovan Facebook case study that a *borderline* cluster — not the post-truth cluster itself — often has the highest betweenness centrality and thus the greatest bridging influence into mainstream discourse, a finding with direct implications for where fact-checkers should look. Together these three papers suggest the PROMPT-style monitoring agenda needs at least three complementary instruments: narrative-similarity detection, demand/supply imbalance detection, and network-topological mapping of verification-resistant clusters.

## Platform ecologies and the war in Ukraine

A cluster of papers examines platform-specific vectors for narrative propagation, with the Ukraine war serving as a recurring test case. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-studied, TikTok-specific propaganda affordance in Ukraine-war content (36.9 billion #Ukraine views in three months), complementing [[Hameleers2026-mc]]'s experimental finding that *video* disinformation about the war outperforms text and AI-generated images in credibility and engagement — but only because ample authentic war footage exists to be decontextualized, suggesting generative-AI anxieties may be overstated relative to low-tech recontextualization. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Sadler2025-vu]] both use the war to show how Russian state narratives (biolabs, NATO-enlargement blame) migrate into Western discourse through different mechanisms — lexical narrative-sharing with low-quality U.S. outlets in the former, Manichean narrative ethics in Spanish-language influencer discourse in the latter. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes the production side, arguing that regimes like Russia run dual-track propaganda — domestic messaging emphasizing high stakes/low costs to sustain legitimacy, foreign-directed messaging designed to erode adversaries' resolve — while [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] notes the structural persistence of such campaigns, which survive by continually appropriating new emerging events rather than fading after any single crisis.

Beyond Ukraine, [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] map how platform affordances shape propaganda's international reach and authorship. Sky News Australia exemplifies "network propaganda," running a dual strategy — parochial Facebook content for domestic audiences, US-coded YouTube content (COVID, voter fraud, the Great Reset) that plugs into the transnational right-wing pipeline. ConspiracyTok inverts the demographic stereotype of the conspiracy theorist, showing young, non-white, and female creators deploying a "generous epistemology" that is simultaneously a vector of standpoint knowledge and of disinformation, antisemitism, and transphobia — a useful corrective to monolithic pictures of who produces and consumes these narratives. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] supplies the topic's LGBTQI+ case study proper: a decade of CitizenGO activity shows a centrally coordinated (not organic) multilingual network in which anti-gender narratives function as transnational "symbolic glue," more evenly distributed across national pages than pro-life content, amplified by a homophilic, partly astroturfed network of right-wing, religious, and pro-life accounts — directly paralleling the coordination logics documented for war narratives.

Underlying all of this, the older quantitative baselines remain load-bearing reference points: [[Vosoughi2018-at]] established that falsehood travels farther, faster, and deeper than truth on Twitter (driven by novelty and human sharing, not bots), while [[Allcott2019-gn]] showed that the relative Facebook/Twitter balance of engagement with fake news shifted substantially after 2016, reminding the newer narrative-centric and platform-specific work that aggregate engagement metrics still matter even once "narrative" rather than "false claim" becomes the unit of analysis. [[Vincent_undated-re]]'s ongoing measurement of TikTok's rising mis/disinformation share (roughly 20%→25%) and persistent low-credibility engagement advantage functions as a contemporary, Europe-specific continuation of that monitoring tradition, directly feeding dashboard-style accountability efforts like PROMPT's.

## Elections, trust, and intolerance

Electoral narratives receive sustained attention through Brazilian and U.S. cases that connect belief in misinformation to downstream democratic harms rather than mere factual confusion. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use the same three-wave Brazilian panel to show that electoral-fraud misinformation belief both increases political intolerance and decreases trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) operating as an indirect amplifier via belief formation rather than a direct driver, and mainstream news consumption acting as a stabilizing counterweight even among "losing" partisans — evidence that professional journalism's institutional role persists even amid narrative erosion. [[Berlinski2023-rj]] complements this experimentally in the U.S. context: unsubstantiated elite voter-fraud claims measurably erode confidence in elections, concentrated among co-partisans, and critically, mainstream fact-checks fail to offset the damage — a sobering finding for any fact-check-centred monitoring intervention. Read together with [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], the picture that emerges is one in which elite cues, platform affordances, and audience narrative literacy jointly reproduce an election-fraud "deep story" that is largely immune to claim-level correction.

## Fact-checking under strain: practice, politics, and platform dependency

A final cluster turns reflexively to the fact-checking institutions that PROMPT-style monitoring is meant to support, and finds them epistemically squeezed from multiple directions. [[Farkas2026-lr]] shows European fact-checkers engaging in defensive "apologia" rhetoric to justify their financial dependence on the very platforms (Meta, Google, ByteDance) whose content they adjudicate, differentiating themselves from platform motives while invoking the pragmatic necessity of collaboration — a tension that becomes acute in [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s empirical rebuttal of Zuckerberg's 2025 framing of fact-checkers as censors: content removal occurs in only ~30% of fact-checked cases, fact-checkers overwhelmingly favor labeling and counter-speech over deletion, and they view Meta's pivot to Community Notes with skepticism as vulnerable to coordinated manipulation. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from inside Maldita.es during the 2024 Valencia floods, argues from practice that claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with narrative mutation (the Bonaire "hidden bodies" conspiracy morphed through multiple claim variants as volume quadrupled), proposing "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" — attacking the underlying logical/rhetorical scaffolding rather than individual claims — as necessary complements, operationalizing exactly the narrative-level intervention that [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], and [[Starbird2025-jj]] call for theoretically. [[Xue2025-bp]] identifies a further complication: fact-checks themselves are often emotionally charged rather than neutrally informational, raising questions about whether affect-laden correction reproduces the very dynamics (desire, catharsis, outrage) that make disinformation narratives sticky in the first place — a worry sharpened by [[Hameleers2026-mc]]'s finding that fact-checks reduce credibility roughly equally across text/image/video modalities (some reassurance) and by [[Rohrbach2026-rc]]'s suggestion that journalistic framing choices can themselves inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs.

## Foundational framings

Several canonical, pre-2020 papers anchor this whole literature's assumptions and remain the implicit baseline against which the narrative turn is argued. [[Freelon2020-yp]] codifies the now-standard definitional apparatus (disinformation as intent-to-harm, versus misinformation and propaganda) that [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], and [[Marwick2026-qd]] each push back against from different angles. [[Lazer2018-mm]] catalogued the early evidentiary gaps and intervention uncertainties (fact-checking's mixed efficacy, bot prevalence, polarization) that much of the later work — [[Berlinski2023-rj]], [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], [[Xue2025-bp]] — has since tried to close empirically. And [[Bennett2018-ys]]'s "disinformation order" thesis — that disinformation reflects a systemic legitimacy crisis of democratic institutions rather than an episodic media-technological glitch — supplies the macro-political frame within which the Brazilian election studies, the anti-gender mainstreaming case, and the Ukraine-war propaganda studies can all be read as symptoms of the same underlying order rather than unrelated phenomena.
