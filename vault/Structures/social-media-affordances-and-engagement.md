# Social Media Affordances & Engagement

## From Filter Bubbles to Causal Feeds: The Evidentiary Core

The anchor of this literature is a long argument about whether algorithmic curation itself — as opposed to user choice, network homophily, or elite behaviour — drives polarization and misperception. Early observational work set a cautiously reassuring baseline: [[Flaxman2016-lm]] found online news consumption dominated by direct, habitual visits to mainstream outlets, with social media and search contributing only modest increases in segregation (alongside modest increases in cross-cutting exposure); [[Bakshy2015-rn]] showed that individual clicking choices, not the News Feed algorithm, did most of the work suppressing ideologically discordant content; and [[Messing2014-jc]] demonstrated that social endorsement cues can override partisan source cues altogether, undercutting the strongest "fragmentation" narratives. [[Bail2018-fk]]'s field experiment complicated any optimism about simply engineering more cross-cutting exposure, showing that forced exposure to the opposing side can backfire, especially among conservatives.

This debate was decisively scaled up by the 2020 Meta/academic collaboration studies, which this corpus treats almost as a reference point against which everything subsequent is measured. [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Guess2023-ai]] found that switching off algorithmic ranking (chronological feed) or stripping reshares dramatically changed what people saw and engaged with but left attitudes and polarization essentially untouched; [[Nyhan2023-gb]] showed like-minded content is pervasive but reducing it does not depolarize; [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] complicated the "null effects" story by documenting an *asymmetric* funnel of engagement in which misinformation and untrustworthy domains concentrate almost entirely in a homogeneously conservative corner of the Facebook ecosystem; and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] extends the null-effects finding specifically to untrustworthy-source exposure, again concentrated among a small subset of heavy users.

More recent work pushes back against the "algorithms don't matter much" consensus by changing the platform, the outcome, or the causal pathway. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that X's algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes rightward — but through a persistence mechanism (algorithm-induced following choices) that the earlier chronological-feed experiments could not have detected, offering a plausible reconciliation with the Meta null results. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] dissect the mechanics behind such effects on Musk-era X/Twitter: the former shows apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage is largely explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while the latter demonstrates that X's recommender embeds a strongly linear, inadvertently-learned left-right dimension that can be surgically removed. [[Brown2026-br]] similarly disaggregates supply from demand on YouTube, finding strong "rabbit hole" dynamics but no independent algorithmic echo-chamber or radicalization effect — though a persistent platform-wide conservative tilt. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment reframes the mechanism entirely: engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived *social norms* about political dialogue (not necessarily direct attitudes), and a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can repair this without sacrificing enjoyment. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] takes the elite-centred variant of this argument furthest, showing that simply blocking political elites from feeds — a structural, exposure-architecture intervention — measurably reduces misinformation susceptibility and improves factual knowledge, again without moving affective polarization. Together, [[Schulz2026-ts]], [[Green2025-ap]], and [[Oswald2025-km]] supply a sobering coda: much of what looks like platform-driven polarization is an artefact of *who chooses to post* and how *networked curation* unbundles stories from sources, meaning visible discourse — the "tip of the iceberg" — systematically misrepresents both underlying opinion and underlying source partisanship.

## Reach and Virality: How Content Travels at Scale

A second cluster asks not about attitudes but about the structural dynamics of reach itself. [[Vosoughi2018-at]] established the foundational finding that falsehood diffuses farther, faster, and deeper than truth on Twitter, propelled by human novelty-seeking rather than bots. [[Guess2021-ym]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] extend this to Facebook at platform scale, showing low-credibility content is a small but non-trivial share of the ecosystem, concentrated among older and more conservative users, with viral reach disproportionately achieved by a small set of posts and actors. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complicates the simple "shares drive views" model that underlies much of this work: amplification from shares to views is reliably positive but is *dampened* for partisan-audience content and *boosted* for high-quality journalism, and — crucially — this relationship fluctuates with Facebook's own governance interventions (notably the 2020 "break the glass" measures), evidence that the platform is an active curator rather than a neutral conduit. [[McNally2025-dn]] arrives at a structurally similar conclusion from a different angle, using time-series methods to show Facebook's News Feed algorithm detectably and differentially treats hard news versus soft content — evidence against the "black box" framing and a case for systemic algorithmic auditing. [[Votta2025-xz]] extends the reach question to paid political advertising, showing ad delivery algorithms (not just advertiser targeting) shape who sees campaign messages and at what cost. [[Mosleh2024-op]] broadens the whole inquiry beyond the Twitter/Facebook duopoly, showing that engagement with partisan and low-quality news diverges meaningfully across seven platforms — a methodological corrective echoed by [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]], whose study of Google News Italia as a health-news gatekeeper for Facebook shows engagement is driven more by emotional topic valence (celebrity health stories) than source identity, foreshadowing the "affect over source" logic that recurs throughout this literature.

## Affective Affordances: Reactions, Emotion, and the Logic of Sharing

A dedicated subliterature treats Facebook's Reactions feature and emotional valence more generally as a measurable affordance shaping visibility. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s systematic review of 64 studies synthesizes the pattern that recurs across nearly every individual case: negative affect — especially anger — reliably drives engagement and sharing of political content, while lifestyle content skews positive. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] confirms this directly for Scandinavian election campaigns, showing populist parties are disproportionately skilled at eliciting "Angry" reactions, and that anger (not love) predicts shares. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and its companion [[Larsson2026-nl]] extend this into a decade-long longitudinal frame for Norway, showing negative sentiment has risen over time (especially under more combative leadership and among challenger/fringe parties), that negativity reliably drives shares and comments while likes follow a distinct, more positive logic, and that the negativity-engagement link itself appears to weaken toward the end of the period — a sign of possible audience fatigue or strategic recalibration. [[Marino2026-slef]] pushes this affective-affordance logic into a non-Western, destabilized setting, showing that even within a hyperpartisan pro-Bolsonaro network, affective engagement (love/anger/share/comment ratios) is highly volatile and event-contingent rather than a fixed echo-chamber property — complicating static models of partisan community. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] identifies a parallel, darker affective dynamic in the influencer economy: misinformation from influencers (versus ordinary users) generates more frequent and more homogeneous toxicity, because credibility and parasocial bonds convert into "brand-related misinformation legitimation" and "community enmeshment." At the other temporal end of this strand, [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] and [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] offer earlier, more infrastructural readings of affective affordances — Farci showing how "Liking" and tagging function as low-cost intimacy signals that collaboratively manage public/private boundaries, and Giglietto showing that even raw popularity metrics (Likes) fail to robustly predict electoral outcomes, an early caution against over-reading engagement metrics as political signal that anticipates [[Oswald2025-km]]'s production-consumption gap argument decades later. [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] closes the loop institutionally: Brazilian legislators *believe* antagonistic, news-cycle-aligned content outperforms substantive legislative communication, and computational analysis confirms they are largely correct — showing how the Reactions-era emotional economy reshapes not just what circulates but what politicians choose to say.

## Theorizing Visibility: Affordances, Imaginaries, and Platform Power

A more conceptual strand supplies the theoretical vocabulary the empirical studies above draw on. [[Slack1783508184-sld3]] (boyd's "networked publics") remains the foundational statement that SNS affordances — persistence, replicability, scalability, searchability — generate predictable dynamics like invisible audiences and context collapse. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] operationalizes this for Twitter specifically, showing how users construct an "imagined audience" to navigate collapsed contexts and how authenticity itself becomes a managed performance — a problem [[Chancellor2026-sld9]] argues has outgrown its original formulation, proposing a structural "grammar" of context (user, platform, data/interaction) to theorize new, algorithm-specific collapses (identity, algorithmic, situational) suited to recommendation-driven rather than network-driven platforms. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] makes a parallel, platform-historical argument at a larger scale: TikTok inaugurates a "second generation" of social media in which "networked publics" give way to algorithmically-constructed "clustered publics," item-centric and opaque rather than people-centric and visible — a shift Gerbaudo argues is spreading across the ecosystem via "TikTokification." [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] offers an instructive historical counterpoint from the earlier, less algorithmically mediated era: Twitter-as-second-screen during Italian televised politics shows participation remaining narrow and TV-logic-dominated even where networked affordances existed, underscoring how much has changed.

Studies of algorithmic *experience* and *governance* complete this section. [[Bucher2017-qh]] shows ordinary users encounter Facebook's algorithm affectively — through "algorithmic imaginary" folk theories, moments of breakdown, and strategic self-adjustment — establishing that algorithms are felt and fought with, not just imposed. [[Petre2019-zh]] and [[Cotter2019-js]] extend this into the realm of algorithmic visibility as contested terrain: Petre shows platforms moralize the boundary between "legitimate strategy" and illegitimate "gaming," a boundary that tracks platform material interest more than principle ("platform paternalism"), while Cotter reframes the same phenomenon among Instagram influencers as a "visibility game" with interdependent, not unilateral, rule-making — a more agentic account that nonetheless concedes the game favours already-resourced actors. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the governance-side complement: "reduction" (algorithmic demotion short of removal) is now a dominant, under-scrutinized moderation technique that concentrates curatorial power while evading the transparency mechanisms built for removal-based moderation — directly anticipating the auditing arguments of [[McNally2025-dn]] and the ambient-circulation problem documented below in [[Rieder2026-pp]].

## Platform Ecologies: Populism, the Far Right, and Platform-Tailored Mobilization

A substantial cluster applies this affordance vocabulary to far-right and populist communication across specific platforms, consistently finding that actors do not simply replicate messages across platforms but tailor content to platform-specific visibility logics. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] provides the most systematic comparative evidence: German far-right networks on Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram exhibit markedly different structures — from Twitter's loose, broadcast-oriented scale to Facebook's hierarchical, AfD-dominated replicability — because affordances are relational outcomes of platform design meeting user practice, not fixed properties. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] documents the same logic in miniature: the Sweden Democrats ran argumentative, text-heavy content on Facebook/X but prioritized memetic, TikTok-native securitizing content (often using non-Swedish, even non-EU footage) aimed at younger voters, despite having a far smaller TikTok following. [[Darius2026-xl]] generalizes this finding across the entire 2024 European Parliament election: populist radical right (and, to a lesser degree, far-left populist) parties receive disproportionate audience support relative to posting volume, with TikTok the most asymmetric arena — a reach advantage driven by communicative style (emotional appeal, anti-elitism, Euroscepticism) rather than sheer activity. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] localizes the mechanism for incidental, non-partisan exposure: sock-puppet audits of German TikTok show AfD content disproportionately reaches politically disinterested users, driven by volume and virality among unofficial "multiplier" accounts rather than overt platform favouritism. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] extend the analysis to the softer, more ambient end of radicalization: far-right Instagram Reels deploy humour and positive affect to normalize extremism through "affective-discursive ecologies," while YouTube's "Tate-space" shows that deplatforming a figure cannot contain a decentralized, recommendation-amplified ecosystem of interviews, remixes, and memes that constitutes "ambient ideology." [[Cabbuag2024-me]] and [[Much2026-gu]] round out the platform-ecology picture with adjacent cultural phenomena — Filipino TikTok "dogshows" normalizing legal-but-harmful humour, and U.S. entertainment podcasts functioning as "gendered media spaces" that incidentally sort young male audiences toward conservative content, each showing how affordance-driven visibility logics bleed into domains far from explicit political messaging. Finally, [[Wang2026-ub]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] examine platform-ecosystem rupture itself: the failed academic migration from Twitter to Mastodon shows that even highly motivated communities cannot easily reconstruct lost network capital on decentralized alternatives, while right-leaning outlets' selective "cross-platformization" between Twitter and Truth Social shows legacy media actors actively curating differentiated presences across a fragmenting platform landscape.

## Misinformation, Trust, and the Limits of Intervention

A final empirical cluster concentrates on misinformation specifically as an exposure and intervention problem. [[Holt2026-zq]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] both complicate the premise that misinformation is a matter of discrete, identifiable false claims: Holt shows user "False News" reports on Facebook target polarized, contextualizing content more than outright falsehoods (a "narrow statement of disagreement" rather than a wisdom-of-crowds signal), while Prochaska shows election misinformation operates as evolving "deep stories" collaboratively performed by influencers, elites, and audiences — meaning and misleadingness live in narrative context that single-post analysis misses. Against this backdrop, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] offers a rare success story for intervention design: a 19-second Instagram prebunking ad measurably and durably (five months) improved users' ability to detect emotional manipulation at remarkably low cost, complementing [[Jaidka2026-sl34]]'s elite-exposure intervention as evidence that *structural* rather than purely cognitive interventions can move the needle where content-labelling approaches have struggled.

## Creator Labor, Audience Concepts, and the Evolving Toolkit

A final set of papers concerns how individuals and researchers adapt to these affordance regimes. [[Volpe2026-um]] shows Italian micro-influencers treating second-hand fashion content as a survival strategy under dual precarity — labour-market and algorithmic — in which "care" and strategic self-branding are inseparable. [[Tiggemann2020-sl44]] demonstrates a countervailing affordance-level intervention: "Instagram vs reality" pairings measurably reduce body dissatisfaction by changing the register of social comparison, showing platform content formats can be repurposed against their own idealization logics. [[Wedel2026-ix]] proposes "informativeness" as an audience-centred replacement for institutional "newsworthiness," showing young adults' judgments of value are shaped by platform-specific selection logics (self-selected on YouTube, actor-mediated on Instagram, algorithmic on TikTok). Methodologically, [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] maps the fragmented field of "algorithm literacy," [[Wan2026-ai]] links political efficacy to algorithmic-feed selection behaviour via real usage traces, and [[Arminio2025-tw]] demonstrates that vision-language models can recover connotative, culturally-embedded meaning from political imagery where CNN-based clustering captures only denotative content — a concrete methodological upgrade for the visual-political-communication research this whole literature increasingly requires. [[Manovich2026-ih]] gestures toward where this toolkit is heading: treating generative AI itself as a new creative medium with its own affordance structure (probabilistic variability, "media cognition," a conservative pull toward training-data norms) suggests that the next wave of platform content — and thus the next wave of engagement and visibility dynamics studied here — will increasingly be authored by, not merely curated by, algorithmic systems.
