# Health Misinformation & Problematic Content

## The Architecture of Coordinated Problematic Content

The core empirical thread running through this cluster is the discovery that health misinformation rarely spreads as a diffuse, organic phenomenon — it is disproportionately manufactured and amplified by identifiable coordinated actors. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the methodological backbone for this claim, showing that Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior (CLSB), detected via the content-agnostic CooRnet package, can surface problematic COVID-19 content on Facebook faster and at a higher hit rate than routine fact-checking. Crucially, the paper documents how Italian covid-skeptic networks evolved tactically — laundering links through reputable outlets, hiding URLs in first comments, exploiting religious and community groups — turning coordination detection into an arms race against platform enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this same Italian dataset toward a different question: not just *who coordinates* but *whose voices* coordination amplifies, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers generates disproportionate share-driven engagement, and that legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain the central conduits recycling these voices — a remediation dynamic that lets problematic content borrow legitimacy from mainstream media formats. [[Song2025-yh]] generalizes the CLSB lens cross-nationally and ideologically, comparing UK and US Facebook vaccine discourse and showing that coordination itself is not inherently malicious: pro-vaccine coalitions coordinate around institutional sources (NHS, CDC) just as anti-vaccine coalitions coordinate around unverifiable claims, with national political culture (safety skepticism in the UK vs. individual-freedom framing in the US) shaping content even when the coordination mechanism is identical. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the coordinated-network paradigm into the Twitter/news ecosystem, showing that a small, overwhelmingly contrarian coordinated retweet network handpicks credentialed but anti-consensus experts for amplification — and, notably, that mainstream news coverage of scientific papers tends to *follow* rather than precede this superspreader activity, positioning platform actors as agenda-setters for the press rather than the reverse.

## Ecosystem-Level Signals: Supply, Demand, and Gatekeeping

A parallel strand treats misinformation not as a network property but as an ecosystem-level imbalance. [[Scalco2026-bd]] formalizes this with the concept of "information voids" — quantifiable mismatches between public demand (Wikipedia views, search trends) and reliable supply (news, social posts) — and shows empirically that these voids, which persist longer than periods of overabundance, are the precise moments when highly credible content recedes and misinformation share rises. This gives an ecological account of *when* problematic content finds an opening, complementing the network-level account of *who* fills it. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examines the supply side from a gatekeeping angle, showing that Google News Italia's aggregation choices shape which health topics circulate on Facebook, but that user engagement is driven less by source credibility than by emotional resonance (celebrity health stories vastly outperform technical medical-science content in comments) — a finding that helps explain why voids and coordinated networks find such fertile ground: audiences reward affect over accuracy regardless of gatekeeping quality.

## Who Gets Exposed, and With What Effect

Moving from the supply-side to the audience, [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Adam2026-tz]] both use tracked, individual-level exposure data to complicate simple contagion narratives. [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds that low-credibility health content exposure is rare in aggregate but extremely concentrated — a small fraction of (mostly older) users account for the vast majority of exposure — and that this exposure flows through habitual cross-domain browsing (other low-credibility sites, political news) rather than search or social referral, challenging platform-centric intervention strategies. [[Adam2026-tz]] complements this with a panel-plus-web-tracking design showing that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and prior predispositions: populists selectively engage with conspiracy-supporting alternative media and selectively avoid, or backfire against, mainstream debunking, even though mainstream media itself circulates surprising amounts of conspiracy-adjacent content. Together these two studies suggest that the "problematic content" pipeline is less about universal contagion than about a demand-driven audience segment whose prior worldview and browsing habits pre-select them into exposure.

## From Exposure to Consequence, and Toward Correction

Two papers push past exposure to ask what work problematic content actually does. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] offers perhaps the most consequential causal claim in the set: using a novel SIRVA epidemic model fit to county-level Twitter, case, and vaccination data, the authors estimate that antivaccine tweets causally produced roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and measurable excess cases and deaths — moving the field beyond correlational or experimental-intention studies toward population-scale causal inference linking online speech to offline mortality. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] complicates the "misinformation" frame itself, showing that UK newspapers' politically polarized citation of GBD versus JSM signatories reflects legitimate scientific disagreement being selectively amplified along partisan lines rather than fringe pseudoscience — a caution against collapsing all contested science communication into the coordinated-network/misinformation paradigm. Finally, [[Costello2024-bg]] closes the arc on an interventionist note: against accounts (echoed implicitly in the selective-avoidance dynamics of [[Adam2026-tz]]) that treat conspiracy belief as identity-protected and evidence-proof, personalized AI dialogues durably reduced conspiracy belief by roughly 20%, suggesting that the depth and tailoring of counter-evidence — not the psychological entrenchment of the believer — has been the limiting factor in correction efforts all along.

Read together, these eleven papers trace an arc from detection (coordinated networks, information voids) through reception (who is exposed, and why) to consequence (causal health outcomes, political science-communication effects) and finally to remedy (AI-assisted correction), with Italian Facebook data, cross-national vaccine discourse, and US health-tracking studies each contributing a different observational lens on the same underlying problem: that problematic health information is neither uniformly viral nor randomly distributed, but structured by coordination, audience predisposition, and ecosystem-level supply gaps alike.
