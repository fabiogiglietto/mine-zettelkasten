# Health Misinformation & Fact-Checking

## Coordinated Networks and the Italian Infodemic

The methodological backbone of this topic is a line of work developed around Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) on Facebook, anchored in the Italian case. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] presents CooRnet itself, operationalizing Camille François's Actors-Behaviors-Content framework as an iterative detection cycle; [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] turns that tool into an applied fact-checking accelerator (the MINE-FACTS project), documenting how an Italian covid-skeptic network emerged from pre-existing political clusters and continuously adapted — link laundering, first-comment link hiding, religious-group infiltration — to evade moderation, and showing that the tool surfaced problematic content at roughly double the hit rate of routine manual fact-checking. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this corpus by tracing how an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster of alternative influencers, amplified through remediation by mainstream journalists and right-leaning outlets, became a disproportionately shareable vector for pandemic falsehoods, while [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] supplies the gatekeeping complement, showing that Google News Italia's aggregation choices interact with topic-level emotionality (celebrity health news vs. technical science reporting) to determine what travels on Facebook regardless of source credibility. Together these four papers sketch a layered Italian ecosystem — algorithmic aggregation, coordinated amplification, and influencer remediation — that fact-checkers must contend with simultaneously, not as isolated failure points.

This CLSB paradigm generalizes beyond Italy. [[Song2025-yh]] applies comparable coordination analysis to UK/US vaccine discourse, finding that coordination itself is ideologically agnostic — it can scaffold institutional pro-vaccine messaging as readily as anti-vaccine conspiracy — while national political culture shapes framing (safety concerns in the UK, individual-freedom rhetoric in the US). [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the actor-mapping approach into the Twitter/news interface, showing a contrarian-dominated coordinated network that amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts and precedes rather than follows news coverage, mirroring the "small persistent cluster, outsized influence" pattern seen in the Italian studies. [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] offers a complementary lens on expert-level polarization, demonstrating that UK newspapers' selective citation of GBD versus JSM signatories reproduces political cleavages through legitimate scientific disagreement rather than outright misinformation — a useful boundary case for distinguishing coordinated disinformation from ordinary politicized science communication.

## Does Exposure Translate into Harm? Quantifying Impact

A second cluster interrogates whether circulating misinformation actually changes minds or outcomes, often with sobering findings about scale. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] both show that exposure to untrustworthy sources is low on average and sharply concentrated in small subpopulations (older adults, heavy consumers of dubious political news), with little evidence of attitudinal shift from the modal user's perspective. [[Allen2024-av]] complicates the picture further: fact-check-flagged misinformation is more persuasive per view but reaches few people, whereas unflagged "vaccine-skeptical" content — often from mainstream outlets — drives a 46-fold larger aggregate effect on hesitancy, suggesting veracity-focused interventions address only a sliver of the harm. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] takes the opposite tack, building a causal epidemiological model (SIRVA) to estimate that antivaccine tweets produced measurable, if modest, downstream cases and deaths — a rare bridge from online speech to offline epidemic outcomes. Read together with [[Scalco2026-bd]], which shows that "information voids" (demand outstripping credible supply) are themselves associated with higher misinformation prevalence around vaccine milestones, this cluster argues for a supply-and-demand, exposure-weighted epistemology of harm rather than a simple content-flagging logic.

## From Manual Debunking to AI-Assisted Fact-Checking

A third strand addresses the fact-checking workflow itself, motivated by the same volume problem that CooRnet was built to solve. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] stress-tests large language models on PolitiFact claims, finding that raw model knowledge and even web search are unreliable, but that curated retrieval-augmented generation built on professional fact-check summaries yields dramatic gains — underscoring that the bottleneck is access to curated evidence, not model capability. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] both explore generative AI as a persuasive corrective rather than a classifier: personalized GPT-4 dialogues durably reduce conspiracy belief by engaging with each believer's specific evidence, and an LLM-powered "conversational inoculation" chatbot outperforms static reading/writing refutations once baseline susceptibility is controlled for. [[Ramos2026-qo]], from Maldita.es, argues from the Valencia flood case that claim-by-claim fact-checking is structurally too slow for crisis-era narrative mutation, proposing narrative monitoring and argument-checking (targeting the fallacious warrants underlying claim families) as necessary complements — a conceptual bridge back to [[Lieu2025-nl]], whose experimental test of the FLICC fallacy taxonomy finds that content category (attacks on climate *solutions*), not fallacy type, drives perceived veracity and polarization, suggesting argument-checking must be topic-sensitive rather than purely logic-form-based.

Institutional questions about fact-checking's legitimacy and tone round out this strand. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend platform-funded partnerships against charges of compromised independence, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows empirically that Meta's post-verification actions rarely amount to removal, undercutting Zuckerberg's censorship framing and revealing fact-checkers' own preference for labeling and counter-speech over deletion. [[Xue2025-bp]] adds a message-design angle, finding that fact-checks themselves are often emotionally charged rather than neutrally "factual," complicating fact-checking's normative self-image as dispassionate correction.

## Psychological Mechanisms, Inoculation, and Behavioral Interventions

A fourth cluster examines why misinformation takes hold and how prebunking/inoculation can intervene before exposure occurs. [[Adam2026-tz]] provides the integrative media-plus-predisposition model, showing conspiracy belief emerges from a "marriage" of alternative-media contagion, mainstream-media mitigation, and selective engagement/avoidance driven by populism and political mistrust — a framework into which the exposure-concentration findings of [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] fit naturally. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that brief inoculation videos scale cheaply as Instagram ads with durable five-month effects, extending lab-based inoculation theory into real platform infrastructure, while [[Szabo2026-rd]] (again) and [[Costello2024-bg]] push inoculation toward conversational, adaptive delivery. [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] shifts focus to downstream social consequences, showing that influencer-sourced misinformation (versus identical content from ordinary users) generates more frequent and distinct toxicity, mediated by credibility-as-legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment" — a reminder that correcting content is only part of managing misinformation's social fallout.

## Democratic Stakes Beyond Health

Finally, two companion studies on Brazil's 2022 election extend the topic's logic from health to electoral misinformation, testing whether the same dynamics of belief, media pathway, and trust erosion travel across domains. [[Rossini2026-jn]] finds that belief in electoral fraud claims increases political intolerance over time, with messaging-app news use indirectly fueling this via misinformation belief, while [[Rossini2026-mj]] shows mainstream news consumption stabilizes trust in election results even among "losing" voters, whereas messaging-app use and misinformation belief erode it — particularly among supporters of an illiberal incumbent. Read against the health-focused papers, these studies suggest that the structural mechanisms identified in the COVID corpus — coordinated amplification, platform-specific pathways, selective exposure along predispositional lines, and the stabilizing role of professional journalism — are not health-specific but generalizable features of how networked misinformation erodes shared reality, whatever the domain.
