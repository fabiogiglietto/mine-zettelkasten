# Health Misinformation & Fact-Checking

## Coordinated Networks and Nefarious Actors: Mapping the Terrain

The empirical core of this collection is a set of studies that treat coordination itself — not just content — as the unit of analysis. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the anchor: the MINE-FACTS project extends the CooRnet coordinated-link-sharing-behavior (CLSB) methodology to map an Italian covid-skeptic network that emerged from pre-existing political clusters (League, Five Star Movement) and documents its evasion tactics — link laundering, first-comment link hiding, cross-posting through image macros, and colonization of religious and community groups. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] picks up exactly where this leaves off, following the *content* rather than the accounts: it shows how an "Alternative Influence Network," anchored by an Intellectual Dark Web cluster, is amplified less by fringe actors than by legacy Italian journalists and right-leaning newspapers, who "remediate" TV and print statements from doctors and intellectuals into shareable Facebook posts that dodge fact-checking because the quoted statements were genuinely made. Together these two Italian studies establish a two-level architecture of nefarious circulation: a coordinated behavioral layer (accounts/pages/groups) and a legitimating media layer (journalists, outlets) that launders fringe claims into mainstream visibility.

[[Song2025-yh]] internationalizes and complicates this picture by comparing CLSB on pro- and anti-vaccine sides in the UK and US, showing that coordination is not intrinsically malicious: anti-vaccine networks disseminate disproportionately unverifiable content, but pro-vaccine coordination (NHS, CDC) uses the identical structural signature to scale credible communication — and that national political culture (safety skepticism in the UK vs. individual-freedom framing in the US) shapes which narratives cohere. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] extends coordinated-network detection from Facebook to Twitter/X and from health claims broadly to *scientific papers* specifically, finding a small, non-automated, contrarian-dominated coordination structure that amplifies credentialed anti-consensus experts rather than the platform's most popular voices, and that news coverage of these papers tends to *follow* rather than precede this superspreader activity — a finding that inverts the assumption that legacy media independently discover and validate fringe science. Where the Italian studies emphasize media remediation as the amplification vector, Efstratiou's tracing of Twitter-to-news lag times pinpoints platform superspreaders, not journalists, as the upstream node. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes the network-mapping tradition toward causal inference, coupling a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model with geolocated antivaccine tweets to estimate that Twitter antivaccine content caused a measurable, if modest, number of vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths — providing a mechanistic bridge between the descriptive network studies above and offline public-health outcomes.

## Gatekeepers, Media Ecosystems, and the Supply-Demand of (Mis)Information

A second cluster asks not who coordinates disinformation but how the broader information ecosystem structurally enables or constrains its circulation. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examines Google News Italia as an algorithmic gatekeeper for health news, finding that while institutional/scientific topics dominate the aggregated agenda, Facebook engagement skews toward emotionally charged "popular" categories (celebrity health, malfunction scandals) — engagement is driven by topic valence more than source credibility, prefiguring the amplification logics later studies formalize. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes a complementary structural concept, the "information void," quantifying moments when public demand for vaccine information outstrips reliable supply across six European countries; these voids, triggered by events like EMA authorizations or the AstraZeneca suspension, empirically correlate with a drop in high-credibility content and a rise in misinformation share — giving Ducci's gatekeeping observations a dynamic, event-triggered mechanism. [[Adam2026-tz]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]] then move from the supply side to actual audience exposure, using web-tracking rather than self-report. Adam et al. find that mainstream media themselves circulate substantial conspiracy-supporting COVID content (contagion), even as their debunking exerts a real, if modest, mitigating effect, filtered through populist selective engagement and avoidance. Lyons et al. show, in the health domain specifically, that exposure to low-credibility content is rare in aggregate but starkly concentrated — disproportionately among older adults, and driven by habitual cross-site referral chains rather than social media or search, diverging notably from the political-misinformation literature's algorithmic-amplification narrative. Read together, these four papers suggest that "supply," "voids," and "exposure" are distinct, only loosely coupled layers of the ecosystem — a plurality of mechanisms that any single coordinated-network map risks flattening.

## From Slow Fact-Checking to Faster Workflows: Institutional Responses

A distinct thread turns from description to intervention design at the institutional level. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] again is pivotal here in its applied register: its content-agnostic CLSB tool, tested with Italian fact-checkers at Facta.news, surfaced problematic content at a rate (~40%) far higher than routine third-party fact-checking baselines (~28%), operationalizing the promise that network signals can speed up — not replace — professional verification. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examines what happens *after* fact-checking, empirically rebutting Meta's framing of fact-checkers as censors: removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases, and fact-checkers themselves favor labeling and counter-speech over deletion, remaining skeptical that Community Notes can substitute for professional verification — a sobering institutional counterpoint to techno-solutionist workflow proposals. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written by practitioners at Maldita.es, argues from the Valencia flood case that claim-level fact-checking is structurally too slow for crisis disinformation, proposing narrative monitoring (tracking clustered claims as evolving units) and argument-checking (targeting the logical/rhetorical scaffolding of claims) as necessary complements — a direct methodological echo of the fact-checking-speed problem [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] first diagnosed, now generalized beyond health to any crisis narrative. [[Xue2025-bp]] adds a message-design lens to this institutional cluster, showing that fact-checks themselves are far from the dispassionate genre they claim to be, routinely deploying emotional language that shapes engagement and audience sentiment toward the fact-checked target — raising the uncomfortable possibility that effective fact-checking workflows may need to strategically appropriate the very emotional tactics used by the misinformation they combat.

## Beyond Debunking: Inoculation, Dialogue, and the Limits of Correction

A fourth thread shifts from institutional workflows to individual-level correction and prebunking, testing whether resistance can be built before exposure or belief reversed after it. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] demonstrates that a 19-second inoculation video, deployed at scale as an Instagram ad, durably improves recognition of fearmongering months later — evidence that lab-validated inoculation theory travels into real feeds. [[Szabo2026-rd]] innovates on the delivery mechanism, showing that LLM-mediated *conversational* inoculation outperforms static reading/writing exercises once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for, because dialogue can adapt to a user's specific objections — a design principle that [[Costello2024-bg]] validates at a much larger and more consequential scale: three-round GPT-4 dialogues tailored to each participant's specific conspiracy evidence produced a ~20% durable reduction in belief that generalized to unrelated conspiracies, challenging motivational accounts that treat conspiracist belief as impervious to counter-evidence. [[Lieu2025-nl]], working in the adjacent domain of climate misinformation but methodologically relevant, complicates the logic-checking premise underlying [[Ramos2026-qo]]'s argument-checking proposal: it finds that the specific rhetorical *fallacy* used in a false claim matters far less for perceived veracity than the claim's *content category*, suggesting that argument-checking strategies may need to be content-sensitive rather than purely logic-form-based to be effective.

## Source, Emotion, and Toxicity: Why Misinformation Sticks

Finally, [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] closes the loop between network-level amplification and audience-level affect by showing that the *source* of misinformation — influencer versus ordinary user — independently shapes not just spread but the toxicity of downstream discourse, with influencer-driven misinformation generating more homogeneous, echo-chamber-like hostility via credibility-legitimation and parasocial "community enmeshment" mechanisms, especially around sociopolitical (including health/safety-adjacent) content. This dovetails with the Italian AIN work ([[Marino2023-9137f448]]) on why particular high-profile amplifiers matter disproportionately, and with Xue's finding that emotionality is not incidental but strategic — together suggesting that any fact-checking workflow optimized purely for factual accuracy, without attention to source credibility signals and affective framing, will underestimate both the speed and stickiness of coordinated health misinformation as it moves from covert networks (Section 1) through porous gatekeepers (Section 2) into publics that institutional correction (Sections 3–4) can only partially reach.
