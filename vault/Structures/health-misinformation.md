# Health Misinformation & Fact-Checking

## Circulation and coordination: mapping the Italian COVID infodemic

The empirical backbone of this collection is a sequence of Italy-focused studies that treat coordinated behaviour, rather than content falsity alone, as the primary signal of problematic health information. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] formalises Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) via the CooRnet tool, operationalising François's Actors-Behaviors-Content framework as an iterative detection cycle; [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] builds directly on this infrastructure for the MINE-FACTS project, tracing how an Italian covid-skeptic network emerged from pre-existing political clusters and documenting evasion tactics (link laundering, first-comment hiding, religious-group infiltration) that a content-agnostic, actor/behaviour-centred tool can catch faster than manual fact-checking. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this actor-mapping lens outward from accounts to the public figures they amplify, showing that an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster and legacy-media remediation of expert soundbites are key engines of problematic narrative circulation—linking coordinated-network research to hybrid-media-system theory. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] complements this actor-centred view with a gatekeeping lens, showing that Google News Italia's algorithmic aggregation shapes which health topics reach Facebook, and that engagement is driven more by emotional topicality than source credibility—an early diagnosis of the attention dynamics that later misinformation gains exploit. [[Scalco2026-bd]] then supplies a demand-side complement to this supply-side tradition, operationalising "information voids" as quantifiable mismatches between information demand and supply and showing empirically that such voids coincide with lower-credibility content and higher misinformation share around vaccine milestones—effectively formalising the mechanism (unmet demand invites junk supply) that the coordinated-network studies describe qualitatively.

## From exposure to behaviour: does misinformation actually move outcomes?

A second cluster asks the harder causal question: given that problematic content circulates, how much does it matter for real-world vaccination behaviour and health outcomes? [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] offers the most ambitious causal architecture, embedding geolocated antivaccine tweets into a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model to estimate a lower-bound causal effect on US vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths. [[Allen2024-av]] arrives at a complementary but more unsettling conclusion: combining survey experiments with exposure data at Facebook scale, it finds that *unflagged* but vaccine-skeptical mainstream content—not fact-check-flagged misinformation—drove the overwhelming majority of aggregate harm, because persuasiveness without reach is inert. Together these two papers push back against an "infodemic" framing that equates virality with harm, insisting instead on the joint necessity of exposure and persuasive effect. [[Lyons2026-ca]] sharpens the exposure side of this equation: pairing survey and web-tracking data, it shows that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in aggregate but concentrated among older adults and habitual cross-domain consumers, arriving via referral chains from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media—a finding that complicates platform-centred mitigation strategies focused on social feeds. [[Song2025-yh]] returns to the coordinated-actor paradigm established in the Italian cluster but applies it cross-nationally (UK/US) and bidirectionally, showing that coordinated link-sharing is not inherently nefarious: anti-vaccine CLSB skews toward unverifiable content while pro-vaccine CLSB mobilises credible institutional sources, with national political culture (safety scepticism in the UK vs. individual-freedom framing in the US) shaping the narrative content coordination carries.

## Psychological undercurrents: why beliefs form and persist

Underlying these circulation and impact studies is a psychological question about belief formation that [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Adam2026-tz]] address from complementary angles. Pennycook and Rand's review reframes susceptibility to false news as primarily a failure of analytic reasoning and knowledge rather than partisan motivation, and documents the belief–sharing gap driven by inattention—a foundational account that the collection's intervention studies (below) implicitly build on. [[Adam2026-tz]] operationalises a parallel "information-plus-predispositions" model for conspiracy beliefs specifically, using panel-and-web-tracking data to show that alternative media produce contagion effects while mainstream media produce mitigation effects, but that populist predispositions channel both selective engagement and counterarguing/backfire—demonstrating that media ecology and individual psychology jointly, not separately, explain conspiracy uptake. [[Lieu2025-nl]] extends the content-side half of this picture into climate misinformation, finding that rhetorical fallacy type matters far less than the substantive content category being attacked (solutions and scientists being the most polarising targets)—a result that resonates with the later argument-checking proposals discussed below.

## Beyond the single correction: inoculation, dialogue, and narrative-level response

A third thread asks what should be done once problematic claims are identified, moving past static debunking toward more adaptive correction. [[Costello2024-bg]] shows that personalised, multi-turn LLM dialogues can durably (≥2 months) reduce conspiracy belief by directly engaging each believer's specific evidence, challenging motivational accounts of conspiracy persistence in favour of analytic-reasoning accounts aligned with [[Pennycook2021-jq]]. [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalises a similar conversational logic for health misinformation specifically, finding that an LLM chatbot ("Forty") outperforms static reading/writing inoculation once individual baseline susceptibility is controlled for, with trust-building and adaptability identified as the mechanism. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from inside a fact-checking organisation covering the 2024 Valencia floods, argues from practice that claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with evolving, emotionally resonant disinformation narratives, proposing narrative monitoring and argument-checking (testing warrants and fallacies rather than individual facts) as necessary complements—a proposal that converges with [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s finding that content/argument structure, not fallacy type alone, drives persuasiveness.

## Scaling and legitimising fact-checking: institutions, platforms, and AI tools

The final arc turns to the fact-checking apparatus itself—its institutional legitimacy and its technical future. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend financial dependence on platform companies, revealing a field whose boundaries and professional identity remain contested and emergent. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] empirically tests the premise behind Meta's 2025 abandonment of third-party fact-checking, showing that content removal was always rare and that fact-checkers themselves favour transparency and counter-speech over deletion, casting doubt on both censorship narratives and Community Notes as a replacement. [[Xue2025-bp]] complicates fact-checking's self-image further by showing that fact-checking posts themselves are often emotionally charged, troubling the field's claim to dispassionate objectivity. On the technical frontier, [[DeVerna2025-dl]] evaluates LLMs as fact-checking engines directly, finding that raw model knowledge and even web search are poor substitutes for curated retrieval—echoing the broader finding across this topic (from CooRnet to information-void detection) that *good tooling requires curated, structured inputs*, not generic capability. Read together with [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Slack1783507719-slc1]]'s actor-mapping tools, this closes the collection's arc: from detecting where problematic health information clusters (coordination, gatekeeping, voids), through measuring whether it matters (exposure, causal impact), to understanding why people believe it (reasoning, media ecology) and what interventions and institutional infrastructures—human and algorithmic—can plausibly keep pace.
