# Health Misinformation & Fact-Checking

## The Behavioral Turn: Coordinated Networks as a Fact-Checking Signal

A substantial cluster of papers filed here treats *coordination*, rather than content, as the diagnostic signal for problematic health information. [[Slack1783507719-slc1]] lays out the methodological backbone — the CooRnet tool for detecting Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB) on Facebook, operationalized as an iterative Actors-Behaviors-Content cycle. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] turns this infrastructure into an applied fact-checking accelerator for the Italian outlet Facta.news, documenting how a covid-skeptic coordinated network emerged from pre-existing political clusters and evolved tactics (first-comment link placement, "link laundering" through reputable outlets, infiltration of religious groups) specifically to evade platform enforcement — and showing that the resulting content-agnostic tool surfaces problematic posts at roughly 40% hit rate versus ~28% for routine manual fact-checking. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends this same Italian dataset forward, following the coordinated network's output into an "Alternative Influence Network" of Intellectual-Dark-Web-style figures whose share-heavy engagement patterns are amplified precisely through legacy media remediation — journalists and right-leaning newspapers remain the central nodes even for ostensibly "alternative" narratives. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] complements this by examining the upstream gatekeeping layer, Google News Italia, showing that while institutional, "scientific" health topics dominate the aggregated agenda, it is emotionally charged celebrity and malfunction stories that actually drive Facebook engagement — a structural precondition for the kind of hyperpartisan amplification the CLSB papers document downstream.

[[Song2025-yh]] broadens this coordination lens comparatively, showing that CLSB is not intrinsically malicious: anti-vaccine and pro-vaccine communities both coordinate, but with starkly different credibility profiles and national inflections (UK anti-vaccine coordination clusters around safety skepticism, US anti-vaccine coordination around individual-freedom framing). [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the coordination paradigm into a different platform and a different register of "misinformation" — not CLSB on Facebook but a coordinated retweet network on Twitter that amplifies a narrow set of credentialed *contrarian* scientists. Read alongside the CLSB-Italy cluster, it suggests that the manipulation of amplification signals (rather than fabrication of falsehoods outright) is a general strategy across platforms and contexts, and that coordinated networks matter partly because they shape what legacy/news media subsequently cover — Efstratiou's finding that news coverage *follows* superspreader activity echoes the remediation dynamics Marino traces in Italy.

## From Exposure to Causal Harm

A second thread asks the harder causal question these coordination studies mostly bracket: does exposure to this material actually move health behavior and outcomes? [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] builds a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets, arriving at a causally-grounded (if modest) estimate of vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths attributable to Twitter antivaccine content. [[Allen2024-av]] delivers a more unsettling corrective at far larger scale: combining survey experiments with crowdsourced persuasion scores across 13,000+ Facebook URLs, it finds that fact-check-flagged misinformation, while more persuasive per view, reached so few people that *unflagged* vaccine-skeptical content — frequently from mainstream outlets — had roughly 46 times the aggregate impact on hesitancy. Together these papers complicate the implicit assumption underlying much of the coordination-detection literature above: that malicious, fringe-identifiable actors are the primary locus of harm. Allen's finding that veracity itself is a weak predictor of persuasive damage (compared to whether content implies vaccines are harmful) also sits in tension with — and arguably motivates — the move toward argument- and narrative-level interventions discussed below, rather than purely veracity-based fact-checking.

## Who Is Exposed, and Through What Media Diet

A third set of studies interrogates the demand side: not what circulates, but who encounters it and why. [[Adam2026-tz]] uses linked panel-and-web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and predisposition — populists selectively engage alternative media and selectively avoid (or counter-argue against) mainstream debunking, producing both contagion and backfire pathways. [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds an analogous but distinct concentration dynamic in the health domain specifically: exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in aggregate but heavily concentrated among older adults with poorer information discernment, arriving not via search or social media referral but via a self-reinforcing network of other low-credibility sites — a cross-domain "habitual consumption" pattern rather than incidental platform exposure. [[Scalco2026-bd]] supplies a structural, ecosystem-level account of *why* such content finds an audience at all: by modeling "information voids" as anomalous demand-supply imbalances across social media and search, it shows that periods of unmet informational demand around real events (vaccine rollouts, safety scares) are precisely when credible content share drops and misinformation prevalence rises — offering a mechanistic bridge between the audience-level findings of Adam and Lyons and the supply-side coordination dynamics documented in the CLSB cluster.

## Beyond the Claim: Narratives, Arguments, and Fallacies

Several papers argue that claim-by-claim fact-checking is structurally overmatched by how health (and adjacent) disinformation actually propagates. [[Ramos2026-qo]], drawing on Maldita.es's real-time response to the 2024 Valencia floods, argues for supplementing fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argument-checking, since corrected claims regress and narratives mutate faster than discrete debunks can track — disinformation functions as an evolving explanatory framework, not an atomized set of falsehoods. [[Lieu2025-nl]] offers a complementary experimental test of the argument-level premise in the climate domain: crossing the content-based CARDS taxonomy with the logic-based FLICC fallacy taxonomy, it finds that *which claim is made* (e.g., attacking solutions) matters far more for perceived veracity and polarization than *which logical fallacy underlies it* — a finding that qualifies Ramos's argument-checking proposal by suggesting fallacy-type alone may be a weak lever, while content category and audience predisposition (again echoing Adam2026-tz) do the heavy lifting.

## Automating and Augmenting Correction

A final cluster evaluates emerging tools for intervention and verification. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] stress-tests the premise that LLMs might themselves serve as fact-checkers, finding that off-the-shelf models — even reasoning- and search-augmented ones — perform poorly on political claims unless given curated retrieval context, a caution that applies directly to any plan (implicit in several papers above) to scale up automated verification of health claims. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] instead point toward LLMs as *dialogic* correction and inoculation agents rather than verification engines: Costello shows that personalized, evidence-dense AI conversation durably reduces conspiracy belief (including COVID-related conspiracies) across topics and persists for months, directly challenging motivational accounts of belief entrenchment that might otherwise seem to license the backfire effects documented in Adam2026-tz; Szabo's MindFort system operationalizes a related idea as prophylaxis rather than cure, showing that conversational "inoculation" chatbots outperform static reading/writing inoculation once individual susceptibility is controlled for. Read together, this final group suggests a trajectory for the field: from behavioral detection of coordinated spread (Giglietto, Slack1783507719, Marino, Song, Efstratiou), through causal and audience-level accounting of actual harm (Bollenbacher, Allen, Lyons, Adam, Scalco), toward argument- and dialogue-level interventions (Ramos, Lieu, Costello, Szabo) that treat health misinformation as a persuasive and narrative phenomenon requiring responses beyond the single-claim fact-check that anchored the field's earliest tools (DeVerna2025-dl's sobering LLM benchmark included).
