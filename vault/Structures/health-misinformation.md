# Health Misinformation

## Coordinated networks and alternative-influence structures

The core empirical thread running through this collection is a shift from asking *what* misinformation says to asking *how it moves*—through which accounts, pages, and intermediary structures. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] establishes the methodological backbone for much of what follows: the MINE-FACTS project shows that Coordinated Link Sharing Behaviour (CLSB), detected via the content-agnostic CooRnet package, can surface an Italian covid-skeptic network faster than manual fact-checking, and documents the tactical repertoire—link laundering, first-comment link placement, religious-group infiltration—that such networks use to dodge platform enforcement. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] builds directly on this dataset, reclassifying the same coordinated accounts by the public figures they amplify and identifying an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster whose posts are disproportionately share-heavy (a signature the paper reads as a marker of hyperpartisan amplification, contrasted with comment-heavy, more contested journalist content). Crucially, Marino shows that legacy media are not bystanders but central relays: mainstream journalists and right-leaning newspapers remediate expert soundbites from TV into shareable covid-skeptic ammunition, blurring the boundary between "alternative" and "mainstream" media that other papers in this set also probe.

[[Song2025-yh]] extends CLSB analysis cross-nationally and cross-valence, comparing pro- and anti-vaccine coordinated Facebook communities in the UK and US. It complicates the assumption that coordination equals malfeasance: pro-vaccine networks coordinate too, circulating credible NHS/CDC content, while anti-vaccine coordination is denser in the UK (organized around safety skepticism) and more diffuse but freedom-framed in the US—demonstrating that CLSB's normative valence is contingent on national political culture rather than an intrinsic property of the technique itself. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] transposes the coordination lens onto Twitter and the circulation of COVID-19 scientific papers, finding an almost entirely contrarian coordinated retweet network that elevates a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts—not the platform's most popular accounts—and showing that news coverage of these papers tends to follow, not lead, superspreader activity, with high- and low-trust outlets tracking conformist and contrarian superspreaders respectively. Together, these four papers sketch a layered picture: platform-level coordination tactics, alternative-influencer clusters, and legacy/news media form an interlocking relay system, not a single point of origin—supporting the "Alternative Influence Network" and hybrid-media-system framing that recurs across the group.

## From correlation to causality: does exposure change behavior?

A second arc interrogates whether documented exposure to problematic content actually moves vaccination behavior—pushing past network mapping toward causal inference. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] builds a compartmental SIRVA epidemic model fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets, US county vaccination rates, and case/death data, estimating that antivaccine content caused roughly 14,000 vaccine refusals and a measurable, if modest, uptick in cases and deaths—explicitly bridging the gap between exposure experiments and epidemic outcomes. [[Allen2024-av]] takes a more skeptical, decompositional stance, arguing that impact requires *both* exposure and persuasive influence, and that fact-check-flagged misinformation, while more persuasive per view, reached so few users that its aggregate effect was dwarfed—by a factor of roughly 46—by unflagged but "vaccine-skeptical" content, much of it from mainstream outlets like local news and the Chicago Tribune. Read against each other, these two papers do not simply disagree; they highlight different loci of harm. Bollenbacher's platform-specific antivaccine tweet networks and Allen's mainstream-adjacent skeptical content both matter, but Allen's finding reframes the "infodemic" narrative that motivates most of the coordination-detection work above: veracity-focused interventions such as fact-check labels target only a sliver of the content plausibly driving hesitancy, since much of the persuasive damage comes from technically true but harm-implying stories circulating outside any coordinated or low-credibility network at all.

## The information ecosystem: supply, demand, and gatekeeping

A third cluster treats misinformation not as a discrete artifact to be flagged but as an emergent property of supply-demand imbalances in the broader information environment. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids"—moments when public demand (Wikipedia views, Google Trends) outstrips reliable supply (Facebook, Twitter, news)—and shows these voids, triggered by events like vaccine roll-outs or the AstraZeneca suspension, correlate with a measurable drop in high-credibility content and a rise in misinformation share, giving mechanistic teeth to previously qualitative "data void" concepts. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examines the pre-pandemic gatekeeping layer itself, showing that Google News Italia's aggregation choices and Facebook engagement patterns are driven less by source credibility than by topic affect: emotionally charged categories like celebrity health stories draw outsized comment and share volume relative to the more prevalent but less "sticky" scientific and healthcare-system news—a dynamic that helps explain why voids and skeptic narratives can outcompete institutional content even when the latter is more abundant. [[Adam2026-tz]] closes this ecosystem loop from the audience side: combining panel survey and web-tracking data in Germany/Switzerland, it shows that alternative media exposure produces contagion effects while mainstream debunking produces mitigation effects on average, but that populist and mistrustful users selectively engage with the former and avoid (or counterargue against) the latter—meaning the same mainstream "correction" content that fills an information void for most users can backfire precisely among those most predisposed to conspiracy belief.

## Audiences and the long tail of concentrated exposure

Finally, [[Lyons2026-ca]] shifts the analytic focus from network structure to audience concentration, using passive web-tracking to show that exposure to low-credibility health content in the US is rare in aggregate but heavily concentrated among a small, older, more conspiracist-leaning subset of users, arriving via referrals from other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media. This finding dovetails with Scalco's void dynamics and Adam's selective-exposure mechanism: misinformation ecosystems are not primarily aggregate media effects but concentrated, habitual, cross-domain consumption patterns among predisposed subpopulations—a conclusion that pushes the coordination-detection and gatekeeping literatures above toward a more audience-centered account of *who* falls into the pathways these networks construct, and why platform-level interventions aimed at content veracity (as Allen also argues) may miss both the mainstream-adjacent skeptical content and the small, already-committed audience segments driving most real-world exposure.
