# Platform Data Access & Research Methods

## The Closing Aperture: Data Access as the Field's Constitutive Problem

The papers filed here converge on a single structural fact: the empirical study of social media is downstream of decisions made by platforms and, increasingly, by regulators. [[Yang2026-tq]] supplies the macro-level evidence—social-media-based social science tripled between 2010 and 2022, then plateaued and declined in 2022–2024, precisely tracking the API restrictions that this literature otherwise treats qualitatively. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] gives the single-platform version of this story in devastating detail, tabulating over 33,000 Twitter studies and showing a 13% drop in output in 2024 alone after Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. Bastos2025-ya and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] narrate the same collapse historically, the latter periodizing platform access writ large into a "Wild West," a brief "Golden Age" of sanctioned academic APIs, and a current "walled garden" era of commercial paywalls—arguing that this trajectory is structural across platforms (Meta's CrowdTangle, Reddit's pricing, TikTok's throttling) rather than idiosyncratic to Musk's Twitter/X. [[Freelon2024-sc]] formalizes this into a periodized taxonomy of access regimes (laissez-faire, academic, walled-garden, pay-to-play, unofficial) and shows that platforms under the most public scrutiny impose the tightest controls—precisely the platforms researchers most want to study.

## Article 40 and the DSA: Regulation as Contested Remedy

A second cluster treats the EU Digital Services Act, and Article 40 in particular, as the field's central regulatory hope and its central regulatory disappointment. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame the DSA's vetted-researcher provisions as a genuine, if turbulent, qualitative shift from platform philanthropy to binding legal right—de Vreese and Tromble explicitly arguing that current friction (delays, narrow eligibility, legal ambiguity) signals the regulation biting into entrenched power asymmetries rather than failing. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] document the practical mechanics and bottlenecks of this regime from the inside—misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, a "circular resource problem" disadvantaging early-career researchers, and a conspicuous regulatory blind spot around LLMs. [[Peters2026-mo]] zooms into one specific fault line: how "data quality" itself became a politically contested term across EU consultation processes, with platforms (notably Meta) strategically inverting researchers' quality arguments to claim access would be futile anyway. [[Lukito2026-nb]] and Schulde2026-df—more precisely [[Schulte2026-df]]—extend this critique empirically, cataloguing 72 access tools and a live German-election case study respectively to show that price, proficiency, and permission jointly gatekeep who can do election research, and that platform observability itself should be treated as reusable infrastructure rather than an artifact of one-off scraping projects.

## Life Inside and After the Walled Garden

A closely related set of papers examines what happens once access is granted through platform-controlled channels. [[Cullen2026-cb]] theorizes CrowdTangle's design and governance as an epistemic mediator—"seeing like an API"—showing how technical constraints shaped not just what could be collected but what could be concluded, a diagnosis rendered newly urgent by the tool's 2024 shutdown. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this into the successor regime of "clean rooms" (Meta Content Library, ProQuest TDM Studio), arguing these environments privilege code-literate, quantitative researchers at Global North institutions while foreclosing qualitative, longitudinal, and cross-platform work by design. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] supply granular audits of ostensibly DSA-compliant research APIs—TikTok's and YouTube's respectively—finding that YouTube's search endpoint is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply after 20–60 days, undermining any claim that these tools meet systemic-risk research obligations. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Jurg2025-ur]] apply DSA transparency mandates themselves as data, using moderator-allocation disclosures and removal-notice audits to reveal linguistic and algorithmic inequities (e.g., Twitter/X moderators for Portuguese receiving 9% of English's allocation) that platforms' own compliance gestures leave invisible.

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Independent Evidence

A more polemical thread argues that data dependency is not merely inconvenient but epistemically corrupting. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] draw explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science, cataloguing mechanisms—selective funding, design bias, performative collaboration, suppressed internal research—by which platforms shape what gets studied and published. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this claim directly: roughly half of high-profile social media papers in Science, Nature, and PNAS carry disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, with an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" once editors and reviewers are included, and industry-tied work skewing toward user-blame topics (misinformation sharing) rather than platform-scale dynamics. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] is the paradigm case under discussion throughout this cluster—the Meta-academic 2020 election studies—cited repeatedly ([[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Allen2025-ot]], [[Pierri2025-hm]]) both as evidence that platform-mediated collaboration can produce rigorous findings (its careful decomposition of CIB versus financially-motivated networks, and its caution about causal claims from exposure data) and as a cautionary tale about undisclosed algorithmic changes mid-study.

## Methodological Workarounds: Platform-Independent and Infrastructure-Building Responses

Against this backdrop, a substantial group of papers builds tools that route around platform gatekeeping entirely. [[Allen2025-ot]] frames Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension-plus-LLM reranking method as exemplary of "platform-independent" experimentation—a productive middle ground between lab studies and rare platform collaborations like FIES. [[Zheng2026-bi]] (TubeStats/TokStats) and [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] (CooRTweet) pursue a parallel logic at the infrastructure level: exploiting platform architecture (video ID indexing; a minimal, object-agnostic definition of coordination) to build durable, cross-platform tools resilient to API volatility. [[Gerard2025-br]], [[Bruns2025-fz]], and [[Iannucci2025-eg]] extend this platform-agnosticism to network analysis itself, proposing narrative-cluster embeddings, "practice mapping," and multiplex temporal models that require only timestamped interaction data, sidestepping platform-specific metadata. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] complicates any workaround premised on scraping, showing that ex-situ collection introduces ~34% content disparity independent of time delay—a sobering methodological check on tools that assume scraped content faithfully represents what users actually saw.

## Measurement Validity Once Data Is In Hand

A final cluster interrogates whether platform-provided or platform-adjacent datasets, once obtained, actually measure what researchers assume. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows that Meta's URL Shares Dataset's 100-share anonymization threshold produces suspiciously uniform trends across 46 countries—an algorithmic artifact, likely tied to Feed-ranking changes, that can overwhelm genuine cross-national signal; [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] uses the same dataset cautiously, explicitly restricting comparison to Europe because of this instability. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit of NewsGuard, finding continuous trustworthiness scores reasonably stable since 2022 but binary classifications highly sensitive to single sources crossing threshold. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] push into the algorithm itself as an object of inference—showing Facebook's News Feed leaves detectable temporal fingerprints of ranking changes, and that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' political ideology—both arguing that outside audit can partially pierce the "black box" framing platforms invite. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] reframes referral-based misinformation measurement entirely, showing that a "direct referrer" paradigm undercounts platform effects by 1.5–15x relative to a counterfactual "embedded referrer" model, a caution that applies equally to CrowdTangle-era and post-CrowdTangle measurement strategies alike. Together with new computational tools for detecting coordination and manipulation under data scarcity—[[Yang2025-iv]], [[Di-Marco2025-aa]], [[Minici2024-tf]], [[Luceri2025-tr]], [[Mannocci2025-ig]]—these papers suggest that the post-API age is producing not a simpler research landscape but a more methodologically self-aware one, in which every dataset's provenance, threshold, and algorithmic mediation must be treated as substantive findings in their own right, not backgrounded infrastructure.
