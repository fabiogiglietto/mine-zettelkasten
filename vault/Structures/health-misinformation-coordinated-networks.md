# Health Misinformation and Coordinated Networks

## The architecture of coordinated amplification

The empirical core of this topic is the mapping of coordinated behavior that moves problematic health content faster, and further, than organic circulation would allow. [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] is the anchor text: the MINE-FACTS project builds a content-agnostic detection pipeline (CooRnet, Coordinated Link Sharing Behavior) explicitly to help Italian fact-checkers get ahead of the COVID-19 infodemic, showing that behavioral signals — synchronized posting, link-laundering through reputable outlets, links hidden in first comments, comment-to-share ratios — can surface problematic content at nearly 40% hit rates versus ~28% for routine fact-checking. This establishes coordination itself, independent of any single claim's truth value, as a diagnostic signal worth operationalizing. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] extends the same Italian covid-skeptic network forward in time, showing that the accounts flagged by CLSB methods don't merely recycle fringe material but function as an "Alternative Influence Network" whose highest-engagement content comes from an Intellectual Dark Web cluster and, crucially, from legacy journalists and right-leaning newspapers remediating expert soundbites from television. The lesson that emerges across both papers is that coordinated networks are parasitic on mainstream media rather than separate from it — they launder credibility by re-circulating fragments of legitimate journalism and expert speech.

[[Song2025-yh]] generalizes this coordination lens cross-nationally, comparing pro- and anti-vaccine CLSB on Facebook in the UK and US. It complicates the assumption that coordination equals malice: anti-vaccine coordinated networks are denser and share more "Unknown"/unverifiable content, but pro-vaccine coordination is equally coordinated, just institutionally anchored (NHS, CDC) — and the thematic content of anti-vaccine coordination differs by political culture (safety skepticism in the UK, individual-liberty framing in the US). Read alongside [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Marino2023-9137f448]], this positions CLSB as a *structural* feature of health discourse rather than a marker exclusive to disinformation — the diagnostic question becomes not "is this network coordinated" but "what is being coordinated, and toward what credibility profile." [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] pushes the same logic onto Twitter/X and the science-communication pipeline proper: a small, non-bot-driven coordinated retweet network almost entirely amplifies contrarian credentialed experts, who are retweeted far out of proportion to their general popularity, and this amplification measurably precedes and shapes which preprints low-trust news outlets pick up. Together these four papers trace an arc from *detecting* coordination (MINE-FACTS) to *characterizing* its content and credibility asymmetries (Marino, Song) to *tracing its downstream effects* on the broader news ecosystem (Efstratiou).

## From supply-and-demand to exposure: measuring the information environment

A second cluster asks not who is coordinating content but what the ambient information environment looks like, and who actually encounters its worst corners. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" — moments where public demand (Wikipedia, Google Trends) outstrips reliable supply (Facebook, Twitter, GDELT) — and shows empirically that these voids are measurably filled by lower-credibility content, giving the MINE-FACTS-style coordination work a demand-side complement: misinformation doesn't just get pushed by coordinated actors, it also rushes in to fill gaps left by information scarcity around major events (vaccine authorizations, suspensions). [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] offers an adjacent gatekeeping study, tracing how Google News Italia's aggregation choices shape what health content reaches Facebook, and showing that engagement is driven less by source credibility than by topic affect — celebrity and malfunction stories draw comments even though "medical science" dominates the aggregated agenda. Read together, Scalco and Ducci suggest two independent levers shaping exposure risk: temporal gaps in authoritative supply, and the gatekeeping/virality logics that reward emotionally salient framing over technical accuracy regardless of source.

[[Lyons2026-ca]] brings this to the level of individual behavioral traces, using web-tracking to show that exposure to low-credibility health content is rare in aggregate but extremely concentrated — a small set of older, conspiracist-leaning, politically right-skewing users account for the overwhelming majority of exposure, and crucially their exposure pathway runs through other low-credibility sites rather than search or social media referral. This is an important corrective to network-level findings: coordinated amplification and information voids describe the *supply* side, but Lyons shows the *demand* side is concentrated in habitual, cross-domain consumption clusters rather than incidental platform encounters — a finding that qualifies how much coordinated-network reach actually translates into individual exposure.

## Does exposure translate into harm? Causal and null findings

A third strand interrogates the causal step that the detection-and-mapping literature mostly assumes: does exposure to this content change beliefs or behavior at a population scale? [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] answers affirmatively via a compartmental epidemic model (SIRVA) fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets, estimating a causal chain from tweet exposure to vaccine refusal to excess cases and deaths — a rare attempt to close the loop from platform speech to epidemiological outcome. [[Allen2024-av]], by contrast, complicates the picture sharply: using large-scale survey experiments and crowdsourced persuasiveness ratings across 13,000+ vaccine-related URLs, it finds that *fact-check-flagged* misinformation, while more persuasive per view, had negligible aggregate impact because so few people saw it; the real driver of hesitancy was unflagged, technically-accurate-but-misleading "vaccine-skeptical" content from mainstream outlets, with 46-fold greater aggregate effect. This directly challenges the coordination/credibility framing of papers like [[Giglietto2022-0e951ac5]] and [[Song2025-yh]], which treat "Unknown"/unverified content as the chief concern: Allen's findings suggest the policy-relevant danger may lie precisely in content that passes veracity checks but still implies vaccines are harmful. The tension between Bollenbacher's tweet-driven causal estimate and Allen's mainstream-media-driven causal estimate is productive: both find measurable population-level harm, but locate its source in different parts of the ecosystem (fringe platform speech vs. mainstream-adjacent framing), suggesting multiple causal pathways operate simultaneously rather than a single dominant vector.

## Predispositions, polarization, and the boundary of legitimate disagreement

A final set of papers addresses the psychological and institutional conditions under which health/science misinformation takes hold, and where the boundary between misinformation and legitimate scientific disagreement actually lies. [[Adam2026-tz]] combines panel survey and web-tracking data to show that conspiracy belief formation is a "marriage" of media exposure and pre-existing populist/mistrustful predispositions, with both contagion (alternative media) and mitigation (mainstream debunking) effects, plus selective exposure and backfire pathways among high-mistrust individuals — directly relevant to understanding why coordinated amplification (as mapped by Giglietto, Marino, Efstratiou) finds receptive audiences in the first place. [[Lieu2025-nl]] narrows in on the content level, experimentally showing that *what* is claimed (content category, per the CARDS taxonomy) matters far more for perceived veracity and political polarization than *how* it is logically malformed (FLICC fallacy type) — a finding with implications for how fact-checking and "logic-checking" interventions should be prioritized.

[[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] serves as a crucial boundary case for the whole topic: it documents UK newspapers' polarized citation of GBD versus JSM-signatory scientists, and explicitly cautions that the scientists involved are mainstream researchers engaged in legitimate scientific disagreement, not the fringe actors populating the coordinated networks elsewhere in this corpus — a reminder that media polarization around COVID science is not synonymous with misinformation, even when its *pattern* (ideological sorting, bubble formation in co-authorship and sharing networks) superficially resembles coordinated network structures. [[Marwick2025-vx]] closes the arc by shifting from acute health misinformation to the longer socialization processes by which "evidence"-laden disinformation becomes integrated into extremist worldviews via Far-Right online communities; its "processual redpilling" model — gradual socialization rather than single-exposure conversion — offers a qualitative counterpart to Adam et al.'s quantitative finding that predispositions and repeated, self-selected exposure jointly produce belief change, suggesting that the mechanisms uncovered for conspiracy and extremist belief formation generalize across both acute health crises and slower-burning ideological radicalization.
