# Generative AI, Persuasion & Synthetic Media

## The persuasion frontier: how good is conversational AI, and why

The empirical core of this topic is a rapidly maturing experimental literature asking, quite literally, how persuasive generative AI is and what makes it so. [[Costello2024-bg]] set the template: personalized, evidence-dense dialogue with GPT-4 durably cut conspiracy belief by ~20%, generalizing across topics and surviving two months — evidence against motivational-immunity accounts of conspiracy thinking. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] then systematically decomposed the *levers* of that persuasion across 19 models and hundreds of issues, showing that post-training and prompting strategy matter far more than raw scale, and that the dominant mechanism is **information density** — the sheer volume of fact-checkable claims a model can pack into a conversation. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] pushes this further, pitting AI against elite human persuaders (championship debaters, professional canvassers) and finding AI wins even after debaters are coached on the AI that beat them; only throttling AI to human typing speed and message length erases the gap, again implicating throughput/fact-density rather than rapport or personalization as the active ingredient. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this across four real elections/ballot measures, finding AI persuasion works mainly through "central route" facts rather than manipulation — though right-leaning advocacy conditions were systematically less accurate, echoing asymmetries in the broader information ecosystem. [[Kotz2026-lk]] extends the paradigm to climate, vaccines, and inequality, showing effects are strongest precisely among skeptics and moderated by trust in science, and that belief change does not automatically transfer to policy support. Together this cluster displaces the folk theory that AI persuades via slick personalization or psychological manipulation, replacing it with a more mundane (and more troubling) mechanism: AI's capacity to generate enormous quantities of plausible-sounding, fact-shaped content faster than any human interlocutor.

That mechanism has a shadow side. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] documents a **persuasion–accuracy trade-off**: the very techniques that make AI more persuasive (information-dense prompting, reward-model post-training) also degrade factual accuracy, sometimes sharply. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates the picture from the demand side, showing that persuasion is not just a property of the message but of perceived source neutrality — brief cues that a chatbot is "biased against your party" cut belief correction by roughly a quarter, operating through increased argumentative pushback rather than disengagement. Read alongside [[Dubey2026-bl]], which finds that high-conspiracy-belief users actually trust "balanced" chatbots *more* than low-belief users (contrary to expectations from reactance theory), a picture emerges in which AI persuasion is powerful but politically and dispositionally contingent, not a context-free force.

## Countering misinformation and conspiracy belief

A parallel strand asks whether these same persuasive capacities can be turned toward epistemic repair. Beyond [[Costello2024-bg]]'s debunking result, [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalizes "Conversational Inoculation" — an LLM chatbot that outperforms static reading/writing prebunking when individual baseline susceptibility is controlled, with effectiveness hinging on interaction-design factors (adaptability, trust, minimized friction) as much as argument content. [[Dubey2026-bl]]'s balanced-news chatbot sits adjacent to this project, testing whether conversational agents can pierce ideological bubbles rather than just correct discrete falsehoods. Taken together with [[Kotz2026-lk]] and [[Hackenburg2025-dj]], these papers suggest the same throughput/information-density mechanism that powers AI persuasion generally can be harnessed for correction — but [[DeVerna2025-dl]] offers an important caveat: even reasoning- and search-augmented LLMs are poor fact-checkers on their own, achieving strong accuracy only when paired with curated retrieval, and exhibiting a citation skew toward credible-but-left-leaning sources. Correction, in other words, is not free; it still depends on curated evidentiary infrastructure, not just model capability.

## Coordinated inauthentic behavior and manufactured consensus

A second major arc concerns AI's role in scaling deception rather than correcting it. [[Orlando2025-ul]] provides a striking existence proof at the mechanism level: generative-agent simulations show that networked LLM agents *spontaneously* reproduce real-world information-operation dynamics — narrative convergence, synchronized amplification, faster hashtag adoption — with mere mutual awareness among agents (not even explicit deliberation) sufficient to generate near-maximal coordination. [[Schroeder2026-im]] theorizes the endpoint of this trajectory as "malicious AI swarms," arguing that LLM-plus-multi-agent architectures constitute a qualitative leap beyond earlier botnets by enabling persistent, adaptive, cross-platform manufactured consensus, training-data poisoning ("LLM grooming"), and erosion of institutional trust — while cautioning that symmetric "pro-social swarms" cannot simply counter this because the attention economy structurally favors bad actors.

Empirical cases ground these theoretical claims. [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category that cuts across a pro-Putin propaganda network, a coordinated gambling campaign, and poorly moderated adult-content groups on Facebook, arguing that what unifies them is not falsity of content but orchestrated affordance-exploitation and coordination — a "Zombie Internet" condition in which human and automated activity become indistinguishable and platform enforcement categories fail to capture the harm. [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] zooms into the gambling case specifically, documenting an exponential, structurally-broken jump in coordinated gambling-promotion volume immediately following ChatGPT's launch, and cataloguing how generative AI recombines rather than replaces existing persuasion drivers (aspirational wealth, manufactured trust, FOMO, cultural localization) at industrial scale, exploiting the regulatory gap between strictly-policed paid ads and loosely-policed organic content. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows a parallel logic in party politics: the Sweden Democrats' 2024 EU campaign deployed platform-specific visual securitization, concentrating memetic, affectively charged (and geographically decontextualized) content on TikTok to target younger voters — a reminder that coordinated visual persuasion predates and extends beyond generative AI proper. [[Beacken2026-zb]] pushes back against uniform-trajectory narratives, arguing comparatively across six democratically weakened states that GenAI propaganda's form and impact are contingent on national political-economic structure, not technologically determined.

## Synthetic and visual media: how much does modality matter?

A related but distinct question is whether AI-generated or synthetic *visual* content is inherently more persuasive than text. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates the "seeing is believing" intuition experimentally: AI-generated still images did not outperform text, and only decontextualized *real* video outperformed text — and only for the highly salient, footage-rich Ukraine war issue, not the more epistemically uncertain MH370 case. This suggests concerns about generative AI's unprecedented visual persuasive power may be overstated relative to older, low-tech decontextualization tactics — though [[Choi2026-bz]] finds a subtler cognitive residue: difficulty authenticating one modality of (deep)fake news carries over to affect confidence and susceptibility specifically within that *same* modality later on. [[Emilio2026-ik]] synthesizes this terrain into the broadest claim of the cluster: the real risk is not discrete deepfakes but "synthetic realities" — a four-layer stack of fabricated content, identity, interaction, and institutional trust — that produces a paradox whereby societies rationally discount *all* digital evidence, taxing legitimate institutions as much as it empowers bad actors.

## Infrastructures of truth: how institutions and training data shape AI outputs

A third arc turns from AI as a persuasive *agent* to the institutional forces shaping what AI *says*. [[Waight2026-ts]] offers striking evidence that state media control is already laundered into LLM outputs: Chinese state-scripted news is memorized by commercial models, additional pretraining measurably shifts model favorability, and a cross-national audit of 37 countries shows lower media freedom predicts more pro-regime output in that country's language — reframing training-data pipelines themselves as a site of geopolitical influence. [[Triedman2025-uy]] documents an adjacent, more overt case: Grokipedia is largely derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically diverges — citing unreliable and blacklisted sources far more often, especially on controversial/political topics — showing how a single actor's editorial choices can reshape an ostensibly neutral knowledge infrastructure at scale. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] supplies the epistemological vocabulary needed to make sense of these cases, arguing that GenAI outputs constitute a new category of "emergent facts" — plausible, context-dependent, probabilistically generated — that resist assessment by evidence-based, interpretative, or institutional fact frameworks, and proposing accuracy/verifiability/relevance/consistency as replacement criteria. [[Xue2025-bp]] adds a smaller but resonant finding: even fact-checking itself, ostensibly the genre of objectivity, is saturated with emotional language that shapes engagement and sentiment — suggesting the corrective ecosystem is not immune to the same persuasive dynamics under study elsewhere in this topic.

## Reception, framing, and trust

A final cluster examines how publics, journalists, and platforms *frame* and receive generative AI, mediating all of the above dynamics. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs in global news is real but far from dominant, varying more by outlet/journalist practice than by region, and serving critique as often as hype. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google's AI Overviews are contested terrain between corporate seamlessness-framing, journalistic alarm over hallucination, and SEO-marketer anxiety — with users' own voices and societal/environmental stakes conspicuously absent from all three discourses. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds, counterintuitively, that audiences rate AI-generated and human-written news as equally credible pre-disclosure, and even read AI-disclosed content *more* eagerly short-term (though this doesn't translate into durable preference) — a finding that sits uneasily against the fear-based framings surveyed by Weinbrand. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework for trust in generative AI drawing on the mass-media and social-media trust literatures, useful scaffolding for organizing these otherwise scattered reception findings. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] provides a sobering theoretical counterpoint to the whole persuasion literature: ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is not understanding but a parasitic reliance on human repair work, implying that much of what looks like persuasive "skill" may be an artifact of human interlocutors doing interpretive labor the system cannot do itself.

## Governance, labor, and the fraud economy

The topic closes on questions of response and accountability. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both reframe AI red-teaming — the primary safety practice meant to catch these harms before deployment — as a sociotechnical, labor-intensive, value-laden practice rather than a neutral technical procedure, drawing explicit parallels to the precarious, psychologically costly history of commercial content moderation. Finally, [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extend the topic into political economy: Swartz argues scams have become a *central* organizing logic of platform capitalism rather than a peripheral abuse, with generative AI simply the latest accelerant of "network scams," while Schiffrin's policy brief documents the resulting deepfake financial-fraud ecosystem in granular detail and argues that effective regulation must shift liability from individual victims onto platforms, telecoms, and financial gatekeepers — a governance conclusion that echoes [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]]'s shared diagnosis that the problem is algorithmic amplification and platform incentive structure, not merely the presence of synthetic content.

## Synthesis

Read together, these papers trace an arc from *mechanism* (why is AI persuasive — information density, throughput, not personalization or manipulation) to *application* (for belief correction as in Costello/Szabo/Kotz, or for coordinated deception as in Orlando/Giglietto/Giada/Schroeder) to *infrastructure* (how training data, platform architecture, and institutional actors shape what gets said and believed) to *reception and governance* (how publics and institutions frame, trust, and attempt to regulate all of the above). The throughline is a shared empirical surprise: across nearly every study, generative AI's power derives less from novel psychological trickery than from its capacity to produce fact-shaped content, sustained coordination, and plausible synthetic realities at a scale and speed no prior technology could sustain — a difference of degree that these papers collectively argue has become a difference of kind.
