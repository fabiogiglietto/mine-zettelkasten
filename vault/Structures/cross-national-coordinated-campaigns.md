# Cross-National Coordinated Campaigns & Elections

## Beyond Italy: mapping a comparative turn

This cluster extends the study of coordinated inauthentic behavior and political advertising past its founding empirical home to ask whether the concepts, methods, and findings developed on Italian data travel. The answer that emerges across these papers is a qualified yes: coordination, hyperactivity, and platform-specific advertising strategies recur everywhere, but their mechanisms, political stakes, and detectability are shaped by national media systems, encrypted-messaging cultures, and the regulatory regime a country happens to sit under. Two country cases anchor the collection—Brazil and Germany—while a wider penumbra of studies (the US as historical benchmark, plus Scandinavia, Romania, the Philippines, and pan-European elections) tests how far the framework stretches.

## Brazil: coordination, encryption, and epistemic contest

Brazil supplies the richest and most varied evidence that inauthentic coordination is not a Twitter/Facebook-only phenomenon. [[Kansaon2025-id]] adapts "Rapid Spread Network" coordination-detection methods from Twitter research to 13 million WhatsApp messages, showing that coordinated accounts drove not just misinformation dissemination but real-world mobilization—doxxing of Supreme Court justices and the January 8, 2023 attacks—while exploiting the platform's own anti-forwarding safeguards. [[Ventura2025-sw]] complements this with a deactivation experiment, providing causal (rather than observational) evidence that WhatsApp itself, apart from public feeds, meaningfully shapes exposure to misinformation—together the two papers argue that any account of Brazilian coordinated campaigns that ignores encrypted messaging is incomplete, a point that echoes [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s broader call to de-center WEIRD, feed-centric misinformation research.

Alongside this infrastructural story sits an institutional and affective one. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus COVID crisis competing for "epistemic authority" through distinct narrative postures—derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and local brokerage—revealing how political elites contest journalistic gatekeeping in a hybrid media system. [[Marino2026-slef]] then tracks a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network longitudinally and finds that hyperpartisan affective engagement is far less stable than echo-chamber theory predicts, pivoting sharply around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt—suggesting that even seemingly closed partisan communities are permeable to external events and even deliberate "brigading." [[Rossini2026-jn]] links these dynamics to democratic erosion directly, showing that belief in 2022 electoral-fraud misinformation predicted rising political intolerance, with messaging-app news use working indirectly through misinformation belief. Finally, [[Ventura2026-yc]] documents the downstream consequence of platform governance itself: Brazil's 2024 ban of X produced a partisan "sorting ratchet," with conservative users disproportionately evading the ban and the platform's news environment shifting rightward in ways that persisted after the ban was lifted—a cautionary tale about state intervention as a tool against coordinated harms. Earlier infrastructural work, [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]], shows that even crude, volunteer-driven ad-auditing (a browser plugin during the 2018 election) can surface irregular political advertising in the absence of platform transparency, prefiguring later, more systematic transparency demands.

## Germany: methodological innovation meets thin coordination evidence

The German case is defined less by dramatic coordination findings and more by methodological experimentation aimed at making multi-platform, multimodal analysis tractable. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] stakes out the substantive claim—that political advertising and coordinated activity were notable features of the 2021 federal election—but remains thin on findings, functioning more as a marker of the object of study than a developed analysis. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] instead advances the toolkit, validating GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision models for face recognition and person-counting in 2021 Instagram campaign content, then using it to document how candidate versus party accounts and stories versus posts diverge in concentrating visibility around front-runners. [[Schulte2026-df]] extends this infrastructural concern to the 2025 election, arguing that the central bottleneck in studying German electoral discourse is not a shortage of studies but the absence of reusable, standardized research infrastructure for multi-platform observability—a diagnosis that treats platform access itself, rather than any single campaign finding, as the object of inquiry. Together, the German papers suggest a field still building the instruments needed to detect coordination and targeted advertising at the scale achieved for Italy or the US.

## The US as benchmark: from IRA ads to platform-scale exposure

Several US-based studies function as the comparative yardstick against which non-Italian, non-US coordination research measures itself. [[Kim2026-wg]] establishes, via direct ad-exposure tracking, that 2016 IRA-linked voter-suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and associated with real turnout declines—directly challenging earlier null findings that relied on indirect exposure proxies. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] (evidently the same Meta-collaborative FIES study) reframe influence operations more broadly as "deceptive networks" spanning both politically- and financially-motivated coordination, showing that reach is extremely concentrated, driven mostly by reshares from non-network accounts, and that naive correlations with democratic attitudes evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled—a sobering methodological corrective for causal claims elsewhere in this literature. [[Allcott2025-jb]] pushes further with an experimental ad-removal design, finding no detectable attitudinal effects of political advertising exposure, while [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] characterizes the diffusion architecture of Facebook misinformation at platform scale. Read together, these US papers argue that reach, targeting, and coordination are measurable and sometimes consequential (as in [[Kim2026-wg]]), but that inferring causal attitudinal effects from exposure or engagement data (as in [[Appel2026-qr]]/[[noauthor_undated-bm]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]]) is far harder than earlier IRA-era scholarship assumed—a caution that should temper claims made about Brazilian or German coordination with less rigorous designs.

## Widening the aperture: Europe, Scandinavia, and the Global South

A final group of papers tests generalizability even further afield. [[Iris2026-pg]] finds a structural, cross-national radical-right visibility bias in 2024 European Parliament election coverage across five countries, independent of electoral strength—suggesting media systems themselves, not just coordinated networks, can amplify radical-right narratives. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric platform-by-platform, prioritizing TikTok meme culture over larger Facebook/X followings to reach younger voters, while [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] finds that Scandinavian populist parties are disproportionately effective at generating "Angry" reactions that drive sharing, a comparatively muted but structurally similar version of the affective dynamics documented in Marino's Brazilian network. [[Oprea2025-lf]] identifies hyperactive, likely inauthentic Facebook accounts amplifying all major parties in Romania's 2024 EU campaign, with detection made harder by restrictive API access—directly paralleling the infrastructural complaints in [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]]. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations altogether as a form of political brokerage in the Philippines, emphasizing the political-economic infrastructure (client-broker-voter relationships) that sustains manipulation rather than treating it as ad hoc propaganda—a conceptual move that resonates with [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s attention to political actors as media producers. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] demonstrates that transnational anti-gender coordination (CitizenGO) operates as a centrally administered multilingual network whose amplifiers show coordination signatures, extending "coordinated inauthentic behavior" concepts to issue advocacy beyond electoral campaigns narrowly defined. [[Nenno2025-xa]], analyzing 24 countries, finds that the US and Brazil form a distinct cluster where news-value markers most sharply distinguish flagged misinformation—empirically corroborating this topic's implicit wager that Brazil, alongside the US, is a uniquely intense site of contested political information.

## Governance and observability as connective tissue

Threading through nearly all these country cases is a shared complaint about data access and platform governance. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys how the EU Digital Services Act has reshaped (and partially reopened) researcher access to election data, while [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content-moderation and "authoritative source" labeling during the 2024 EU elections, finding inconsistent application across languages and opaque removal communication. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] historicizes this governance terrain, tracing three phases of platform election policy—protective, civic-integrity, and rollback—driven disproportionately by US election cycles but with secondary influence from Brazil and Germany. Taken together with [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Oprea2025-lf]], these governance papers suggest that the comparative study of coordinated campaigns beyond Italy is now as much a story about the shifting, uneven infrastructure of platform transparency as about the coordinated behavior itself: what gets called "inauthentic," what can be detected, and what can be causally attributed all depend on data regimes that vary as much across time and platform as across nation.
