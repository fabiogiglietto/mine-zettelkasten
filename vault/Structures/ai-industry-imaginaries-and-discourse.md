# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media as Co-Producer of AI Imaginaries

A first cluster of papers treats journalism not as a neutral conduit but as an active site where sociotechnical imaginaries of AI are negotiated and circulated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from hegemonic in global news coverage, varying more by outlet and editorial culture than by region or ChatGPT-driven news cycles — complicating any simple story of media-wide AI hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across the UK, US, China, and India, showing that utopian and dystopian tropes (per Cave and Dihal) are distributed unevenly across national presses, each refracting its own political-economic stakes in generative AI. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this North–South axis quantitatively, demonstrating that Global North outlets adopt a pessimistic, risk-oriented register while Global South coverage is developmental and optimistic, with geopolitical framings (the US–China "AI race") cutting across both. Together these studies suggest AI imaginaries are not monolithic exports from Silicon Valley but are locally inflected, even as they converge on a shared techno-capitalist master frame.

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this literature by arguing that "AI hype" should be understood as a structuring infrastructure embedded in journalistic practice — in sourcing routines, professional anxiety, and the dual role of journalists as both hype-makers and hype-watchers — rather than as a free-floating discursive bubble. This systemic view dovetails with [[Richter2026-bt]], which traces who actually populates AI discourse over a decade (2012–2021) and finds diverging national trajectories: German Twitter discourse institutionalized toward academic and advocacy actors, while US discourse collapsed into industry and then individual tech-influencer dominance. Read together, these two pieces suggest that media hype is downstream of a prior, structural question — which stakeholders get to speak for AI in the first place — and that this structural asymmetry, entrenched before the generative-AI boom, conditions the hype dynamics later analyzed by Nguyen, Wang, and Mota.

## Industry Self-Narration: Techno-Hagiography and Quiet Futuring

A second cluster turns from media reception to the narratives industry actors author about themselves. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the topic's clearest taxonomy, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as the dominant imaginaries mobilized by the AI industry during the Altman controversy, and showing how these operate through "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by claiming unique competence to resolve it). This vocabulary of future-occupation is deepened by [[Hepp2026-oi]], which distinguishes the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors from a "quiet futuring" diffused through Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and physical sites (AGI House, Lighthaven, Frontier Tower). Hepp shows that Effective Altruism and Rationalist communities supply much of the longtermist raw material that Stanusch's industry actors later amplify into public controversy — the two papers describe complementary moments of the same imaginative supply chain, one documenting the reservoir, the other its weaponization in a crisis.

Corporate platforms also narrate themselves directly to publics, and two papers dissect this self-fashioning at the level of discourse. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom frames problematic content as originating from "outside" bad actors (inauthenticity, foreign interference) while casting Meta as the capable technological solver — a rhetorical structure that externalizes platform-native harms and even appears to shape regulatory vocabulary in the EU and Australia. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds a structurally similar move in Google's framing of AI Overviews: seamless, librarian-like helpfulness paired with hedged "experimental" accountability, set against more critical journalistic and anxious SEO-marketer discourses, with user and environmental concerns conspicuously absent from all three registers. Both papers illustrate how platform communications construct legitimacy through selective absence as much as through affirmative claims.

[[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical dissection of Grokipedia extends this logic from discourse to infrastructure: Musk's "alternative" encyclopedia turns out to be largely derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically skews toward unreliable and ideologically aligned sources on controversial topics, revealing how a counter-imaginary of "unbiased" AI-generated knowledge can function, in practice, as a vehicle for ideological capture dressed in neutral, infrastructural clothing — a concrete instance of the "apolitical" self-presentation Hepp identifies among Bay Area technologists.

## Political Economy, Decoys, and Infrastructural Labor

A third set of papers insists that discourse analysis must be anchored to material political economy. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] is the topic's most systematic theorization of obfuscation, arguing that AI critique itself is routinely captured by five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that appear to interrogate AI while actually stabilizing the underlying "Project of AI" — a world-building enterprise of financiers and corporations. This framework retrospectively illuminates several other papers in this collection: Stanusch's Longtermism and Regulatory Ambivalence imaginaries map closely onto Vertesi's "inevitability" and "regulatory" decoys, while Hurcombe's externalization of platform harm exemplifies decoy logic at the level of a single company's self-defense.

[[Tornberg2026-im]] complements this with a macro-historical argument that generative AI marks a transition from the "platform society" to an "AI society," in which platform-era mechanisms of behavioral capture give way to "cognitive lock-in" and struggles over visibility are displaced by "alignment politics" — contests over what models may say or refuse. This reframing usefully bridges the discourse-analytic papers above (which document alignment's narrative effects) and [[Gillespie2026-aa]], which insists that practices like red-teaming, often folded into reassuring "safety" narratives, are sociotechnical systems resting on outsourced, precarious, psychologically costly labor — the invisible human substrate that safety-decoy rhetoric (per Vertesi) and alignment-politics framing (per Törnberg) tend to obscure. Read as a sequence, these three papers move from naming the discursive mechanism of obfuscation (Vertesi), to theorizing its systemic logic (Törnberg), to grounding it in the concrete labor it hides (Gillespie).

## The Affective Register: Slop, Slopaganda, and Degenerate Aesthetics

A smaller but distinct pair of papers shifts from institutional discourse to the texture of AI-generated content itself. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Sianne Ngai's concept of the gimmick — content that works simultaneously "too little" (labor-saving, deskilled) and "too hard" (straining for algorithmic attention) — arguing that these stigmatized genres expose contradictions of "too late capitalism" while harboring unexpected critical or frictional potential. [[Topinka2026-hb]] pushes this into explicitly political territory with the concept of "slopaganda," arguing that AI-generated political content (the "Trump Gaza" video, "Kirkinator" memes) works not by deceiving viewers into belief but by affectively attuning to platform moods via RLHF-trained "ideological attunement machines," generating sticky, racialized affect without coherent claims. Together, these papers suggest that industry imaginaries of AI as frictionless helpfulness (Weinbrand's "supersmart librarian," Hurcombe's technological-solutions frame) have a shadow discourse in the degraded, affect-laden content these same systems actually produce at scale — a tension largely absent from the official corporate and journalistic framings analyzed elsewhere in this topic.

## Questioning the Frame Itself

Finally, [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] (Esposito) offers a conceptual counterpoint to the entire apparatus of "artificial intelligence" talk surveyed above: rather than asking how AI is framed as intelligent, humanlike, or world-building, it asks whether "intelligence" is the right register at all, proposing that systems like ChatGPT succeed because users generate meaning from their outputs, not because machines understand content. This argument functions as a kind of meta-commentary on the whole topic — a reminder that the mentalistic framings tracked by Nguyen, the hagiographic imaginaries mapped by Stanusch, and the infrastructural self-presentations analyzed by Hurcombe and Weinbrand are all, in Esposito's terms, downstream of a more basic category error about what these systems are doing. Positioned at the end of the arc, it invites a return to first principles even as the rest of the literature moves toward ever more granular accounts of who profits from, and is obscured by, the imaginaries that error sustains.
