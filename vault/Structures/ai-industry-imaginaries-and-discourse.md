# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media Framings and the Making of Public AI Epistemology

A first cluster of papers treats journalism as a primary site where AI's meaning is negotiated rather than simply reported. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs, while present, is far from dominant or uniform—it is shaped more by outlet culture than by region, and can serve critique as easily as hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across the UK, US, China, and India, mapping generative AI coverage onto Cave and Dihal's utopia/dystopia axis and showing that national "AI imaginaries" are inflected by distinct political-economic stakes. [[Mota2026-sl59]] pushes the comparative axis further, contrasting a risk-saturated Global North discourse with a developmentalist, optimistic Global South framing, and periodizing coverage around Cambridge Analytica and ChatGPT as inflection points—implicitly asking whose fears and whose hopes get to define "AI politics." Together these studies establish that media imaginaries are not epiphenomenal decoration but constitutive of divergent public epistemologies, even as [[Dodds2026-df]] insists that hype must be understood as more than discourse: it is an infrastructural force embedded in journalism's professional practices, sourcing routines, and funding logics, which sustain the invisibility of labour, environmental cost, and alternative futures. [[Richter2026-bt]] complements this by empirically tracing who has actually driven AI discourse over the longue durée (2012–2021 Twitter data), finding a genuinely divergent US/German trajectory—individualized tech-influencer capture versus institutionalized multi-stakeholder discourse—that helps explain why later hype and regulatory postures diverge so sharply between the two countries.

## Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Longtermism, and Quiet Futuring

A second, closely linked group examines how the industry narrates itself, often in direct response to moments of crisis or contestation. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the sharpest analytic vocabulary in the corpus: analyzing the Altman firing/rehiring episode, it identifies Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry imaginaries, and shows how the industry uses "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by claiming sole competence to resolve it) to manage its public image—while noting that counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter demonstrate this narrative capture remains incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] deepens this account spatially and sociologically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs from a "quiet futuring" enacted in Bay Area labs, houses, and community spaces, anchored in Effective Altruism and Rationalist milieus that supply the very ideas (AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race) later amplified by loud futuring—while presenting themselves, contradictorily, as apolitical. Where Stanusch traces imaginaries through a single controversy, Hepp traces their ongoing production through place and figuration, suggesting that the industry's discursive power rests as much on ambient community-building as on individual hero narratives.

Corporate platforms also narrate themselves defensively in response to accountability pressure. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse on problematic content, showing how "inauthenticity," "technological solutions," and "enforcement" frames externalize responsibility while positioning Meta as capable problem-solver—a discursive strategy that appears to have seeped into regulatory language itself. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds an analogous pattern in Google's framing of AI Overviews as a "seamless" evolution of search hedged by "experimental" disclaimers, contested unevenly by cautious journalists and anxious SEO marketers, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three actors' discourse. Both papers thus extend the Altman-controversy logic of premediation/preclusion into the register of routine corporate communications: platforms manage critique by defining its terms.

## Beneath the Narratives: Political Economy and Its Decoys

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies the sharpest theoretical anchor for the whole topic, arguing that AI is best understood as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise of financiers and corporations—and that much critical and accountability work, however well-intentioned, gets captured by five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) that seem to challenge the industry while actually stabilizing it. This reframes several of the above papers: Stanusch's longtermism is Vertesi's "inevitability decoy" instantiated; Hurcombe's and Weinbrand's platform self-narration performs precisely the deflection Vertesi warns accountability scholars against replicating. [[Baym2026-tr]] arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis from a different angle, revisiting a decade of social media critique to show that despite voluminous scholarly critique, wealth concentration ("broligarchy"), data extraction, and algorithmic harm have all intensified—implicitly corroborating Vertesi's claim that critique-as-usual has not disrupted the underlying Project, and calling instead for engaged, cross-sector intervention. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] operationalizes this political-economy lens at the level of labour, showing how AI red-teaming—framed publicly as a rigorous safety practice—in fact reproduces the outsourced, precarious, psychologically taxing labour arrangements of earlier content moderation, with harms and value judgments defined opaquely and internally by companies rather than through public deliberation. Read together, these three papers insist that the imaginaries mapped above are not free-floating narratives but actively legitimize concrete configurations of capital, labour, and infrastructure.

## The Affective and Aesthetic Underside: Slop, Brainrot, and Slopaganda

A further pair of papers examines how AI-industry logics circulate not through explicit argument but through low, affectively saturated cultural forms. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's Theory of the Gimmick, arguing these forms are simultaneously labour-saving deskilling devices and strained bids for attention, exposing the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism," while retaining an ambivalent critical potential as friction against platform smoothness. [[Topinka2026-hb]] extends this into explicitly political territory with the concept of "slopaganda"/"agitslop": AI-generated political content that need not be believed to be effective, because it works as an affective lure attuned—via RLHF and Global-South-outsourced labelling labour—to the moods and racialized fantasies already coursing through platform feeds. Both papers push the topic's imaginaries-and-discourse focus toward the level of aesthetic form and affect, suggesting that industry power operates not only through explicit narrative (hagiography, longtermism) but through the ambient, ideologically-attuning texture of generated content itself.

## Rethinking the Object: What "AI" Discourse Obscures

Finally, [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] offers a conceptual counterpoint that underlies the entire topic: it argues the very framing of systems like ChatGPT as "artificial intelligence" replicating human cognition is a category error—these systems succeed not through machine understanding but through the interpretive labour of human users. This claim resonates with, and arguably grounds, the discourse-analytic work above: if "AI" itself is partly a rhetorical accomplishment rather than a stable technical referent, then the anthropomorphic framings tracked by Nguyen, the hagiographic and longtermist imaginaries mapped by Stanusch and Hepp, and the "epistemic authority" performed by Google's AI Overviews in Weinbrand are all variations on the same underlying move: mobilizing the language of intelligence and inevitability to secure legitimacy, resources, and deflection from accountability. Read as a whole, the papers filed here trace an arc from documenting how AI narratives circulate in media and industry communication, to diagnosing the political-economic interests these narratives serve and obscure, to examining the affective textures through which such narratives are lived and felt, and finally to questioning whether "artificial intelligence" is itself the right conceptual object for any of this analysis.
