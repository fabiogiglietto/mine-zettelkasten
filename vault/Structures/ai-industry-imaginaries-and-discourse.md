# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Framing the Frame: Media, Imaginaries, and the Public Epistemology of AI

A first cluster of papers treats journalism and mass media as the primary site where AI's sociotechnical imaginaries are negotiated, tested, and sometimes contested. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] offers the most granular empirical account, showing that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs in global news are real but far from hegemonic—present in less than a tenth of coverage and driven more by outlet-level editorial culture than by regional difference, and often deployed critically rather than credulously. [[Wang2025-zy]] complements this with a comparative mapping of utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, operationalising Cave and Dihal's "hopes and fears" framework to show that national imaginaries are structured by local political-economic stakes rather than a single global narrative. [[Mota2026-sl59]] extends this comparative lens temporally and geopolitically, tracing three phases of AI-politics coverage (2010–2024) and documenting a stark North–South sentiment asymmetry: pessimistic, risk-laden framing in the Global North against optimistic, developmental framing in the Global South, with US–China rivalry as a recurring organizing frame. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes this entire terrain by arguing that "AI hype" in journalism is not simply a discursive artifact to be debunked but a structuring infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources, embedded in newsroom practice and sourcing routines that systematically privilege tech executives over other actors—a claim empirically anticipated by [[Richter2026-bt]], whose longitudinal Twitter analysis shows US AI discourse drifting toward individualized tech-influencer dominance while German discourse institutionalised around academic, advocacy, and governmental actors, with each trajectory now shaping divergent national responses to the GenAI boom.

## Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Quiet Futuring, and Platform Discourse

A second thread moves from media coverage of AI to how the industry narrates itself. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] provides perhaps the topic's sharpest conceptual vocabulary, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as the dominant imaginaries mobilised during the Sam Altman controversy, and showing how industry actors use "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by claiming sole competence to resolve it) to monopolise AI's future—though incompletely, as counter-imaginaries of governing urgency and intersectionality surfaced on X/Twitter. [[Hepp2026-oi]] adds spatial and figurational depth to this picture, arguing that alongside the "loud futuring" of CEOs there exists a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks that supplies the ideational reservoir (AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitics) later amplified and condensed by loud actors—all while presenting itself as apolitical. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] narrows the lens to a single product controversy, Google's AI Overviews, showing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers compete to legitimise or contest a shift from indexical gatekeeper to generative epistemic authority, with user perspectives and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three discourses—an absence that resonates with the "decoy" logic developed elsewhere in this literature.

## Beneath the Narrative: Political Economy, Decoys, and Platformization

The most structurally ambitious contributions push past discourse analysis toward political economy. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] is the pivot: it argues that AI is best understood as a "Project" of world-building capital accumulation, and that critical engagement itself risks becoming complicit by chasing five "decoys"—ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory—that appear to challenge AI while actually stabilising the underlying network of power. This framework retroactively illuminates much of the topic: the "inevitability decoy" names precisely the temporal deflection Stanusch calls premediation; the "safety decoy" explains why Longtermism and existential-risk talk can coexist with regulatory ambivalence; and the "regulatory decoy" clarifies why industry courts oversight (echoed in Altman's Senate testimony) without ceding real power. [[Helmond2026-ll]] historicises this power shift infrastructurally, tracing platformization's move from open-web signal capture to an AI-centred phase involving new actors (Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir) and new frontiers (space-based compute), warning that the analytic term itself risks conceptual erosion even as the phenomenon intensifies. [[Baym2026-tr]] closes this arc on a personal-institutional register, revisiting a decade-old critique of platform power to show that wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, and data extraction have all worsened under generative AI, and calling for scholarship to move beyond diagnosis toward collaborative intervention—a call that anticipates Vertesi's warning that critique alone can be enrolled into legitimation.

## Hidden Labor and the Human Costs Obscured by Imaginaries

Two companion papers puncture industry imaginaries by attending to the labor they render invisible. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine AI red-teaming as a sociotechnical rather than purely technical practice: the former traces its structural parallels to commercial content moderation—outsourced labor, unaccountable internal value judgments, and psychological harm to workers—while the latter documents how public red-teaming events promise democratization of AI safety but remain shaped by institutional framing of what counts as harm. Together they demonstrate empirically what Dodds and Vertesi argue conceptually: that hype and decoys operate by erasing the human infrastructure—annotators, red-teamers, moderators—that sustains AI systems.

## Cultural Residue and Geopolitical Variants: Slop, Propaganda, and Democratic Backsliding

At the discourse's cultural and geopolitical margins, [[Galip2026-ix]] theorises "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's gimmick concept, arguing that these stigmatised, seemingly degenerative outputs paradoxically expose the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism" and may harbour frictional, resistant potential against platform enshittification—offering an aesthetic counterpoint to the industry's frictionless self-image. [[Beacken2026-zb]] extends the critical lens geopolitically, showing that GenAI-enabled propaganda in six democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) does not follow a uniform, techno-deterministic trajectory but is shaped by local political-economic structures—reinforcing, from the vantage of digital authoritarianism, the broader collection's insistence that AI's "inevitability" narrative obscures profoundly uneven and contingent realities on the ground.

## A Specimen from Within: Industry Discourse in Action

[[Jayaram2026-wd]], Google Research's own account of its Paper Assistant Tool for automating scientific peer review, sits apart from the rest of the topic as a primary artifact rather than an analysis of one. Framed unreflectively around a "scaling crisis" in peer review and a taxonomy of progressive automation "roles" culminating in "Total Automation," it exemplifies precisely the techno-solutionist, inevitability-laden self-narration that [[Vertesi2026-lv]], [[Stanusch2026-ec]], and [[Dodds2026-df]] identify as characteristic of industry discourse: a confident technical fix presented as neutral infrastructure improvement, with no engagement with the labor, epistemic, or institutional stakes of displacing human judgment. Read alongside the rest of the topic, it functions less as a counter-voice than as raw material—an instance of the very imaginary-work this literature seeks to denaturalise.
