# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media as Co-Producers of AI Imaginaries

A cluster of studies treats journalism and mainstream media not as neutral conduits but as active co-producers of AI's sociotechnical imaginaries. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs in global news are real but far from dominant, varying more by newsroom culture than by region or country — a finding that complicates any simple story of media uncritically parroting industry hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, mapping generative AI coverage onto Cave and Dihal's utopia/dystopia axis and showing how national political-economic conditions inflect which hopes and fears get amplified. [[Mota2026-sl59]] pushes the comparison further into a Global North/South frame, finding that Northern outlets skew toward risk and existential threat while Southern outlets frame AI through developmental optimism — a geopolitical asymmetry that mirrors, and is amplified by, US–China "weapon vs. wonder" rhetoric. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the whole phenomenon: AI hype in journalism is not merely a discursive bubble but a systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources, sustained by repetitive sourcing patterns that privilege tech executives and self-proclaimed experts while erasing hidden labor and material costs — a point that resonates strongly with [[Vertesi2026-lv]]'s account of "decoys" below. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] offers a micro-case of this dynamic in the context of Google's AI Overviews, tracing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest the meaning of a single product launch, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three discourses. Finally, [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] gesture toward the reception and geopolitical ends of this pipeline: Italian publics show a "critical ambivalence" toward AI shaped by uneven media exposure, while GenAI propaganda in democratically weakened states demonstrates that industry narratives of inevitability are refracted very differently once they meet local political structures — a reminder that imaginaries do not travel unchanged.

## Industry Self-Narratives: Hagiography, Longtermism, and Quiet Futuring

A second cluster examines how AI industry actors themselves construct legitimating narratives. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] is paradigmatic here: analyzing the Sam Altman firing/rehiring controversy, it identifies Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, and shows how the industry manages controversy through "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning itself as the only competent problem-solver). [[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates the picture of monolithic industry messaging by distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors from a "quiet futuring" diffused through Bay Area labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and start-up houses — an ostensibly apolitical, fragmented landscape that nonetheless supplies the ideational reservoir (AGI timelines, job-loss anxieties, US-China race framing) that loud futuring later condenses and amplifies. Read together, these two papers suggest that industry imaginaries operate on multiple registers simultaneously: spectacular personality-driven narratives during moments of crisis, and slower, community-embedded discursive work that primes which futures seem thinkable at all. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] show parallel dynamics in platform corporate communications rather than AI labs specifically: Meta's Newsroom uses floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" to externalize blame for platform harms while presenting itself as their capable solver, and platforms more broadly deploy "changecraft" — visible aesthetic redesign, ideological continuity-framing, and incremental "patchworking" — to naturalize consolidations of power as user-serving innovation. Though not AI-specific, both essays model the discursive strategies (externalization, technological solutionism, reactive proceduralism) that recur in explicitly AI-industry contexts, and Hurcombe's account of Meta's AI-driven content-moderation claims (e.g., "AI detected 95% of hate speech") shows the two domains directly overlapping.

## Political Economy and the Decoys That Obscure Power

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] provides the most systematic theorization of how such narratives function ideologically, arguing that AI development is better understood as a "Project" of world-building capital accumulation than as a technological artifact, and identifying five "decoys" — ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory — through which critique is captured and redirected into forms that stabilize rather than threaten industry power. This framework retroactively illuminates much of the topic: Stanusch's Longtermism is Vertesi's "inevitability decoy" and "safety decoy" in action; Hepp's "quiet futuring" is precisely the discursive labor that keeps these decoys ideologically fresh; and Dodds's account of hype-as-infrastructure names the same phenomenon Vertesi calls "network shape-shifting." [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal Twitter analysis complements this political-economy account empirically, showing that the US AI discourse shifted decisively toward industry and individual tech-influencer dominance between 2012 and 2021, while Germany institutionalized around academic, advocacy, and governmental actors — evidence that today's imaginary landscape (and its capture by industry voices) has a traceable formative history predating the GenAI boom. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extends the political-economy critique into knowledge production itself, documenting extensive undisclosed industry ties among authors, editors, and reviewers of high-profile social media research — a finding that suggests the "decoys" Vertesi describes may be reproduced even within ostensibly independent scholarship addressing platform and AI power.

## The Materiality Behind the Narrative: Labor and Infrastructure

Several papers examine what industry imaginaries characteristically obscure: labor and infrastructure. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both interrogate AI red-teaming, arguing it is a sociotechnical practice shaped by value judgments, outsourced labor arrangements, and psychological harms that remain invisible behind its technical framing as "safety" — a direct instantiation of Vertesi's safety decoy, showing how safety work itself can function to legitimize opaque, market-driven arrangements rather than genuinely democratize oversight. [[Helmond2026-ll]] traces a parallel material shift at the infrastructural level, arguing that "platformization" is entering an AI-centered phase in which Big Tech repositions around "Big AI" and extends compute infrastructure into new domains (space, energy, defense) while retiring the social-graph paradigms that once defined platform imaginaries. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], an Anthropic-authored report on user-reported economic impacts, exemplifies industry's own preferred register for discussing AI's material effects: survey-based, individualized productivity narratives that foreground personal benefit while leaving aside structural questions of displacement and power that Vertesi's "disruption decoy" specifically targets.

## Adjacent Platform Discourse and Cultural Reception

Rounding out the collection, [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Baym2026-tr]], both anniversary reflections on social media's trajectory, extend the critical political-economy lens backward across two decades, arguing that "social" media has become "parasocial" and that inequality, algorithmic opacity, and precarious labor have all worsened despite sustained critique — a sobering backdrop against which the newer AI-specific imaginaries examined above should be read, since they largely repeat and intensify dynamics already diagnosed in platform studies. Finally, [[Galip2026-ix]] offers a distinct cultural-theoretical register, applying Ngai's theory of the gimmick to "AI slop" and "brainrot" as aesthetic forms that expose the contradictions of generative-AI production under "too late capitalism" — a reminder that the industry's narrative work is contested not only by journalists, regulators, and scholars but by the very aesthetic residue of the technology's outputs, which can carry unintended critical friction against the seamless futures that loud and quiet futuring alike promise.
