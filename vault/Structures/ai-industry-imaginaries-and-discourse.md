# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Industry's Own Mythology: Longtermism, Hagiography, and Quiet Futuring

At the center of this literature sits a question of narrative construction: how does the AI industry talk about itself, and what does that talk make visible or invisible? [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the sharpest anatomy of this process, using the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a natural experiment in imaginary-formation. It identifies three dominant industry-aligned imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography — and, crucially, two rhetorical strategies by which the industry manages controversy: "premediation" (deflecting present critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by positioning the industry itself as best equipped to resolve it). This dual movement — externalizing present harms while internalizing the authority to define and solve future ones — recurs as a structural motif across the entire topic.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates the picture by arguing that critical AI scholarship has overfocused on the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors (cyberlibertarianism, solutionism) while neglecting a "quiet futuring" enacted by Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks. This quiet register supplies the ideational reservoir — AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race — that loud futuring later amplifies, while presenting itself as apolitical. Read together, [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]] show longtermism operating at two scales simultaneously: as a discursive weapon deployed reactively during crises, and as an ambient, spatially anchored culture that pre-exists and outlives any particular controversy.

## Naming the Decoys: Political Economy Beneath the Narrative

Where the imaginaries literature maps *what* the industry says, [[Vertesi2026-lv]] asks what such framings systematically prevent us from asking. Its argument that AI is best understood as a "Project" — a world-building enterprise through which financiers and corporations assemble networks of capital and infrastructure — reframes the entire topic's stakes. The paper's five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) function as a taxonomy of exactly the moves catalogued elsewhere: the safety decoy names what Stanusch calls Longtermism/premediation; the regulatory decoy accounts for why industry actors like Altman court oversight strategically. Vertesi's warning that even critical scholars and participatory design efforts can be "enrolled" into legitimizing the Project gives this entire structure note its most self-reflexive caution — a reminder that cataloguing imaginaries is not the same as escaping them.

## Corporate Platforms as Discourse Machines

Two papers examine how individual platforms operationalize these dynamics through official communications. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse on problematic content, showing how "floating signifiers" like *coordinated inauthentic behaviour* let Meta frame harms as external to its own architecture while presenting itself as the capable solver — a corporate-communications instantiation of Stanusch's preclusion, with the added finding that Meta's vocabulary has diffused into regulatory language itself (EU, Australia), suggesting the discourse doesn't just legitimize but actively shapes governance. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] performs an analogous analysis of Google's AI Overviews, tracing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers contest the meaning of a new "generative epistemic authority," with Google's own discourse hedging accountability via "experimental" framing while erasing user, democratic, and environmental concerns entirely — an absence that echoes Vertesi's disruption and safety decoys operating at the level of a single product launch.

## Media, Journalism, and the Public as Co-Producers

A second cluster examines how these industry narratives circulate through and are refracted by journalism and publics. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes "AI hype" not as discourse but as infrastructure — embedded in newsroom sourcing practices, professional anxiety, and resource allocation — and shows how repetitive reliance on tech-executive sources renders hidden labor and material costs invisible, a labor-erasure that resonates with Vertesi's account of shape-shifting network power. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] and [[Wang2025-zy]] both use large-scale content analysis to test whether media coverage straightforwardly reproduces industry narratives; Nguyen's finding that mentalistic-agentic framing is present but not dominant, and often deployed critically, complicates any simple hypodermic-needle model of imaginary transmission, while Wang's cross-national mapping of utopian/dystopian tropes (UK, US, China, India) shows imaginaries refracted through distinct political-economic conditions rather than uniformly imported from Silicon Valley. [[Mota2026-sl59]] extends this comparative lens globally, finding systematic Global North pessimism versus Global South optimism in AI coverage — a geopolitical asymmetry that gives empirical texture to the "AI race" narratives Hepp identifies as a discursive thickening in Bay Area discourse. At the reception end, [[Fattorini2026-bo]] shows Italian publics holding these circulating narratives at arm's length, exhibiting "critical ambivalence" rather than either hype-driven enthusiasm or technophobia — evidence that industry and media imaginaries do not simply overwrite public understanding.

## Who Gets to Narrate: Stakeholders and Counter-Imaginaries

[[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal analysis of Twitter/X discourse (2012–2021) complicates any assumption of monolithic industry dominance, showing sharply diverging US and German trajectories — the former toward individual tech-influencer capture, the latter toward institutionalized, multi-stakeholder discourse — with consequences for each country's subsequent regulatory posture. This finding dovetails with the counter-imaginaries (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) that [[Stanusch2026-ec]] identifies on X/Twitter during the Altman controversy, together suggesting that industry narrative control, while powerful, is neither uniform across geography nor uncontested.

## Beneath the Narrative: Labor, Practice, and Participation

A final cluster interrogates the material practices the industry's narratives tend to obscure. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine AI red-teaming as sociotechnical labor rather than neutral technical procedure, showing how internally defined harm categories, outsourced labor, and psychological toll replicate the invisibilized "ghost work" patterns of content moderation — a direct empirical instance of what Vertesi's safety decoy predicts discursively. [[Matias2025-px]] pushes toward a remedy, arguing that participatory public involvement can improve the actual science of AI evaluation, not merely its legitimacy — an implicit rejoinder to Vertesi's warning that participatory design can be co-opted, by specifying conditions (equipoise, measurement, interpretation) under which public involvement strengthens rather than launders industry claims. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s forensic comparison of Grokipedia and Wikipedia offers a concrete case study of ideological capture through infrastructure — showing how an ostensibly neutral knowledge platform reproduces a founder's politics through citation practices and selective rewriting, materializing the "Project of AI" that Vertesi describes in the register of encyclopedic authority.

## Coda: Stakes and Alternatives

[[Baym2026-tr]] closes the loop by insisting that a decade of critical scholarship on platform power has not slowed the concentration of wealth and narrative control it diagnosed — a sobering check on the entire body of work assembled here. Its call to move from critique toward interdisciplinary, engaged intervention echoes Dodds' call for "breakdown and repair" and Matias' participatory remedy, suggesting that the collective arc of this topic runs from *diagnosing* imaginaries (Stanusch, Hepp, Wang, Nguyen) through *locating their political-economic function* (Vertesi, Hurcombe, Weinbrand) to *asking what forms of counter-practice — labor organizing, participatory science, comparative journalism — might actually unsettle them* (Gillespie, Matias, Richter, Baym). [[Ng2026-og]]'s call to make social theory a structural prior for agentic AI design stands somewhat apart from this arc empirically, but shares its underlying premise: that AI systems, imaginaries, and infrastructures are irreducibly social phenomena, and that treating them as isolated technical artifacts — whether in system design or in public narrative — is itself part of the problem this topic collectively diagnoses.
