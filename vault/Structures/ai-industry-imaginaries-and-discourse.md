# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Industry's Own Mythology: Longtermism, Hagiography, and Quiet Futuring

The anchor texts here treat AI imaginaries not as passive narrative but as active infrastructure of power. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy to show how industry-aligned discourse coalesces around three imaginaries—Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography—deployed through twin strategies of "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning the industry itself as the only legitimate arbiter of the problems it creates). [[Hepp2026-oi]] extends this by distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs from a "quiet futuring" enacted in Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist milieus—a reservoir of AGI-timeline, job-loss, and geopolitical narratives that loud futuring later amplifies, all while presenting itself as apolitical. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] supplies the sharpest theoretical frame: AI is a "Project" of world-building capital, and the concerns that seem to critique it—ontological debates, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulation—function as "decoys" that actually stabilize the very political economy they appear to interrogate. Read together, these three papers trace a single arc: imaginaries are produced (Hepp), weaponized in moments of crisis (Stanusch), and structurally naturalized as the terrain on which even critical scholarship operates (Vertesi).

## Corporate Discourse as Rhetorical Infrastructure

A second cluster examines how individual companies craft language to manage accountability. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], though pre-dating the generative AI boom, remains the methodological ancestor here: the semantic elasticity of "platform" let YouTube address users, advertisers, and regulators simultaneously while obscuring its editorial interventions—a template later papers apply to AI-specific vocabularies. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom performing an analogous maneuver, using floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" to externalize responsibility for problematic content onto outside bad actors while claiming competence as the sole capable solver—discourse that has since diffused into EU and Australian regulatory language. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds Google performing similar work around AI Overviews, hedging accountability through "experimental" framing while journalists and SEO marketers contest the meaning of this reconfiguration of search, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three actors' discourse. Together these studies show corporate communications as a genre with recurring rhetorical moves: externalize harm, foreground technological solutionism, and claim exclusive authority to resolve the problems one's own products create.

## Journalism and Public Discourse as Contested Relay

A third strand asks how these industry narratives travel through—and are sometimes resisted by—media and public discourse. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes AI hype as a systemic infrastructure embedded in journalism's community of practice rather than mere discursive bubble, noting how repetitive sourcing toward tech executives sustains hype while rendering hidden labor and environmental costs invisible. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] complicates any simple story of media complicity, finding that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but not dominant, varying more by newsroom practice than region, and sometimes serving critique rather than hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] both pursue cross-national comparison—the former mapping utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, the latter tracing a Global North pessimism versus Global South developmental optimism across twelve outlets, with US-China rivalry as a recurring geopolitical frame. [[Richter2026-bt]] pushes back against assumptions of uniform industry dominance altogether, showing that the formative (pre-GenAI) Twitter discourse diverged sharply by country: Germany institutionalized toward academic and advocacy voices while the US became a tech-influencer discourse—a divergence the paper argues pre-configured each country's subsequent AI politics. Read against the "decoy" and "quiet futuring" literature, this cluster suggests industry narrative-setting is powerful but incomplete and unevenly contested, dependent on national media ecologies and professional norms.

## The Political Economy of Knowing AI

A related concern is how industry shapes not just public narrative but the scientific and infrastructural means of evaluating AI itself. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, in general and then quantitatively, how tech companies exert tobacco-industry-style influence over the research literature that studies their products—through undisclosed funding ties, suppressed internal research, restricted data access, and "industrial saturation" of authorship and editorial roles that reaches roughly 80% once reviewers are counted. [[Balluff2026-if]] extends this concern methodologically, showing how unreflective adoption of commercial LLMs in communication research itself reproduces corporate dependency, opacity, and bias. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] turn to red-teaming, arguing that this ostensibly technical safety practice is in fact a sociotechnical system whose value judgments, labor arrangements, and psychological costs echo the earlier history of content moderation—a practice easily absorbed into Vertesi's "safety decoy." [[Matias2025-px]] offers a partial counter-model, arguing that genuine public and lived-experience involvement can materially improve—not merely legitimate—the science of AI evaluation, gesturing toward an alternative to industry-captured knowledge production. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], Anthropic's own survey of 81,000 Claude users on AI's economic effects, can be read reflexively within this cluster: it is itself an instance of an AI company producing quantified narrative about its own social impact, blurring the line between "evidence" and self-legitimating discourse that the Bak-Coleman papers warn against.

## State Power and the Appropriation of AI-Mediated Narrative

Where the papers above focus on corporate framing, another set shows how state and political actors exploit AI systems to inject or amplify their own narratives. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates empirically that state-coordinated media is memorized by commercial LLMs and causes measurably more pro-regime outputs when models are queried in languages tied to countries with lower media freedom—showing that "training data" is itself a site of geopolitical narrative laundering. [[Triedman2025-uy]] examines Grokipedia as a case of a single actor's political framing embedded at encyclopedic scale, finding it heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically skewed toward unreliable and ideologically aligned sources on controversial topics. [[Beacken2026-zb]] broadens the lens comparatively, arguing that GenAI propaganda in democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) is shaped by local political-economic structure rather than following a uniform, technologically deterministic trajectory—directly challenging inevitability narratives of the kind Vertesi criticizes. Together, these studies show that the "imaginaries" literature must reckon not only with Silicon Valley's own narrative production but with how state and partisan actors instrumentalize the resulting infrastructure.

## Publics as Audience, Skeptic, and Target

A final cluster examines how these circulating narratives land with, or are resisted by, ordinary publics—and how AI's growing persuasive capacities raise the stakes of narrative control. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] and [[Gottfried2026-ww]] find, in Italy and the US respectively, not technophobia or uncritical hype-acceptance but "critical ambivalence": rising adoption alongside persistent skepticism, context-dependent trust, and declining confidence in effective regulation. [[Suk2026-ai]] proposes a multilevel framework situating trust in generative AI within the longer trajectory of trust research on mass and social media, implicitly asking whether industry-favorable imaginaries can durably capture public trust the way earlier media narratives did. This question becomes urgent in light of [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]], both showing that conversational AI can out-persuade even expert humans and shift beliefs and policy support across contested issues—capacities that make whoever controls AI's underlying narratives and deployment newly powerful over public opinion itself, and that lend concrete stakes to Vertesi's warnings about unaccountable network power. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] provides a useful corrective undercutting technological mystique from a different angle: chatbot "performance" is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding, a finding that quietly deflates techno-hagiographic claims about AI cognition circulating in industry discourse. Finally, [[Ng2026-og]], though framed as a technical position paper on multi-agent systems, belongs at this topic's edge: its call to import social theory as a "structural prior" for agentic AI is itself a normative intervention into how the field imagines AI's social embeddedness, illustrating that even ostensibly technical AI research is not exempt from the imaginary-work this literature otherwise locates in journalism, corporate communications, and state propaganda.

## Baym's Retrospective as Coda

[[Baym2026-tr]] serves well as a closing note across this whole structure: revisiting her decade-old warnings about wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, and data extraction, Baym finds all four intensified rather than resolved, with tech "broligarchy" converting platform power into political power. Her call to move from critique toward interdisciplinary, engaged intervention echoes the throughline connecting every paper in this collection—from Vertesi's decoys to Bak-Coleman's industrial saturation to Matias's participatory alternative: naming AI's imaginaries is necessary but insufficient; the harder task is building institutions and practices robust enough to outlast the narratives that currently protect concentrated power.
