# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Discourse Industry Around AI: Imaginaries, Hype, and Their Omissions

The papers filed here converge on a single, deceptively simple claim: AI "itself" is never the sole object of public understanding — what circulates instead are narrated versions of AI, produced by specific actors with specific interests, and these narrations do real work in the world. They mobilize capital, redistribute legitimacy, and foreclose alternative futures. The corpus traces this claim across several registers: news media framing, industry self-narration, platform/corporate discourse, and the aesthetic/affective textures of AI-generated content itself.

### Media as Co-Producer of Imaginaries

A cluster of studies treats journalism not as a neutral conduit but as an active co-producer of AI's sociotechnical imaginaries. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from dominant, varying more by newsroom practice than by region or culture — a finding that complicates any simple "media hypes AI" narrative. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparatively, mapping utopian/dystopian tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage and showing how national political-economic conditions inflect which "hopes and fears" (per Cave and Dihal) get emphasized. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into a Global North/South axis, finding risk-oriented pessimism in Northern outlets against developmental optimism in the South, with geopolitical framing (especially the US-China "AI race") as a connective tissue running through both. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes the whole phenomenon at a structural level: AI hype is not merely discourse but an infrastructure embedded in journalism's professional practices, sourcing routines, and political economy, one that renders invisible the labor, environmental costs, and material infrastructures sustaining AI — a diagnosis that anticipates the political-economy critique developed later in the corpus. [[Richter2026-bt]] adds a historical corrective, showing that before the GenAI boom, who "drove" AI discourse varied substantially by country (German institutionalization vs. American industry/influencer capture) — a reminder that today's industry-dominated discourse was not inevitable but itself the product of a formative decade's power struggles.

### Industry Self-Narration and Its Signature Imaginaries

A second cluster examines how industry actors narrate themselves directly. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] is the pivot here, identifying the imaginaries of Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography that crystallized around the Sam Altman controversy, and naming the dual strategies — premediation and preclusion — by which industry both externalizes present critique into speculative futures and internalizes it by positioning itself as uniquely capable of resolving the problems it raises. Crucially, the paper notes what such discourse omits: climate impact, labor effects, historical comparison. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates this picture by insisting that industry imaginaries are not monolithic "loud futuring" emanating from CEOs alone, but are fed by a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, communities, and pioneer groups (notably Effective Altruism and Rationalism), who supply the longtermist reservoir of ideas that loud futuring later amplifies — while presenting themselves, implausibly, as apolitical. Both papers trace longtermism/TESCREAL-adjacent thinking as a structuring ideology that displaces present harms with speculative existential risk.

### Platform and Corporate Discourse as Strategic Framing

A related set of papers examines how specific companies discursively manage accountability. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supplies the foundational move: the term "platform" itself does ideological work, borrowing computational, architectural, figurative, and political connotations to claim neutrality while occluding editorial power — a template later studies apply to AI specifically. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom deploying floating signifiers ("coordinated inauthentic behaviour," "authenticity") to externalize blame for platform harms while positioning the company as capable technological solver, with discourse terms diffusing into actual regulation. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds Google performing an analogous move with AI Overviews — framing a redefinition of search as seamless innovation while hedging accountability through "experimental" labels, with user voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent across all three actor groups studied (Google, journalists, SEO marketers).

### Naming the Decoys: Political Economy as the Missing Center

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the corpus's most systematic theoretical synthesis, arguing that AI critique itself can function as a "decoy" — ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory debates all seemingly substantive yet ultimately co-constituting and stabilizing the underlying "Project of AI," a world-building enterprise of capital and network power. This paper effectively names what [[Stanusch2026-ec]], [[Hepp2026-oi]], and [[Dodds2026-df]] each independently observe: that safety/longtermist/regulatory discourse, however earnest, tends to misdirect attention from financialization and infrastructural consolidation. [[Tornberg2026-im]] extends this political-economic framing forward, theorizing a shift from "platform society" to "AI society" in which struggles over visibility give way to "alignment politics" — contests over what models can say — and critical platform studies' concepts must be revised to grasp a new enclosure of cognition itself.

### Labor, Aesthetics, and the Underside of Imaginaries

Two further strands examine what grand imaginaries obscure at the level of labor and cultural form. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] turns to red-teaming, arguing it is sociotechnically shaped by value judgments, outsourced labor, and psychological harm largely invisible in public discourse about AI "safety" — a concrete instance of Vertesi et al.'s safety decoy. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] shift register entirely, reading AI "slop" and "slopaganda" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick and ambient rhetoric respectively, showing how generative content itself — gleeful, degraded, affectively "sticky" — encodes the labor arbitrage, alignment politics, and ideological attunement that the more discourse-analytic papers describe institutionally. Where [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Vertesi2026-lv]] trace decoys at the level of policy and controversy, these papers trace them at the level of aesthetic experience, showing that even ostensibly meaningless content is saturated with the same RLHF-driven, Global-South-labor-dependent political economy diagnosed elsewhere in the corpus.

### The Arc

Read together, these fifteen papers move from description (how is AI framed?) to diagnosis (why these frames, and whose interests do they serve?) to critique of critique itself (which forms of concern, even scholarly ones, inadvertently legitimize the industry). The throughline is that AI imaginaries — whether mentalistic metaphors in news copy, techno-hagiographic reverence for founders, platform neutrality claims, or safety-focused existential risk — consistently function to occupy the future, absorb present critique, and divert attention from labor, infrastructure, and capital concentration. The task these papers collectively set for critical AI studies is to resist both naive hype and naive debunking, instead making visible the mechanisms — premediation, preclusion, decoys, quiet futuring, cognitive lock-in — by which AI's power geometries reproduce themselves through discourse.
