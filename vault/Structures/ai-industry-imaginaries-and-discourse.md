# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Discursive Construction of AI's Power

The papers filed here converge on a single wager: that AI's power is not merely technical or economic but *narrative*—built, defended, and naturalized through recurring stories, metaphors, and silences. Taken together they trace a landscape running from the industry's own self-mythologizing, through the media apparatus that amplifies or contests it, to the critical vocabularies—slop, decline, decoy—that have emerged to name what the dominant narratives leave out.

### Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Longtermism, and Quiet Futuring

At the center of this cluster is the AI industry's own discursive labor. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers the most direct anatomy of this work, showing how the Sam Altman controversy crystallized three industry imaginaries—Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography—operationalized through twin strategies of "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (absorbing critique by positioning industry as sole capable steward). [[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates the picture by insisting that the "loud futuring" of CEOs studied by Stanusch is only half the story: a "quiet futuring" circulates among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies, all while presenting itself as apolitical. Both papers locate Effective Altruism and longtermist x-risk discourse as a displacement mechanism—one that trades present harms for speculative future ones. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this logic into a systematic framework of five "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) through which the "Project of AI" recruits even well-intentioned critics, journalists, and regulators into legitimating the very political economy they mean to contest—explicitly naming the "safety decoy" as the formal cousin of Stanusch's Longtermism and Hepp's quiet futuring. [[Richter2026-bt]] provides a longitudinal corrective to assumptions of monolithic industry dominance, showing that before the GenAI boom, US and German Twitter discourse diverged sharply—US toward individualized tech-influencer dominance, Germany toward institutional pluralism—suggesting that the conditions for industry capture of discourse are historically contingent rather than inevitable.

### Media as Amplifier, Critic, and Co-Producer

A second cluster examines how journalism and news media metabolize these industry narratives. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes AI hype not as discourse but as *infrastructure*—a systemic force redistributing legitimacy and resources through journalism's own professional anxieties, sourcing habits, and contradictory role as both hype-maker and hype-watchdog. This matches [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs, while present, is neither dominant nor monolithic—varying by outlet and serving critique as often as credulity—and [[Wang2025-zy]]'s cross-national mapping of utopian/dystopian "AI imaginaries" that shows media narratives refracting distinct national political economies (UK, US, China, India). [[Mota2026-sl59]] extends this comparative lens globally, finding a systematic Global North (risk-pessimist) / Global South (opportunity-optimist) asymmetry that encodes geopolitical position into affective tone. Two studies zoom into specific corporate-media interfaces: [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects how Meta's Newsroom deploys "floating signifiers" like coordinated inauthentic behaviour to externalize blame for platform harms while positioning the company as capable problem-solver—with these framings demonstrably migrating into regulatory language; [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] shows Google performing an analogous move with AI Overviews, hedging accountability through "experimental" framing while journalists and SEO marketers contest the stakes, with the user's voice and environmental costs notably absent from all three actors' discourse. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shows the same opacity operating at the level of practice rather than narrative: red-teaming is sold as rigorous technical safety work while its underlying labor arrangements, value judgments, and psychological costs remain invisible—exactly the kind of hidden infrastructure Dodds and Vertesi describe discourse as obscuring.

### Reframing the Object: From "Intelligence" to Communication and Enclosure

A third strand questions the master category "artificial intelligence" itself. Esposito, in [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] and [[Slack1783073271-slbb]], argues that framing systems like ChatGPT as intelligence-replicating is a category error: these are communication partners whose outputs are made meaningful by human interpretive labor, not machines that understand. This conceptual move parallels [[Tornberg2026-im]]'s macro-historical argument that generative AI marks a shift from "platform society" to "AI society," in which platform-era mechanisms of behavioral capture give way to "cognitive lock-in" and alignment politics—effectively theorizing the infrastructural analogue to the discursive capture other papers describe at the level of narrative.

### Naming the Wreckage: Slop, Decline, and Diminished Expectations

A final cluster turns to the vernacular and affective registers through which publics process AI's degraded promises. [[Galip2026-ix]] theorizes "slop" via Ngai's gimmick—a form that works simultaneously too little and too hard, exposing cultural production's contradictions under "too late capitalism." [[Topinka2026-hb]] extends this into the political register with "slopaganda," arguing that AI-generated political content works not through deception but affective attunement, with alignment/RLHF literally training models to resonate with existing ideological formations. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] historicizes this entire mood, showing how "internet decline narratives"—enshittification, slop, dead internet theory—have become the dominant mode for narrating technological history, while cautioning that such declinism risks a US-centric "corporational determinism" and erases user agency and non-Western experience. [[Baym2026-tr]]'s retrospective closes the loop empirically: the four harms she named in 2015 (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) have demonstrably worsened, lending material weight to the decline narratives Hegarty treats critically, while her call to move "beyond critique" echoes Vertesi's warning that critique alone can be captured as another decoy.

### The Arc

Read together, these papers chart a shift from identifying *what* industry says (hagiography, longtermism, inevitability) to interrogating *how* saying it works (premediation, floating signifiers, alignment-as-attunement) to asking what vocabulary critics themselves should use without being recruited into the apparatus they study (decoys, quiet futuring, slop, decline). The collection's own throughline is a hard methodological question: as AI imaginaries proliferate across LinkedIn controversies, newsroom press releases, search engine summaries, and meme videos, each new act of naming—even critical naming—risks becoming infrastructure for the next cycle of industry narrative.
