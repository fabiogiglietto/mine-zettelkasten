# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media, Narrative, and the Reproduction of Industry Power

The bulk of this collection interrogates how the AI industry's preferred self-understanding — inevitable, benevolent, apolitical, and best-positioned to solve its own problems — gets circulated, laundered, and occasionally contested across media ecosystems. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the sharpest theoretical vocabulary for this dynamic: analyzing the Sam Altman firing/rehiring controversy, it identifies "Longtermism," "Regulatory Ambivalence," and "Techno-Hagiography" as dominant industry imaginaries, sustained through twin strategies of *premediation* (deflecting present critique into speculative futures) and *preclusion* (absorbing critique by positioning industry as the only competent problem-solver). This vocabulary of displacement resonates directly with [[Vertesi2026-lv]]'s more totalizing argument that AI accountability discourse itself is structured around five "decoys" — ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory — that seem to challenge AI's political economy while actually stabilizing it. Where Stanusch et al. find industry imaginaries incompletely monopolized (X/Twitter counter-imaginaries around governance urgency and intersectionality push back), Vertesi et al. go further, warning that even well-intentioned FAccT-style critique can be enrolled into legitimating the "Project of AI" if it engages decoys at face value rather than interrogating financialization and network power directly.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates this picture geographically and sociologically, arguing that critical AI-imaginary scholarship has overfocused on the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors while neglecting a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies. This bidirectional loud/quiet relationship helps explain why Longtermist and existential-risk framing (identified independently by Stanusch et al. as displacing present-day harms) recurs so persistently: it is not simply CEO rhetoric but a durable output of Bay Area social figurations that present themselves as apolitical while being, per Hepp, "deeply political."

## Journalism as Amplifier, Watchdog, and Site of Struggle

A second cluster examines journalism's ambivalent structural role. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes "AI hype" not as discursive froth but as systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources — journalists are simultaneously hype-makers (via sourcing patterns favoring tech executives) and hype-watchers (via watchdog critique), a contradiction that explains why debunking hype alone is insufficient; the paper calls instead for "breakdown-and-repair" work that renders hype's mechanisms visible. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] empirically tests one dimension of this — mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs — finding that anthropomorphism is real but not monolithic, driven more by outlet-level editorial practice than region, and serving critique as often as promotion; ChatGPT/OpenAI's outsized share of coverage nonetheless shows how a single industry actor can define an entire beat. [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] extend the frame comparatively across nations: both find that Global North coverage skews risk-oriented and pessimistic while Global South (and Chinese/Indian) coverage skews developmental and optimistic, suggesting that "AI imaginaries" are not a single industry export but are refracted through national political-economic positioning — a point [[Richter2026-bt]] substantiates historically by showing that the stakeholder composition of AI discourse (industry-dominated and increasingly individual/influencer-driven in the US vs. institutionalized and pluralistic in Germany) was already diverging on Twitter a decade before the GenAI boom, with lasting consequences for each country's regulatory posture.

## Platform Self-Narration and Discursive Control

Where the above papers examine media *about* AI, [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] examine platforms' own discursive self-fashioning. Hurcombe's analysis of Meta's Newsroom shows a rhetorical playbook — externalizing "problematic content" onto inauthentic outside actors while claiming technological competence to fix it — that closely parallels the premediation/preclusion logic Stanusch et al. identify in OpenAI-adjacent discourse, and that arguably prefigures the "decoy" structure Vertesi et al. theorize at industry-wide scale. Weinbrand's study of Google's AI Overviews shows Google performing a similar hedge (calling a major epistemic infrastructure shift "experimental" while journalists and SEO marketers contest its authority), with user, environmental, and democratic concerns conspicuously absent from all three actors' discourse — an absence that echoes Vertesi et al.'s claim that decoys work precisely by directing attention away from material and infrastructural costs.

## Geopolitics, Training Data, and the Limits of Discursive Analysis

Two papers push beyond discourse analysis toward the material substrates through which power operates. [[Waight2026-ts]] demonstrates empirically that state media control literally seeps into LLM training corpora and outputs, showing that institutional power shapes AI not only through public narrative but through the technical pipeline itself — a finding that materializes what [[Beacken2026-zb]] approaches from political science, arguing that GenAI-enabled propaganda in democratically weakened states (Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, Turkey) is not a uniform, tech-deterministic phenomenon but is conditioned by local structural factors. Together they caution against treating "AI industry discourse" as a monolith even as they confirm that power asymmetries (state or corporate) reliably find channels into AI systems.

## Publics, Users, and the Limits of Industry Narrative Control

Several papers gauge whether industry-preferred imaginaries actually land with publics, or whether lived experience produces friction. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] finds Italians hold a "critical ambivalence" rather than uncritical enthusiasm, with symbolic imagery (humanoid robots) persisting mainly among those lacking direct AI experience — suggesting that direct use erodes industry-cultivated mystique. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] (Anthropic's own user survey) is a useful counterpoint precisely because it is industry-authored: it frames displacement anxiety and productivity gains in terms amenable to Anthropic's Economic Index program, illustrating how even ostensibly neutral "research" can double as imaginary-construction. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] offers a conceptual corrective underlying much of this discourse: ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is parasitic on human repair work, not genuine understanding — a finding that quietly undercuts the mentalistic/agentic framings [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds circulating in the press and the techno-hagiographic mythos [[Stanusch2026-ec]] identifies in industry discourse. Meanwhile [[Unknown2025-qj]]'s study of public red-teaming shows one concrete site where publics are invited into AI safety work, though even here institutional framing determines what counts as a harm worth surfacing — a micro-level echo of the "safety decoy" Vertesi et al. describe at scale.

## Synthesis

Read together, these papers trace an arc from *narrative* (how imaginaries are built and circulated in media, platforms, and industry self-presentation) to *structure* (how those imaginaries function to displace attention from political economy, labor, and material infrastructure) to *materialization* (how power actually gets encoded into training data, stakeholder networks, and regulatory capture). [[Baym2026-tr]]'s reflection on a decade of platform critique supplies a fitting coda: despite voluminous critical scholarship — much of it represented in this very set — concentrated wealth, opaque algorithms, precarious labor, and data extraction have only intensified. The throughline connecting [[Stanusch2026-ec]], [[Vertesi2026-lv]], [[Hepp2026-oi]], and [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] is a shared wager that naming the discursive mechanisms — premediation, decoys, quiet futuring, floating signifiers — is necessary but not sufficient; the papers collectively press toward Dodds et al.'s call for "breakdown and repair," insisting that critical AI scholarship must guard against being itself recruited into the very imaginaries it sets out to analyze.
