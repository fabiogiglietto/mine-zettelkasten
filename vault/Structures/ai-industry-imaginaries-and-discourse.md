# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Industry's Own Mythology: Longtermism, Heroism, and Decoys

At the core of this topic sit three papers that most directly theorize how the AI industry narrates itself into legitimacy. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the November 2023 Sam Altman controversy as a moment when industry-aligned discourse crystallized into three recognizable imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography — and shows how these operate through "premediation" (deflecting present concerns into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning the industry as uniquely qualified to solve the problems it creates). This vocabulary of temporal displacement finds a structural echo in [[Vertesi2026-lv]], which reframes AI development as a "Project" of capital accumulation and identifies five "decoys" — ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory — that seemingly invite critique while actually stabilizing the underlying political economy. Where Stanusch traces imaginaries as they surface in a controversy, Vertesi generalizes the mechanism: even accountability researchers, journalists, and regulators can be "enrolled" into legitimating the very power structures they mean to interrogate. Both papers converge on a shared diagnosis that existential-risk and safety framings function less as sincere concern than as strategic instruments of futurity-management.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates this picture by insisting that the "loud futuring" of CEOs and financiers examined by Stanusch and Vertesi is only half the story. Its ethnography of San Francisco's AI communities reveals a "quiet futuring" — discursive thickenings around AGI arrival, job loss, and geopolitical competition, incubated in effective-altruist and rationalist milieus — that supplies the ideational reservoir loud futuring later amplifies. This adds an important corrective: Longtermism, as identified by Stanusch, is not simply a public-relations posture invented for crisis management but has genealogical roots in a bounded social figuration (EA, Rationalism) that presents itself as apolitical while doing intensely political work of world-building. Read together, these three papers establish a layered model of industry imaginary-production: grassroots ideational communities (Hepp) feed elite hagiographic and longtermist narratives (Stanusch) that in turn function as decoys diverting scrutiny from material accumulation (Vertesi).

## Journalism as Amplifier, Filter, and Occasional Critic

A second cluster examines how news media metabolize — rather than originate — these imaginaries. [[Dodds2026-df]] offers the most systemic account, arguing that AI hype should be understood not as discourse or bubble but as infrastructure: a set of professional practices, sourcing habits, and managerial anxieties that redistribute legitimacy and resources before technologies materialize. This matches [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal finding that stakeholder composition on Twitter/X diverged sharply between the US (increasingly dominated by industry and individual tech influencers) and Germany (progressively institutionalized around academia, advocacy, and government) — suggesting that "who gets to speak" for AI is itself a path-dependent outcome of national media ecologies, not a fixed feature of the technology's meaning.

[[Nguyen2026-vm]] and [[Wang2025-zy]] both push back against monolithic readings of media complicity. Nguyen's content analysis of 18,000+ articles finds that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs, while present, is neither dominant nor uniformly uncritical — it can index adversarial mockery as easily as corporate hype, and its variation tracks outlet-level editorial practice more than region. Wang's comparative mapping of UK, US, Chinese, and Indian coverage operationalizes Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears framework to show that national sociopolitical context, not a single global "AI imaginary," structures utopian/dystopian emphasis. [[Mota2026-sl59]] extends this geopolitical lens temporally and North/South, showing pessimistic risk-framing concentrated in Global North outlets against developmental optimism in the Global South, with US–China rivalry as a persistent organizing frame — a "weapon or wonder" dualism that reveals how the same technology is narrated to serve divergent state and market interests. Together, Nguyen, Wang, and Mota demonstrate that journalism is not a passive echo chamber for industry imaginaries but a differentiated, geographically and institutionally contingent site of negotiation — even as Dodds's account of hype-as-infrastructure suggests the underlying sourcing and professional incentives still tilt coverage toward tech-executive perspectives.

## Corporate Platforms Narrating Their Own Legitimacy

A more granular register of analysis turns to how specific companies discursively manage controversy and redefine their own products. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom using floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" to externalize responsibility for platform harms onto outside bad actors while presenting Meta as technologically capable problem-solver — a framing move that, notably, has diffused into regulatory language in the EU and Australia, illustrating how corporate discourse can shape the very policy instruments meant to constrain it. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] performs a structurally similar analysis of Google's AI Overviews, tracing how Google frames a contested redefinition of search as a seamless, hedge-labeled "experiment," while journalists foreground hallucination risk and SEO marketers voice commercial anxiety — with user experience, environmental cost, and democratic implications strikingly absent from all three discourses. Both papers exemplify at the level of individual platforms the "decoy" logic Vertesi theorizes abstractly: technical/procedural framings (content moderation tools, AI-generated summaries) absorb critical attention that might otherwise interrogate business models and infrastructural power.

## Safety Practices as Discursive and Material Labor

[[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] extend the critique of industry narrative-making into the specific practice of red-teaming, arguing it has been "rapidly normalized" as a safety guarantee despite opaque, market-driven organization. Gillespie draws the parallel to commercial content moderation's history to expose the value judgments, outsourced labor, and psychological toll hidden behind red-teaming's technical veneer, cautioning that automation narratives risk erasing this labor from view entirely — a direct instance of Vertesi's "safety decoy" made concrete at the level of workplace practice. Unknown2025-qj complements this by examining public-interest red-teaming events, suggesting that participatory formats can partially counter the internal, proprietary framing of harm that Gillespie critiques, though institutional context still shapes what counts as a vulnerability worth naming. Both papers insist that safety discourse cannot be assessed as rhetoric alone; it must be traced into labor arrangements and organizational design.

## Publics, Reception, and Aesthetic Counter-Currents

The final cluster turns from producers of imaginaries to their reception and unruly cultural residue. [[Fattorini2026-bo]] grounds the discussion empirically, finding Italian publics hold a "critical ambivalence" toward AI — high exposure but low self-assessed understanding, context-dependent acceptance, and symbolic reliance on humanoid-robot imagery — which suggests that industry and media imaginaries do not simply transfer intact into lay understanding but are refracted through demographic and experiential variables. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Manovich2026-ih]] offer more provocative counterpoints from cultural and media theory: Galip reads "AI slop" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick as a form that inadvertently exposes the contradictions of "too late capitalism," suggesting stigmatized generative content may carry frictional, resistant potential rather than simply reflecting industry hype. Manovich, meanwhile, deliberately brackets social and ethical critique to analyze generative AI as a medium with its own cognitive and aesthetic logic — a move that stands somewhat apart from this topic's critical thrust but usefully demonstrates the discursive plurality this literature must contend with: even scholarly accounts of "what AI is" are themselves imaginary-laden choices about where to place analytical attention, echoing Vertesi's warning that focusing on artifacts (here, aesthetic ones) can itself become a way of not looking at political economy.

## Synthesis

Across these fifteen papers, a consistent architecture emerges: imaginaries are produced in specific communities (Hepp's quiet futuring, EA/Rationalist milieus), condensed into public-facing narrative strategies during moments of crisis (Stanusch), stabilized as decoys that redirect accountability efforts (Vertesi), and then circulated, filtered, and sometimes contested through journalism (Dodds, Nguyen, Wang, Mota, Richter) and corporate platform discourse (Hurcombe, Weinbrand). Beneath the level of narrative, material practices like red-teaming (Gillespie, Unknown2025-qj) show how safety rhetoric is entangled with labor exploitation, while public reception (Fattorini) and cultural residue (Galip, Manovich) reveal that these imaginaries never land as intended — audiences remain ambivalent, and the technology's own detritus can generate friction against the industry's preferred story of seamless, inevitable progress.
