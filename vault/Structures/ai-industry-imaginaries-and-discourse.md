# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media Frames and the Reproduction of Techno-Capitalist Narratives

A large cluster of these papers treats journalism as a co-producer, rather than a neutral reporter, of AI's public meaning. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs is real but far from dominant, and that anthropomorphic language serves multiple functions—uncritical repetition of vendor claims, routine explanation, and adversarial critique—complicating any simple hype/critique binary. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across the UK, US, China, and India, showing that utopian/dystopian tropes (per Cave and Dihal) are inflected by national political-economic context rather than universal. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into a Global North/South divide: Northern coverage is risk-saturated and pessimistic, Southern coverage developmental and optimistic, with US–China rivalry as a recurring geopolitical anchor—evidence that AI imaginaries are never merely technological but always inflected by a nation's structural position in the world economy. [[Beacken2026-zb]] pushes this structuralist argument into authoritarian contexts, arguing against techno-deterministic assumptions that GenAI propaganda spreads or functions uniformly; instead, local political and economic conditions in weakened democracies determine its form.

[[Dodds2026-df]]'s editorial reframes "hype" itself as an infrastructural, structuring force rather than mere discourse—something that redistributes legitimacy and resources, is produced and policed simultaneously by journalists, and depends on narrow sourcing practices that privilege tech executives. This systemic view of hype dovetails with [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal finding that the *sources* of AI discourse have diverged sharply by country: US Twitter discourse became a tech-influencer echo chamber while German discourse institutionalized around academic, advocacy, and governmental accounts—suggesting that who gets to narrate AI's future is itself a contested, path-dependent outcome with downstream regulatory consequences.

## Industry Self-Narration: Imaginaries, Heroics, and Discursive Cover

Several papers turn directly to how AI firms and their surrounding communities construct legitimating narratives. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] dissects the Sam Altman controversy to identify Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry imaginaries, arguing that firms use "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning themselves as the only legitimate problem-solvers) to monopolize the discursive terrain—though counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter show this control is incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by insisting that critical AI studies has overfixated on the "loud futuring" of CEOs, neglecting the "quiet futuring" of Bay Area labs, EA/Rationalist communities, and pioneer groups whose AGI-timeline debates, job-loss discourse, and geopolitical framing supply the ideational reservoir that loud futuring later amplifies—while presenting itself, contradictorily, as apolitical.

[[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] apply discourse-analytic methods to specific corporate communications infrastructures. Hurcombe shows how Meta's Newsroom externalizes responsibility for platform harms onto "inauthentic" outside actors while claiming technological mastery over the problem—terms like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" then diffuse into regulatory language, illustrating a feedback loop between corporate framing and policy. Weinbrand finds Google's AI Overviews discourse similarly hedges accountability through "experimental" framing while journalists voice critical caution and SEO marketers register commercial anxiety—with users' voices and environmental costs conspicuously absent across all three actor groups. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], the historical anchor of this cluster, shows the template for this move: the very word "platform" was strategically deployed by YouTube to connote neutrality and openness while obscuring substantive editorial and commercial interventions—a discursive sleight of hand that the AI-era papers show being redeployed and updated.

## Political Economy Critique: Naming the Decoys

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the most systematic counter-framework, arguing that AI is best understood as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise through which capitalized actors consolidate power—and that prevailing critiques (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) function as "decoys" that appear substantive but actually stabilize the underlying political economy. This paper functions as a theoretical keystone for the topic: it explains *why* imaginaries like Longtermism ([[Stanusch2026-ec]]) or techno-hagiographic hero narratives ([[Hepp2026-oi]]) do political work, and why even well-intentioned accountability efforts (red-teaming, fairness audits) risk becoming enrolled into legitimation rather than critique. [[Baym2026-tr]] arrives at a structurally similar diagnosis from a different angle—a decade-long retrospective showing that wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, labor precarity, and data extraction have all intensified despite abundant critical scholarship, and calling for research that moves beyond diagnosis toward actual intervention.

## Labor, Infrastructure, and the Human Costs Behind the Narrative

A related set of papers insists that AI imaginaries obscure concrete labor arrangements. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine red-teaming as a sociotechnical practice rather than a purely technical safety procedure: the former tracing parallels to content moderation's history of outsourced, psychologically taxing "ghost work" and warning that automation narratives distract from urgent labor concerns; the latter documenting how public red-teaming events can widen participation in defining AI harms even as institutional framing shapes what counts as harm at all. Both push against sanitized industry narratives of "safety" that erase the humans doing the adversarial imagining. [[Triedman2025-uy]]'s empirical dissection of Grokipedia extends this critical-infrastructure lens to knowledge platforms themselves, showing how an ostensibly AI-native encyclopedia is in fact heavily derivative of Wikipedia while laundering ideologically slanted, unreliable sourcing under a veneer of technological novelty—a concrete instantiation of how "AI-generated" framing can mask both extraction and political bias.

## Aesthetic and Cultural Registers of Critique

Finally, [[Galip2026-ix]] shifts register from institutional discourse to cultural aesthetics, reading "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick as forms that are simultaneously too effortless and too strained, exposing the contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism." This paper usefully complicates the topic's overall arc: where most contributions here trace how industry narratives *legitimize* power by producing coherence (heroic founders, inevitable futures, neutral platforms), Galip suggests that the sheer aesthetic degeneracy of everyday generative output may itself constitute a frictional, if ambivalent, site of resistance to the frictionless futures that industry imaginaries promise.

Together, these papers trace an arc from *where* AI narratives circulate (news media, corporate newsrooms, social platforms, community spaces) to *how* they are constructed (framing devices, imaginaries, discursive decoys, gimmicks) to *what* they obscure (labor, infrastructure, political economy, geopolitical asymmetry)—suggesting that critical AI discourse studies is converging on political economy and labor as the load-bearing categories beneath the industry's preferred vocabulary of futures, risk, and disruption.
