# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Industry Imaginaries: Naming the Narratives

The core of this topic is the effort to name and dissect the narrative repertoires through which the AI industry presents itself. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the most precise vocabulary: analyzing the Altman firing/rehiring episode, it identifies Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, and shows how the industry absorbs critique through two complementary moves—"premediation" (deflecting present concerns into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning itself as the only legitimate solver of the problems it raises). This diagnosis of displacement is echoed and radicalized by [[Vertesi2026-lv]], which reframes AI development as a "Project" of capital accumulation and identifies five systemic "decoys"—ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, and regulatory—that induce critics, regulators, and even accountability researchers to fight on terrain that ultimately stabilizes the industry's political economy rather than threatening it. Where Stanusch's imaginaries operate at the level of discourse during a crisis moment, Vertesi's decoys operate as a structural grammar that channels critique itself into legitimation.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates both accounts spatially and sociologically, arguing that critical AI scholarship has overfixated on the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors (the kind of techno-hagiography Stanusch documents) while neglecting the "quiet futuring" of Bay Area labs, Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities, and start-up houses that supply the ideational reservoir—especially long-termist and existential-risk framings—later amplified by loud futuring. This paper effectively traces Longtermism's social infrastructure back to the EA/Rationalist milieu Stanusch flags as its origin, showing how an imaginary that appears "apolitical" is constitutively political in shaping AGI timelines, labor futures, and geopolitical framing.

## Media and Journalism as Co-Producers of Imaginaries

A second cluster examines how news media take up, refract, or contest industry narratives rather than simply transmitting them. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] complicates any simple "media amplifies hype" story: across a large corpus of English-language coverage, mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but not dominant, and anthropomorphic language serves uncritical reproduction, routine simplification, *and* adversarial critique—suggesting journalism is a contested site of epistemic negotiation rather than a passive conduit. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends the comparative lens cross-nationally, mapping utopian/dystopian "AI imaginaries" across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers and showing these diverge along national political-economic lines. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into an explicit Global North/South split, finding Northern outlets structurally pessimistic and risk-oriented while Southern outlets frame AI developmentally and optimistically—read against a backdrop of US-China "weapon vs. wonder" geopolitical framing and Big Tech infrastructural concentration.

[[Dodds2026-df]] theorizes the underlying mechanism these studies observe: AI hype is not merely discursive but a systemic, structuring infrastructure embedded in journalism's community of practice, sustained through repetitive sourcing patterns that privilege tech executives and foreclose alternative actors and futures. [[Richter2026-bt]], using longitudinal Twitter data from the pre-GenAI formative decade, offers a valuable corrective and complication: stakeholder composition driving AI discourse was not uniformly industry-dominated but diverged sharply by country, with Germany institutionalizing around academic/advocacy/governmental actors while the US discourse became increasingly concentrated among industry voices and tech influencers—a divergence the authors argue conditions each country's subsequent GenAI regulatory trajectory.

## Platforms Narrating Themselves

A related set of papers turns to corporate self-presentation as a discursive practice in its own right. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse on "problematic content," showing how floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" externalize blame onto outside bad actors while Meta positions itself as capable problem-solver—a frame that has since diffused into regulatory language itself. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] performs a structurally similar analysis of Google's AI Overviews, tracing how Google, journalists, and SEO marketers compete to legitimize or contest a reconfiguration of search, trust, and epistemic authority, with user and societal/environmental concerns conspicuously absent across all three discourses. [[Triedman2025-uy]] provides an empirical, almost forensic counterpart: a large-scale comparison of Grokipedia against Wikipedia reveals the ideological fingerprints beneath Musk's "alternative" knowledge platform—heavier reliance on unreliable and blacklisted sources, ideological divergence concentrated precisely in politically controversial entries, and even citation of Musk's own social media posts as sourcing, illustrating how techno-hagiographic self-mythologizing can be materially encoded into infrastructure.

## Political Economy, Enclosure, and the Limits of Critique

[[Tornberg2026-im]] situates these discursive dynamics within a broader structural argument: generative AI marks a transition from "platform society" to "AI society," in which platform-era monopoly mechanisms give way to "cognitive lock-in" and struggles over visibility give way to "alignment politics"—contests over what models can say, refuse, or do. This paper, like Vertesi's, insists that critical AI studies must track enclosure of cognition and infrastructure rather than surface-level technical debates. [[Baym2026-tr]] offers a retrospective register to this argument, revisiting a decade-old diagnosis of social media's threats (wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious labor, data extraction) to show these have intensified rather than abated under generative AI, embodied in the rise of a tech "broligarchy"—and calls for scholarship to move beyond critique toward interdisciplinary intervention, a sentiment that resonates with Vertesi's worry that critique alone can be absorbed as a decoy. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] grounds this political economy in labor: AI red-teaming, following the trajectory of content moderation, is a sociotechnical practice whose value judgments, outsourced labor arrangements, and psychological costs remain hidden behind a market-driven, individually-defined notion of "safety"—itself one of Vertesi's named decoys.

## Conceptual Undercurrents: What Is Being Narrated?

[[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] supplies a conceptual counterpoint underlying much of this literature's skepticism toward industry self-description: it argues that the "intelligence" framing of systems like ChatGPT misconstrues what these systems actually do, since their apparent success derives from human interpretive labor rather than machine understanding—an argument that quietly undercuts both techno-hagiographic and longtermist imaginaries by denying their basic ontological premise.

## The Cultural Backwash: Slop, Decline, and Contested Meaning

A final cluster examines how publics, rather than industry or journalists, make sense of generative AI's output and its degradation of informational trust—effectively the reception-side counterpart to the production-side imaginaries above. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] historicizes "internet decline narratives" (enshittification, slop, dead internet theory) as a dominant but risky interpretive frame that can reinstate corporate-centric, nostalgic, and Western-centric histories even as it voices legitimate dissent. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] theorize AI "slop" and "slopaganda" through aesthetic and affective registers—Ngai's gimmick and ambient rhetoric, respectively—arguing that these forms are neither simply deficient nor simply excessive but expose contradictions of platform capitalism and function as "ideological attunement machines" optimized via alignment and RLHF. [[Li2026-zv]] and [[So2026-up]] bring this down to empirical and conceptual bedrock: Li's folk-taxonomy study of Reddit communities shows ordinary users already possess a sophisticated, contested vocabulary for AI content that official detection/moderation regimes fail to capture, while So's SLOP framework reconceives the phenomenon not as a content category but as a social condition in which the evaluative signals once used to judge quality have broken down—a diagnosis that, like Vertesi's and Tornberg's, locates the crisis not in individual artifacts but in the collapse of trustworthy infrastructures for judgment itself.

Together, these twenty papers trace an arc from the identification of specific industry imaginaries (Longtermism, Techno-Hagiography, quiet futuring) through their circulation and contestation in journalism and platform self-narration, to the political-economic and infrastructural "decoys" that make such narratives so effective, and finally to the degraded informational ecology—slop, declinism, broken evaluative signals—that these imaginaries help produce and obscure.
