# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Imaginary as Analytic Object

This cluster treats AI discourse not as neutral description but as world-making practice — a set of narratives, metaphors, and framings that actively construct what AI *is* taken to be, who gets to speak about it, and which concerns are rendered visible or invisible. The conceptual lineage runs back through [[Bucher2017-qh]], whose notion of the "algorithmic imaginary" establishes that people's affective, felt experience of algorithms — not their technical substrate — is itself generative, recursively shaping the systems it describes. That insight, recast at the level of industry and media rather than individual users, animates nearly every paper here: imaginaries are not false consciousness to be corrected by better facts, but productive forces that mobilize resources, redistribute legitimacy, and foreclose alternatives.

## Journalism as Co-Producer of AI Imaginaries

A strong empirical strand examines how news media construct AI's meaning. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs, while present, do not uniformly dominate coverage; a "techno-capitalist master frame" organizes global AI news, with ChatGPT/OpenAI occupying disproportionate attention. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparatively, mapping utopian/dystopian "hopes and fears" tropes across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers and showing how national political-economic conditions inflect otherwise similar narrative structures. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into an explicit Global North/South divide, finding Northern coverage risk-oriented and pessimistic against Southern coverage's developmental optimism — a geopolitical asymmetry that recasts AI as "weapon" or "wonder" depending on one's structural position. [[Richter2026-bt]] complicates any simple "industry dominates discourse" story by tracing a decade of Twitter stakeholder composition, showing German discourse institutionalizing toward academic/advocacy actors while US discourse concentrated into industry and, increasingly, individual tech-influencer voices — a divergence the paper argues pre-structured each country's subsequent GenAI regulatory posture. Synthesizing this terrain, [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes "AI hype" itself as a systemic infrastructure embedded in journalistic practice — sourcing patterns, professional anxiety, geopolitical framing — rather than a transient discursive bubble, and calls for "breakdown and repair" scholarship that renders hype's mechanisms visible.

## Corporate Self-Narration and Platform Discourse

A second strand turns from media-about-AI to companies' own discursive self-positioning. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom uses "floating signifiers" like *coordinated inauthentic behaviour* to externalize responsibility for platform harms onto outside bad actors while presenting Meta as the competent technological solver — a framing that has since diffused into regulatory language itself. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds a structurally similar move in Google's launch of AI Overviews: Google frames the shift from indexical gatekeeper to generative epistemic authority as seamless and user-friendly while hedging accountability through "experimental" labels, against which journalists mount cautionary counter-discourse and SEO marketers voice commercial anxiety — with user and environmental concerns conspicuously absent from all three actor positions. Both papers demonstrate a recurring discursive architecture: ambiguous terminology and selective visibility work to legitimize corporate power while deflecting scrutiny from platform-level and business-model causes of harm.

## Industry's Own Mythologies: Longtermism, Hagiography, and Quiet Futuring

Closer to the industry's internal self-understanding, [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the November 2023 Altman controversy as a critical moment that surfaces dominant industry imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography — managed through "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning the industry as uniquely able to solve problems it creates). Counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) reveal this monopolization as incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this account by insisting that the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors is only half the picture: a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities, and co-working spaces supplies the ideational reservoir — AGI timelines, job-loss narratives, AI geopolitics — that loud futuring later amplifies, all while presenting itself, self-servingly, as apolitical. Together these two papers trace how imaginaries originate not just in press releases but in a layered ecology of insider communities and crisis moments.

## Political Economy Beneath the Narrative

A more structurally minded set of papers argues that discourse analysis must be tethered to material power. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] is the most programmatic statement: AI is a "Project" of world-building through which capitalized actors assemble networks of power and capital, and critiques that engage AI's "decoys" — ontological ambiguity, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulation — inadvertently co-construct and stabilize the very political economy they aim to contest. This argument resonates with [[Tornberg2026-im]]'s account of a shift from "platform society" to "AI society," in which platform-era monopoly mechanisms give way to "cognitive lock-in" and struggles over visibility become "alignment politics" — an enclosure of cognition itself that demands updated critical-AI concepts. [[Baym2026-tr]] supplies a sobering retrospective from adjacent terrain: the wealth-inequality, algorithmic-opacity, labor-precarity, and data-extraction harms she diagnosed in social media a decade ago have intensified under the "broligarchy," with generative AI now appropriating user data at even greater scale — a reminder that imaginary-critique must eventually cash out in material accountability. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] grounds this political-economy lens in a specific practice, showing how AI red-teaming, marketed as rigorous technical safety work, actually reproduces content-moderation-style labor arrangements, outsourcing, and psychological harm — hidden infrastructure that hype narratives about "automated safety" render invisible.

## The Affective and Aesthetic Underside: Slop, Brainrot, Slopaganda

A final cluster examines the cultural residue of these imaginaries at the level of circulating content itself. [[Galip2026-ix]] reads AI "slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick — devices that work simultaneously "too little" (deskilling, labor-saving) and "too hard" (straining for attention) — arguing these degraded forms expose contradictions of cultural production under platform capitalism while harboring unexpected critical friction. [[Topinka2026-hb]] extends this into explicitly political territory, theorizing "slopaganda" as an affective lure rather than belief-manipulating propaganda: AI content that is obviously fake yet affectively potent, optimized through RLHF alignment to attune to ambient platform moods rather than to persuade coherently. Both papers insist that the industry's narratives of frictionless, benevolent technological progress are belied by the genuinely disordered, often racialized and exploitative cultural detritus the same systems produce.

## Against the Premise: Reframing "Intelligence" Itself

Underlying all of the above is a terminological question that [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] presses most directly: the very label "artificial intelligence" may already be a framing move, smuggling in cognitivist assumptions that these systems replicate human understanding when they in fact succeed by giving humans material from which *they* generate meaning. This argument functions as a conceptual counterpart to the empirical and political-economic critiques elsewhere in the set — a reminder that the industry's most powerful discursive device may be the master category "AI" itself, prior to any specific narrative of longtermism, hagiography, or hype built upon it.
