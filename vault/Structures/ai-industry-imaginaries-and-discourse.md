# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media as Co-Producer of AI's Sociotechnical Imaginaries

A cluster of papers treats journalism not as a neutral conduit but as an active site where AI's public meaning is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from dominant in global news coverage, and that anthropomorphic language does contradictory work — sometimes reproducing tech-business narratives uncritically, sometimes fueling adversarial critique (calling chatbots "bullshitters"). This variation, the authors argue, tracks organisational and editorial practice more than national culture, complicating any simple story of a monolithic "hype machine." [[Wang2025-zy]] extends the comparative lens using Cave and Dihal's hopes-and-fears framework across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, showing that utopian/dystopian tropes are inflected by each country's political-economic position rather than emerging from technology "as such." [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into an explicit Global North/South asymmetry: Northern coverage is risk-saturated and pessimistic, Southern coverage developmental and optimistic, with AI oscillating between "weapon" and "wonder" in a discourse structured by US–China rivalry and periodised around Cambridge Analytica and ChatGPT. Together these three papers establish that AI imaginaries are geographically stratified products of media systems, not simply industry talking points passively relayed.

[[Richter2026-bt]] adds a crucial historical corrective to this picture: before the GenAI boom, who spoke for AI on Twitter diverged sharply by country, with Germany institutionalising toward academia/advocacy/government while the US moved toward pure industry and, increasingly, individual tech-influencer dominance. This "formative decade" stakeholder asymmetry, the paper argues, pre-configured the very media and regulatory environments that [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] observe post-2022 — a reminder that today's imaginaries have discourse-network prehistories.

## Hype as Infrastructure, Not Just Narrative

[[Dodds2026-df]] reframes "AI hype" itself: rather than a discursive bubble, hype is a systemic infrastructure that redistributes legitimacy and resources, sedimented in journalistic communities of practice through repetitive sourcing (tech executives over civil society), temporal narratives of inevitability, and geopolitically specific framings. This account dovetails with [[Nguyen2026-vm]]'s finding that critical denials of AI agency are rare, and gives structural explanation for why they are rare — hype is baked into newsroom incentives and source hierarchies, not merely journalistic naivety. [[Baym2026-tr]] offers a retrospective political-economy companion piece, arguing that despite a decade of critical scholarship exposing platform harms, wealth concentration ("broligarchy"), algorithmic opacity, and data extraction have all intensified, with generative AI now folding user-generated content into a new round of extraction. Baym's call to move from critique-for-its-own-sake to interdisciplinary intervention resonates with Dodds's "breakdown and repair" agenda: naming hype's mechanisms is necessary but insufficient without engaged redesign of the underlying incentive structures.

## Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Longtermism, and Quiet Futuring

At the discursive core of the topic sit three papers that dissect how AI industry actors narrate themselves. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] uses the Sam Altman controversy as a natural experiment, identifying Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as the industry's dominant self-imaginaries, deployed via two rhetorical strategies — "premediation" (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning industry as uniquely capable of resolving the problems it raises). Crucially, present-day harms (climate, labor, historical precedent) are shown to be structurally absent from this discourse, while counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter (Governing Urgency, Intersectionality) demonstrate that the industry's monopoly on AI's future is incomplete. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by insisting that scholarship has overweighted such "loud futuring" by CEOs and underweighted the "quiet futuring" of Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and Effective Altruist/Rationalist networks — the reservoir of longtermist ideas (AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitics) that loud futuring later condenses and amplifies. Both papers converge on longtermism as the load-bearing imaginary connecting industry hagiography to existential-risk discourse, and both stress that apparently apolitical framing (Altman-as-genius; technologists' "silly tech thing" self-deprecation) is itself deeply political.

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] pushes this critique furthest by arguing that even the concerns raised by critical scholars — ontology, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulation — function as "decoys" that co-constitute rather than challenge the underlying "Project of AI": a world-building enterprise through which financiers and corporations consolidate network power. Read against [[Stanusch2026-ec]] and [[Hepp2026-oi]], this paper supplies the political-economic mechanism behind premediation and quiet futuring — longtermism and safety talk are not merely rhetorical postures but decoys that enroll well-meaning critics, regulators, and participatory-design efforts into legitimating the very accumulation they seek to check. The "inevitability decoy" and "safety decoy" in particular give conceptual precision to what Hepp calls the AGI-timeline obsession and what Stanusch calls Longtermism's displacement of present-day harms.

## Platforms Managing Their Own Change

Two papers turn from imaginaries about AI to the discursive management of AI-inflected platform change itself. [[De2026-ld]] theorises "changecraft" — the strategic vocabulary (creator benevolence, democratization, connectivity) platforms use to render infrastructural shifts aesthetically visible while reframing ideological pivots as mere continuity, thereby masking power consolidation. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] applies a cognate lens to Google's AI Overviews, showing Google's "seamless helper" framing sitting against journalists' cautionary hallucination narratives and SEO marketers' commercial anxiety — with user perspectives and environmental costs conspicuously absent across all three actor groups, echoing the systematic absences [[Stanusch2026-ec]] finds in industry discourse and [[Dodds2026-df]]'s point about hype rendering hidden labor and infrastructure invisible.

## Behind the Curtain: Labor, Practice, and the Limits of Discourse Critique

A final set of papers insists that discourse analysis must be anchored to material practice. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both reframe AI red-teaming as sociotechnical rather than purely technical, tracing its normalization to unexamined value judgments, labor arrangements, and psychological costs borrowed from the history of content moderation; [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns that claims red-teaming will be "automated away" are themselves a discursive move that obscures the ghost workers currently doing the work — a labor-erasure dynamic structurally similar to the absences Vertesi identifies in the "safety decoy." [[Galip2026-ix]] closes the topic from an unexpected angle, applying Ngai's theory of the gimmick to "AI slop" and "brainrot": these stigmatized outputs are framed by industry as effortless and by critics as worthless, yet both framings obscure the outsourced labor and energy costs beneath them, while the genres themselves may harbor a "frictive," resistant potential against the seamless, sanitized imaginaries promoted elsewhere in this corpus (cf. [[De2026-ld]]'s "aesthetic of progress" and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]'s "seamless librarian" metaphor). Read together, this final group suggests that critical AI discourse studies cannot stop at narrative analysis: it must follow the imaginaries down into labor markets, red-team rooms, and the degraded cultural residue of generative systems to see what the industry's polished self-narration is designed not to show.
