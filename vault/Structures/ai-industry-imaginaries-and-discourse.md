# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Arc: From Description to Critique of AI's Discursive Infrastructure

The papers filed here trace a progression from *documenting* how AI is talked about to *interrogating why* those ways of talking exist and whom they serve. At one end sit careful empirical mappings of media and industry language; at the other end sits an argument that such mapping, however sophisticated, risks becoming complicit unless it is anchored to political economy. Read together, the collection stages a methodological and political debate about where critique should locate its object: in texts, in practices, or in capital.

## Mapping Media Imaginaries: Comparative and Computational Approaches

A cluster of papers treats journalism as the primary site where AI's sociotechnical imaginary is negotiated. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs, while present, are neither dominant nor uniform—variation tracks editorial practice more than national culture, and anthropomorphism can serve critique as easily as hype. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends the comparative lens across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, operationalizing Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears typology to show that utopian-dystopian oscillation is itself patterned by national political-economic stakes. [[Mota2026-sl59]] sharpens this into an explicit Global North/South axis: Northern coverage is risk-saturated and pessimistic, Southern coverage developmental and optimistic, with US–China rivalry functioning as the discursive fulcrum around which both "weapon" and "wonder" framings turn. Together these three works establish that AI media discourse is not monolithic hype but a differentiated field whose fault lines mirror existing geopolitical and infrastructural asymmetries—setting up the more pointed question of *who* populates and drives that field.

[[Richter2026-bt]] answers this empirically at the level of stakeholders rather than texts, showing that the "industry dominates AI discourse" thesis oversimplifies a more divergent story: German Twitter discourse institutionalized toward academic/advocacy/governmental actors, while US discourse individualized toward tech-influencer voices even as industry retained an outsized baseline presence. This finding complicates the media-framing papers by suggesting the composition of speakers, not just the content of frames, is a variable requiring comparative, longitudinal analysis—and that early-2010s stakeholder configurations pre-figured later regulatory divergence.

[[Dodds2026-df]] provides the connective theoretical move for this cluster, reframing "AI hype" not as a discourse or bubble but as a *systemic infrastructure*—embedded in newsroom sourcing patterns, professional anxiety, and resource allocation—that journalists simultaneously produce and police. Its call for "breakdown and repair" rather than mere debunking anticipates the collection's later insistence (via [[Vertesi2026-lv]]) that critique itself can be absorbed by the structures it targets.

## Industry Self-Narration: Imaginaries as Strategic Practice

A second cluster shifts attention from media coverage to the discourse industry actors *produce themselves*, treating imaginaries as tools of legitimation rather than incidental narrative. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers the sharpest case study: controversy mapping of the Altman firing/rehiring reveals Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography as dominant industry-aligned imaginaries, deployed through twin strategies of "premediation" (deferring critique into speculative futures) and "preclusion" (positioning the industry as the only competent arbiter of its own risks). Crucially, the paper shows this monopolization is incomplete—counter-imaginaries of Governing Urgency and Intersectionality persist on X/Twitter—suggesting imaginaries are contested terrain rather than settled ideology.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates the picture geographically and figurationally, arguing that critical AI scholarship has fixated on the "loud futuring" of CEOs while neglecting a "quiet futuring" among Bay Area labs, pioneer communities, and EA/Rationalist networks that supplies the very ideas loud futuring later amplifies (AGI arrival, job loss, US–China competition). This paper effectively supplies the *genealogy* behind Stanusch's Longtermism imaginary, tracing it to Effective Altruism's TESCREAL lineage and showing how apolitical self-presentation coexists with deeply political effects.

[[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] show the same discursive logic operating in platform corporate communications rather than lab culture. Meta's Newsroom externalizes "problematic content" onto inauthentic outside actors while presenting itself as capable technological solver—exploiting floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" that later diffuse into regulatory language itself. Google's framing of AI Overviews follows a structurally similar move: seamless helpfulness discourse paired with strategic "experimental" hedging, positioning Google as both commercial platform and public epistemic infrastructure, while user, environmental, and democratic concerns are conspicuously absent from all three actor groups studied (Google, journalists, SEO marketers). Both papers demonstrate a recurring discursive grammar across the industry: locate the problem outside the platform, and locate the solution inside it.

## Discourse as Cover: Labor, Practice, and the Limits of Narrative Critique

A third strand asks what industry and media discourse renders *invisible*. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine red-teaming, showing that its rapid normalization as "the" safety practice obscures the labor arrangements, psychological toll, and value judgments embedded within it—echoing the earlier history of content moderation. Where [[Unknown2025-qj]] explores red-teaming's public-interest potential through interviews with practitioners at public events, [[Gillespie2026-aa]] warns that automation narratives around red-teaming distract from urgent questions about who currently performs this work and under what conditions. These papers extend the collection's concern with discourse into the register of *practice*: imaginaries of "safety" and "alignment" are not just narrative constructs but organize real labor hierarchies that discourse analysis alone cannot fully surface.

[[Galip2026-ix]] pushes this concern with obscured labor and value into aesthetic theory, reading "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's Theory of the Gimmick as forms that work simultaneously "too little" (deskilling, labor-saving) and "too hard" (strained algorithmic bids for attention). Its provocation—that these degraded genres might harbor "soft resistance" against platform frictionlessness—offers a rare counter-note in the collection, suggesting that even the discursive detritus of AI industry production can carry unintended critical charge.

## The Meta-Critique: Decoys and the Political Economy Beneath the Discourse

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] functions as the collection's theoretical keystone, arguing that AI is best understood as a "Project"—a world-building assemblage of capital, infrastructure, and network power—and that most existing critique, including much of the discourse-analytic work surveyed above, functions as a "decoy." Its five decoys (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory) map with striking precision onto themes explored empirically elsewhere in this topic: the inevitability decoy names exactly what [[Hepp2026-oi]]'s Longtermism and [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s premediation strategy accomplish; the safety decoy diagnoses the very red-teaming apparatus studied by [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]]; the regulatory decoy reframes Meta's and Google's self-presentation as public-interested actors ([[Hurcombe2025-cs]], [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]) as strategic courting of regulation that entrenches incumbents rather than constrains them.

This positions [[Vertesi2026-lv]] not merely as another case study but as a reflexive challenge to the entire topic: it asks whether meticulous discourse mapping—however revealing of framing asymmetries, sourcing patterns, or national divergence—risks becoming enrolled in the Project of AI it seeks to hold accountable, unless analysis is explicitly redirected toward financialization and network power. Read as the collection's closing argument, it reframes the preceding twelve papers as valuable empirical diagnostics of *symptoms*—hype, imaginaries, framings, obscured labor—while insisting that accountability research must ultimately follow the money and infrastructure, not just the metaphors.
