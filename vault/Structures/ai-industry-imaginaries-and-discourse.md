# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## The Discursive Construction of AI Power

The papers gathered here converge on a shared premise: AI's meaning and legitimacy are not given by the technology itself but actively manufactured through narrative work performed by industry actors, journalists, and platforms. Rather than treating "hype," "imaginaries," or "framings" as epiphenomenal noise around AI development, this cluster of scholarship insists these discursive formations are constitutive of AI's political economy — they mobilize capital, redistribute legitimacy, and determine which harms become visible and which recede from view.

### Naming the Mechanism: Imaginaries, Hype, and Decoys

Several papers offer conceptual vocabularies for capturing how industry narrative-work operates. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] identifies three imaginaries — Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography — mobilized by the AI industry during the Sam Altman controversy, and names two strategies, "premediation" and "preclusion," through which industry actors externalize critique into speculative futures or internalize it by positioning themselves as the only competent stewards of AI's risks. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this logic into a political-economy register, arguing that AI development is a "Project" of capital accumulation and that ostensibly critical engagements — with ontology, inevitability, disruption, safety, or regulation — function as "decoys" that redirect accountability efforts away from the underlying assemblage of financiers, infrastructure, and network power. [[Dodds2026-df]] reframes hype itself not as discourse but as infrastructure: a systemic force embedded in journalistic communities of practice that redistributes resources and legitimacy before AI's promises ever materialize. Together these three papers form the conceptual spine of the topic, each insisting that critique which stays at the level of surface narrative risks being absorbed back into the very power structures it seeks to expose.

[[Hepp2026-oi]] complicates this picture geographically and sociologically, distinguishing the "loud futuring" of CEOs and investors from a "quiet futuring" enacted in Bay Area labs, start-up houses, and pioneer communities (Effective Altruism, Rationalism). This quiet futuring supplies the ideational reservoir — AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race — that loud futuring later amplifies, while presenting itself, disingenuously, as apolitical. This adds a crucial figurational layer to the imaginaries literature: industry narrative is not simply top-down messaging but a circulatory system between elite spokespeople and dispersed communities of belief.

### Media as Co-Producer, Not Mere Conduit

A second cluster examines how news media absorb, refract, or resist industry narratives. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] finds that mentalistic-agentic framing of LLMs, often assumed to simply reproduce corporate hype, is in fact unevenly distributed and serves multiple rhetorical functions, including critique — a finding that cautions against treating anthropomorphism as inherently complicit. [[Wang2025-zy]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] both operationalize comparative frameworks (Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears; Global North/South framing) to show that national and geopolitical context systematically shapes whether AI is narrated as existential threat or developmental opportunity, with Northern outlets skewing pessimistic and Southern outlets skewing optimistic — a divergence that itself encodes geopolitical asymmetries in infrastructural power. [[Richter2026-bt]] extends this comparative lens temporally, showing that the US Twitter discourse became progressively individualized around tech influencers while German discourse institutionalized around academic and advocacy actors — a divergence the authors argue pre-structured each country's later posture toward GenAI regulation. Read together with [[Dodds2026-df]]'s account of journalists as simultaneously "hype makers" and "hype watchers," these studies suggest that media are neither innocent bystanders nor simple mouthpieces, but structurally implicated co-producers of AI's sociotechnical imaginary, whose variance is driven as much by national-institutional context and editorial practice as by any monolithic "tech industry line."

### Platforms as Narrators of Their Own Legitimacy

A related strand turns to corporate self-narration. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects how Meta's Newsroom uses floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" to externalize the origins of platform harm while presenting itself as the sole capable remediator — a discursive move that has since diffused into regulatory language itself, illustrating how industry framing can colonize the very policy apparatus meant to constrain it. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] finds a structurally similar pattern in Google's framing of AI Overviews: seamless, benevolent infrastructure rhetoric from Google itself, set against more critical journalistic framing and anxious commercial framing from SEO marketers, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent across all three. Both papers demonstrate the same evasive maneuver identified more abstractly by [[Vertesi2026-lv]] and [[Stanusch2026-ec]]: platforms position themselves simultaneously as not-the-problem and as uniquely qualified to solve it, deploying ambiguous terminology to expand or contract their own accountability at will.

### Hidden Labor and the Underside of the Imaginary

A final strand asks what industry narrative obscures materially. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] and [[Unknown2025-qj]] both examine red-teaming as a site where sociotechnical value judgments, labor precarity, and psychological harm are hidden behind a veneer of technical rigor, echoing the earlier trajectory of content moderation labor. [[Baym2026-tr]] widens the lens to the political economy of the "broligarchy," documenting how a decade of platform critique has coincided with, rather than checked, extraordinary wealth concentration — a sobering empirical counterpoint to the discursive analyses elsewhere in this set, suggesting that naming decoys and imaginaries is necessary but insufficient for accountability. [[Galip2026-ix]] closes the loop from a different angle, reading the degraded aesthetic categories of "AI slop" and "brainrot" through Ngai's theory of the gimmick, showing how even the industry's own overproduction and cultural detritus can be read as symptomatic of the contradictions — labor-saving yet effortful, degenerative yet strangely resistant — that the more directly political-economic papers in this set diagnose at the level of infrastructure and capital.

### Synthesis

Across these fourteen papers, a coherent arc emerges: from identifying discrete imaginaries and hype mechanisms ([[Stanusch2026-ec]], [[Dodds2026-df]]), to tracing their circulation and contestation across media and platforms ([[Nguyen2026-vm]], [[Wang2025-zy]], [[Mota2026-sl59]], [[Richter2026-bt]], [[Hurcombe2025-cs]], [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]), to locating their communal wellsprings ([[Hepp2026-oi]]), and finally to insisting that discourse analysis alone cannot substitute for confronting the material substrate of labor, capital, and infrastructure that these narratives work to conceal ([[Vertesi2026-lv]], [[Gillespie2026-aa]], [[Unknown2025-qj]], [[Baym2026-tr]], [[Galip2026-ix]]). The through-line is a methodological and political warning: critical AI scholarship must be reflexive about its own susceptibility to being absorbed as another "decoy," lest analysis of industry narrative become merely another node in the very apparatus of legitimation it seeks to unsettle.
