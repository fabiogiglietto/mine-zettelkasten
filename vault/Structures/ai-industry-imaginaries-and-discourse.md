# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media Framings of AI: Hype, Imaginaries, and National Divergence

A first cluster of papers interrogates how journalism itself becomes a site where AI's meaning is negotiated, rather than a neutral conduit reporting on it. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows empirically that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs — attributing thought or feeling to chatbots — are real but far from dominant, varying more by outlet culture than by region, and serving purposes from uncritical hype amplification to adversarial critique ("bullshitters"). [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across the UK, US, China, and India, mapping utopian/dystopian tropes via Cave and Dihal's framework and showing that national imaginaries of generative AI are inflected by distinct political-economic stakes. [[Mota2026-sl59]] complements this with a Global North/South comparison, finding a stark asymmetry: Northern outlets skew pessimistic and risk-oriented (fear, existential framing) while Southern outlets frame AI through developmental optimism — a divide that reproduces broader geopolitical asymmetries around technological dependency and the "weapon vs. wonder" duality. Together these three papers establish that AI media discourse is neither monolithic nor simply "hype," but a structured field of variation shaped by editorial culture, national positioning, and geopolitical stakes.

[[Dodds2026-df]] provides the conceptual scaffolding that ties this cluster together, reframing "AI hype" not as a discursive bubble but as systemic infrastructure: a set of practices (sourcing patterns favoring tech executives, professional anxiety, temporal narratives of inevitability) that redistributes legitimacy and resources before technologies materialize, while obscuring hidden labor and environmental costs. This systemic view is empirically grounded by [[Richter2026-bt]], whose longitudinal Twitter analysis shows that the composition of hype-driving stakeholders is historically contingent rather than fixed — Germany institutionalized toward academic/advocacy actors while the US discourse individualized around tech influencers — suggesting that today's industry-dominated AI discourse is a path-dependent outcome of choices made across the 2012–2021 formative decade, not an inevitable structural feature of AI discourse as such.

## Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Quiet Futuring, and Corporate Discourse Management

A second, closely linked set of papers turns to how industry actors actively construct self-legitimizing narratives, rather than simply being covered by media. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] is the pivotal text here, identifying the imaginaries of Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography that saturated industry-aligned discourse during the Sam Altman controversy, and theorizing the twin strategies of *premediation* (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and *preclusion* (positioning the industry as sole legitimate problem-solver) — mechanisms that recur, in different guises, across nearly every paper in this section. [[Hepp2026-oi]] complements this by showing that Silicon Valley's imaginative work is not reducible to the "loud futuring" of CEOs; a "quiet futuring" among labs, Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities, and pioneer figurations supplies the ideational reservoir (AGI arrival, job loss, geopolitical race) that loud futuring later amplifies — while presenting itself, contradictorily, as apolitical.

Corporate discourse management at the level of platform communications is examined by [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]]. Hurcombe's analysis of Meta's Newsroom shows how "floating signifiers" like *coordinated inauthentic behaviour* externalize responsibility for platform harms onto outside bad actors while positioning Meta as capable solver — discourse that has since diffused into regulatory language itself. Weinbrand's study of Google's AI Overviews finds an analogous pattern of hedged, seamless self-presentation ("superfast librarian") set against more critical journalistic and anxious SEO-marketer framings, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent from all three discourses. Both papers demonstrate a recurring industry playbook: appropriate ambiguous terminology, position the corporation as trustworthy infrastructure, and displace critique toward externalities — a corporate-communications counterpart to the premediation/preclusion dynamic Stanusch identifies at the level of industry-wide imaginaries.

## Political Economy Beneath the Narrative

A third cluster insists that discourse analysis alone is insufficient without attending to the material and infrastructural power the narratives serve to obscure. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] offers the most systematic articulation, arguing that AI is best understood as a "Project" of world-building by capitalized actors, and cataloguing five "decoys" — ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory — through which even well-intentioned critique becomes co-opted into legitimizing the underlying political economy. This argument directly reframes several other papers' findings as decoy-work: Stanusch's Longtermism imaginary exemplifies the safety/inevitability decoys, while Hurcombe's and Weinbrand's corporate framings illustrate the regulatory and disruption decoys operationalized at platform scale. [[Helmond2026-ll]] extends the infrastructural argument temporally, arguing that "platformization" is entering an AI-centered phase in which Big Tech (and new entrants like Nvidia, Anduril, Palantir) repositions around "Big AI," with material consequences (space-based compute, military and energy sector entanglement) that outstrip any single narrative frame. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] grounds this political economy in labor, showing that red-teaming — often narrated as a technical safety practice — is actually a sociotechnical system with hidden labor arrangements and psychological costs, echoing the earlier ghost-work history of content moderation; the essay is thus a case study in exactly the kind of decoy Vertesi warns against ("safety" language obscuring exploitative labor). [[Baym2026-tr]] closes this section with a retrospective register, arguing that despite a decade of critical scholarship the material conditions of platform power (wealth concentration, data extraction, precarious labor) have only worsened, and calling — much like Vertesi and Gillespie — for research that moves beyond diagnosing discourse toward interdisciplinary intervention in structures of power.

## Aesthetic and Affective Registers: Slop, Gimmick, and the Limits of "Intelligence"

A final, more theoretically experimental cluster examines how AI's cultural and cognitive claims are contested from below, through aesthetic and semiotic critique rather than industry- or journalism-focused framing analysis. [[Galip2026-ix]] and [[Topinka2026-hb]] both take up "AI slop" as a genre, but push in complementary directions: Galip, via Ngai's theory of the gimmick, reads slop as a form that works simultaneously "too little" and "too hard," exposing contradictions of cultural production under "too late capitalism" while retaining unexpected critical/frictional potential; Topinka's concept of "slopaganda" argues that AI political content need not deceive to be effective — it works as an affective lure attuned to platform moods, with AI alignment (RLHF) itself functioning as an "ideological attunement machine" that intensifies existing racial and political formations rather than disrupting them. Both papers implicitly counter the industry narratives examined earlier: where Stanusch's Techno-Hagiography and Hepp's quiet futuring imagine AI as approaching godlike capability, slop/brainrot scholarship reveals the degraded, recursive, and often exploitative underside of the same generative systems (including Model Autophagy Disorder and Global South RLHF labor).

[[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] provides a foundational conceptual corrective that arguably underwrites this entire cluster of critiques: it challenges the premise, common to hagiographic and hype-laden discourse alike, that systems like ChatGPT approximate human cognition, arguing instead that their apparent "intelligence" is really a communicative effect produced by human interpretive labor on machine outputs. This deflationary account of what LLMs actually do offers a theoretical counterweight to the anthropomorphizing tendencies documented empirically in [[Nguyen2026-vm]] and mythologized in [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s Techno-Hagiography, suggesting that the industry imaginaries surveyed throughout this topic rest on a conceptual sleight of hand — mistaking human meaning-making for machine understanding — that the political-economy and cultural-critique literatures, in their different registers, are all working to expose.
