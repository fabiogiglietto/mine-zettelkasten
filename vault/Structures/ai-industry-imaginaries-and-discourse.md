# Critical Analysis of AI Industry Imaginaries and Discourse

## Media as Co-Producer of AI Imaginaries

A cluster of papers treats journalism not as a neutral conduit but as an active site where AI's sociotechnical imaginaries are forged. [[Nguyen2026-vm]] shows that mentalistic-agentic framings of LLMs are real but far from hegemonic in global news coverage, varying more by newsroom culture than by region—complicating simple "AI hype" narratives by showing anthropomorphism can serve critique as much as promotion. [[Wang2025-zy]] extends this comparative lens across UK, US, Chinese, and Indian newspapers, operationalizing Cave and Dihal's hopes/fears framework to show that utopian/dystopian tropes are nationally inflected rather than universal. [[Mota2026-sl59]] pushes the comparison further into a Global North/South axis, finding risk-laden pessimism in Northern outlets against developmental optimism in the South, with geopolitics (the US–China "AI race") as the connective tissue across both registers—AI-as-weapon and AI-as-wonder. [[Dodds2026-df]] synthesizes this body of journalism scholarship into a theoretical claim: AI hype is not merely discourse but an infrastructural force that redistributes resources and legitimacy, sedimented in journalists' sourcing habits (overwhelmingly tech executives), professional anxieties, and nationally specific geopolitical framings. Together these papers establish that media imaginaries are plural, contested, and structurally patterned—yet collectively still anchored to a recognizable "techno-capitalist master frame," as [[Nguyen2026-vm]] puts it.

## Industry Self-Narration: Hagiography, Hype, and Strategic Framing

A second strand turns from media reception to industry's own discursive production. [[Stanusch2026-ec]] offers perhaps the topic's central case study: the Altman firing/rehiring controversy, where industry-aligned discourse mobilizes Longtermism, Regulatory Ambivalence, and Techno-Hagiography through twin strategies of *premediation* (deflecting critique into speculative futures) and *preclusion* (absorbing critique by positioning the industry as its own best regulator)—though counter-imaginaries on X/Twitter show this monopolization is incomplete. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] documents an analogous discursive apparatus at the platform level, showing how Meta's Newsroom frames "problematic content" through floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" that externalize blame onto bad actors while casting Meta as competent problem-solver—language that has since diffused into regulatory discourse itself. [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] traces a parallel dynamic around Google's AI Overviews, where Google's seamless-helper framing contrasts with journalists' cautionary discourse and SEO marketers' commercial anxiety, with the user's voice and environmental costs conspicuously absent across all three actor positions. These studies converge on a shared mechanism: industry actors do not simply defend their technologies but actively construct the terms of legitimate critique, often by appropriating ambiguous, elastic concepts (authenticity, safety, "experimental" status) that can be stretched or narrowed to suit commercial interest.

## The Political Economy Beneath the Narrative

[[Vertesi2026-lv]] provides the topic's most systematic theorization of *why* these framings matter, arguing that AI is best understood as a "Project"—a world-building enterprise through which capitalized actors assemble power, infrastructure, and capital—and that critiques fixating on ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, or regulatory questions function as "decoys" that inadvertently stabilize the very political economy they seem to challenge. This reframes much of the discourse analyzed elsewhere in the topic: Stanusch's Longtermism and Techno-Hagiography map directly onto Vertesi's safety and inevitability decoys, while Hurcombe's "technological solutions" frame exemplifies how accountability work can be captured by the actors it targets. [[Tornberg2026-im]] complements this with a structural account of what the political economy is becoming, theorizing a shift from "platform society" to "AI society" organized around cognitive lock-in, generative epistemics, and alignment politics—a vocabulary for naming the enclosure that imaginary-work like hagiography and hype help legitimize. [[Richter2026-bt]]'s longitudinal Twitter analysis supplies empirical ballast for this political-economic reading, showing that US discourse drifted toward concentrated industry and individual-influencer dominance while German discourse institutionalized across academic, advocacy, and governmental actors—demonstrating that the "who" of AI discourse has durable, nationally divergent consequences for subsequent regulation.

## Quiet Futuring, Communities, and the Making of Imaginaries

Where the above work often focuses on "loud" public-facing discourse, [[Hepp2026-oi]] excavates the less visible social infrastructure that supplies it. The Bay Area's "quiet futuring"—Effective Altruist and Rationalist communities, pioneer labs, start-up houses—generates the long-termist and AGI-timeline vocabulary that loud futuring (CEOs, investors) later amplifies, while presenting itself as apolitical despite being constitutively political. This is a crucial complement to [[Stanusch2026-ec]]: Longtermism is not simply an industry PR strategy but has deep roots in actual communities whose ideas percolate upward into hagiographic and inevitability framings. It also nuances [[Vertesi2026-lv]]'s decoy framework by showing that the ideational reservoir behind these decoys has its own social geography and figurational history, not merely strategic intent.

## Labor, Infrastructure, and What Gets Obscured

Several papers trace what industry imaginaries systematically render invisible. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shows how AI red-teaming, framed publicly as rigorous technical safety practice, actually replicates the outsourced, precarious, psychologically costly labor arrangements of earlier content moderation—a sociotechnical reality obscured by safety rhetoric. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] similarly identifies the absence of platform affordances and hyperpartisan amplifiers from Meta's own account of "problematic content." [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] notes the erasure of environmental costs and user perspectives from AI Overview discourse. [[Baym2026-tr]] widens the lens to the broader political economy of platforms and generative AI, arguing that wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, precarious creative labor, and data extraction have intensified rather than abated, with user-generated content now feeding generative AI training in ways that compound older harms—an argument that functions as an empirical bridge between discourse critique and material consequence, and that explicitly calls for scholarship to move past critique toward collaborative intervention.

## Competing Vocabularies and Temporal Framing

Finally, two papers interrogate the deeper conceptual and historiographical categories through which AI discourse is organized. [[Slack1783075716-sl1c]] challenges the "artificial intelligence" framing itself, arguing that systems like ChatGPT succeed through human interpretive labor rather than machine understanding—a conceptual corrective that undercuts both techno-hagiographic and doom-laden imaginaries by relocating "intelligence" in the human-machine relation rather than the machine alone. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] examines the mirror-image narrative: "internet decline narratives" (enshittification, techlash, dead internet theory) that organize public and academic understanding of digital history around a negative trajectory culminating, increasingly, in the generative-AI boom. Hegarty's critique that such narratives risk "corporational determinism" and erase user agency offers a cautionary note for this whole topic area: critical analysis of AI industry discourse must itself avoid collapsing into a totalizing counter-narrative that, like the hype it critiques, centers a handful of US firms and forecloses plural, situated accounts of how AI is lived, resisted, and reimagined elsewhere.
