# Platform Data Governance & Research Access

## The arc of data access: from Wild West to walled garden

The foundational narrative running through this topic is one of enclosure. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the hinge moment — Facebook's 2018 Pages API shutdown — as the start of a "post-API age" in which platform-specific research infrastructure can vanish overnight; [[Bruns2019-nr]] extends this into the "APIcalypse," arguing that post-Cambridge-Analytica restrictions functioned less as privacy protection than as a means of frustrating critical, public-interest scholarship, with Social Science One offered as a PR-friendly but intransparent substitute. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes the whole two-decade history (prehistory, laissez-faire, authentication, limited options, academic cooperation) across six platforms, showing that the platforms under the most public scrutiny (Meta, X) now impose the tightest controls. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and Bastos2025-ya narrate the Twitter/X case specifically — from "Wild West" openness through a brief "Golden Age" Academic API to Musk's commercial paywall — while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the human cost: a 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship through 2022 followed by stagnation and a 13% decline in 2024. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review corroborates this at the level of the whole social-science literature, showing the long rise and recent plateau/decline of social-media-based empirical research concentrated on Twitter/X and Facebook, alongside a structural mismatch between platform popularity and research usage. van-Dijck2018-up and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supply the deeper theoretical scaffolding here, showing how the rhetoric of "platform" neutrality and the broader "platform society" naturalize private control over what is, in effect, public infrastructure — a framing that later DSA-era papers draw on directly.

## Article 40 and the DSA as regulatory intervention

Against this backdrop, Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act is treated across many papers as a genuine — if fragile — rupture in the pattern of voluntary, revocable access. [[Ohme2026-nv]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] frame the current turbulence (platform resistance, procedural friction) not as regulatory failure but as evidence the law is finally constraining entrenched platform power, urging researchers to "stand firm" through implementation rather than abandon the framework. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Philipp2026-tl]] detail the practical landscape this creates for election and systemic-risk research, while [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] documents the European Commission's formal proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for breaching Article 40.12. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Annabell2026-ev]] push into the conceptual substrate of the regulation itself, showing that "data quality" and even "data" are contested, politically negotiated terms — floating signifiers whose narrow, platform-favored definitions (aggregated, anonymized, pre-structured datasets) are being actively fought over in Delegated Act consultations, with Meta notably inverting researchers' own arguments to claim low data quality makes access futile. [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA comparatively, arguing that EU "flow optimisation," German/Austrian "patrolling," and US "free circulation" regimes all converge on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" — transparency and auditing as distributed governance rather than direct content control.

## Auditing the infrastructure: what the new APIs and portals actually deliver

A cluster of empirical audits asks whether DSA-mandated access mechanisms actually work. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within 20–60 days and rankings inconsistent across identical repeated queries — a serious problem for any DSA systemic-risk research that relies on retrospective retrieval. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content moderation transparency during the 2024 EU elections, documenting inconsistent application of publisher-context labels and vague removal statements, and calls for reconceiving transparency as "observability." [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] turn to Meta specifically: CrowdTangle's shutdown and its replacement by the Meta Content Library's "clean room" model are shown to impose severe technical and skill barriers (Jupyter-only analysis, mandatory data deletion, no cross-platform work), systematically disadvantaging qualitative researchers, smaller institutions, and the Majority World. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds methodologically, building TubeStats and TokStats as independent, randomly-sampled infrastructure to escape both API gatekeeping and popularity-biased sampling. [[Lukito2026-nb]] synthesizes all of this into a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent and access infrastructure overwhelmingly WEIRD-centric. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys the resulting landscape of APIs and tools in the DSA era more generally.

## Consequences for visibility: political content reduction, the 100-share threshold, and algorithmic governance

A second strand uses exactly these access channels to demonstrate that platform policy decisions — often undisclosed — materially reshape what the public and researchers can see. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], drawing on the Meta Content Library, finds Meta's political content reduction policy cut Italian MPs' average reach by 72%, beginning roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout and never fully reversing after the January 2025 policy change — with extremist accounts compensating through sharply increased posting volume, a transparency failure Meta never disclosed in its DSA compliance reports. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] shows, using the Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset, that share-to-view amplification on Facebook fluctuates in lockstep with known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), demonstrating active curation rather than neutral conduit behavior. Earlier work interrogates the datasets themselves: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both show that the 100-public-shares censoring threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset is not a benign anonymization choice but a systematic distortion — inflating apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to representative panels, and producing near-identical cross-country trajectories that likely reflect feed-ranking changes rather than local events. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends the URL Shares approach to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares without proportional rises in views — again pointing to platform-level dampening — while [[Holt2026-zq]] examines what users flag as "False News" in the same dataset, finding user reports track political disagreement more than falsity, a finding made newly urgent by Meta's shift toward Community Notes. [[Allcott2019-gn]] offers an earlier data point in this same lineage, tracking the post-2016 divergence between Facebook and Twitter misinformation engagement. [[McNally2025-dn]] complements these dataset-level findings with direct algorithm auditing, showing Facebook's News Feed changes produce detectable, lagged effects on hard-news engagement — explicitly framed as a case for DSA Article 40(4) systemic audits. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] supplies the conceptual vocabulary tying all of this together: "reduction" (algorithmic demotion without removal) is a form of content moderation that remains largely invisible to transparency regimes, a point [[Simeone2025-vo]] illustrates concretely by showing how Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of a small set of accounts produced disproportionate network-wide "ripple effects" on authority structure and discourse.

## Who gets to see, and who is seen: equity, moderation workforce, and global asymmetries

Several papers push the visibility question beyond algorithmic ranking into questions of who is protected and who is excluded altogether. [[Tonneau2025-bv]], using DSA-mandated transparency data, finds stark cross-lingual disparities in human moderator allocation — Global South languages receiving as little as 7.5% of English's per-content moderator allocation on X — while millions of EU users post in languages with no human oversight at all. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] broadens this into a critique of "privatized public discourse," arguing that the harms of platform governance fall disproportionately on the Global South, where weak regulatory infrastructure (illustrated by Brazil's failed PL 2630) leaves states unable to counter platform lobbying. [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] make the same equity argument from the research-access side: data-access infrastructure built for DSA compliance or U.S. election research is not globally portable, reinforcing a WEIRD-centric research base even as the underlying harms are global.

## Industry influence on the research that studies platforms

A distinct but closely related concern is that platform dependency does not just constrain *what* can be studied but *how independently* it can be studied. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical study [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document systematic, largely undisclosed industry ties in high-profile social media research — roughly half of papers in top journals have disclosable Meta/X/Google/Microsoft ties, concentrated in a small recurring set of authors and editors, with industry-tied work skewing toward user-focused topics (misinformation sharing) rather than platform-level dynamics, and receiving disproportionate citation and media attention. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this in terms borrowed from tobacco and pharma research-ethics literature, arguing that platforms' exclusive control of data makes academic independence structurally harder to achieve than in other contested-science domains, and that the 2020 Meta election studies exemplify the risk (undisclosed algorithmic changes mid-study). [[Pierri2026-ib]] names a specific instance: an independent reanalysis found a Meta-researcher collaboration understated how temporary 2020-election algorithm changes inflated Meta's apparent success at curbing misinformation. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a methodological rejoinder from the opposite direction, using Piccardi et al.'s browser-extension/LLM-reranking approach as a model for "platform-independent" experimentation that sidesteps dependency on platform cooperation altogether — a response directly motivated by the same closing API environment chronicled elsewhere in this set.

## Platform governance discourse and the broader moderation landscape

A final cluster examines how platforms narrate and justify their own governance choices, providing context for why data access and visibility policy remain so contested. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows Meta's Newsroom discursively externalizing blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors while eliding its own platform affordances (Facebook Groups) and hyperpartisan verified accounts. [[De2026-ld]] generalizes this into "changecraft," the strategic discursive management of continual platform change across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] traces the resulting election-policy trajectory across five platforms — from narrow 2018 protections through 2020–22 "civic integrity" expansion to post-2022 rollback — a backlash chronicled concretely in [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]]'s account of Meta's abandonment of third-party fact-checking (where removal is in fact rare, contradicting Zuckerberg's "censorship" framing) and in [[Rogers2026-zz]]'s longitudinal study of borderline content's persistently right-leaning character despite Meta's "content-agnostic" moderation rhetoric. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows how Meta's narrowing definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" (from "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors") leaves much manufactured attention — gambling networks, hijacked groups, political activists — on-platform. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents fact-checkers' own rhetorical defense of platform funding dependency, while [[Moran2025-qn]] surveys the broader professional crisis in trust-and-safety work amid political backlash and layoffs. [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] applies similar discourse analysis to climate misinformation specifically, finding platforms substitute performative "soft" interventions and responsibility-shifting rhetoric for enforceable rules. Several papers extend this landscape into adjacent technical and regulatory territory — [[Ahuja2025-ku]] on dark patterns and DSA Article 25, [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] on inadvertent political profiling in X's recommender embeddings, [[Votta2025-xz]] on ad-delivery algorithms in political campaigns, [[Vincent_undated-re]] on cross-platform misinformation prevalence measurement, and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] on deepfake-enabled fraud as a governance failure of platform gatekeepers — all reinforcing the throughline that platform self-governance requires external, data-grounded scrutiny.

## Looking ahead

Two forward-looking pieces reframe the entire debate. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues political communication research should move beyond individual causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior itself as democratic infrastructure, a reframing the DSA's systemic-risk language implicitly endorses. [[Tornberg2026-im]] goes further, arguing that generative AI is already driving a shift from the "platform society" to an "AI society," in which data-as-a-service becomes research-as-a-service and the EU's AI Act — unlike the DSA — offers no equivalent researcher-access regime, leaving the next infrastructural enclosure largely unaddressed by the very framework this literature has fought to build. [[Miller2026-cp]]'s account of X's AI Note Writer API for Community Notes is an early, concrete instance of exactly this next frontier — governance questions about AI-mediated content assessment arriving before any Article-40-equivalent transparency mandate exists to audit them.
