# Platform Data Governance & Access

## The infrastructure crisis: from API openness to enclosure

The corpus opens with a shared historical diagnosis: platform data access for researchers has moved through discrete eras, from an early "Wild West" of loosely governed APIs, through a "Golden Age" of academic-oriented access, into a present of enclosure and paywalling. [[Freelon2018-ao]] first names this rupture as the "post-API age," triggered by Facebook's 2018 Pages API shutdown, and [[Freelon2024-sc]] extends the periodization across six platforms, showing how each regime — laissez-faire, academic API, walled garden, pay-to-play — reflects platform-specific scandal histories and commercial calculations rather than principled data policy. [[Bruns2019-nr]] supplies the polemical version of this story, terming the Cambridge-Analytica-triggered restrictions the "APIcalypse" and arguing that platforms exploited privacy concerns to frustrate accountability research, a claim echoed structurally (if less polemically) by [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], which traces Twitter/X specifically through Wild West, Golden Age, and walled-garden phases. Bastos2025-ya offers a retrospective eulogy for Twitter as open research infrastructure, and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the resulting damage bibliometrically: a 25% annual growth in Twitter-based scholarship collapsing into a 13% decline by 2024 once Enterprise API pricing hit $42,000/month. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this pattern across disciplines, showing that social-media-based empirical research tripled between 2010 and the early 2020s before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024 — direct evidence that infrastructural politics reshape the very possibility of a research field, not just its convenience.

This enclosure is not unique to Twitter/X. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes the pattern across 72 access tools using a "price, proficiency, permission" framework, showing that consumption-side (demand) data is chronically scarce relative to production-side (supply) data, and that platform-permission-based access is inherently ephemeral — "independence by permission." Meta's own trajectory (Social Science One → CrowdTangle → FORT → Meta Content Library) is repeatedly cited as the paradigm case of this instability, most vividly in [[Cullen2026-cb]], whose "Seeing like an API" framework shows how CrowdTangle's technical design and governance rules actively constrained what researchers could see and conclude, and in [[Bruns2026-pn]], which documents how the Meta Content Library's "clean room" successor imposes web-disconnected, code-only, privacy-first constraints that systematically exclude qualitative, cross-platform, and under-resourced researchers. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to this same closure by building independent infrastructure (TubeStats/TokStats) for random sampling of YouTube and TikTok, addressing the "denominator problem" that opportunistic, popularity-biased samples create.

## The DSA as regulatory answer — and its friction

A second cluster treats the EU Digital Services Act, particularly Article 40, as the emergent institutional response to this enclosure. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] converge on cautious optimism: DSA-mandated access is a genuine legal shift from platform philanthropy to binding right, but implementation remains uneven, slow, and contested. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] explicitly reframes platform resistance — legal smokescreens, narrow interpretations, delays — not as evidence of regulatory failure but as proof the law is finally constraining real power asymmetries; [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] documents this resistance empirically (response times from 2 to 100+ days, boilerplate denials, EU findings that TikTok and Meta breached Article 40.12). [[Peters2026-mo]] adds a granular case study of how "data quality" itself became a contested, politically negotiated concept during the Delegated Regulation consultation, with Meta strategically inverting researchers' arguments to claim access would be futile. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the DSA era demands not just new access but a new conceptual object — collective/infrastructural platform behavior rather than individual causal effects — while [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a broader comparative legal analysis of EU/US content-moderation regimes as forms of "security bureaucratisation."

Empirical audits of the DSA's actual research tools reveal how much remains unresolved. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]] both find that ostensibly DSA-compliant APIs (TikTok's Research API, YouTube's Data API) are riddled with inconsistencies, temporal decay, and non-replicability, undermining their use for systemic-risk research; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] extends this critique to moderation labor itself, showing DSA transparency reports expose stark language-based disparities in human moderator allocation, disadvantaging Global South languages. [[Jurg2025-ur]] similarly audits YouTube's electoral content moderation and finds inconsistent application of authoritative-source labels and opaque removal communication, calling for expanded API access. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] both treat the practical, infrastructural bottlenecks of multi-platform research as a first-order problem requiring standardization, not merely more studies. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] extends DSA-adjacent regulatory analysis to Article 25's dark-pattern provisions, showing how HCI taxonomies can operationalize legal categories for enforcement (e.g., X's paid verification checkmarks).

## Structural critique: who controls the evidence base?

A distinct but related thread interrogates the political economy of platform-dependent knowledge production itself. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] form a tight sequence arguing that platforms' monopoly on data creates conditions for undisclosed industry influence structurally analogous to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]]'s empirical audit is the most damning: roughly half of high-profile social media papers in Science/Nature/PNAS have disclosable industry ties, mostly undisclosed, concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, yielding an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" of the field once editors and reviewers are included. These papers directly implicate the 2020 Facebook/Instagram Election Study collaborations as exemplars of "performative" or design-biased partnership, a critique [[Allen2025-ot]] complicates by situating Piccardi et al.'s platform-independent browser-extension methodology as a promising alternative that sidesteps platform gatekeeping altogether — precisely the kind of "research on research" reform [[Heiss2026-qv]] calls for.

## Auditing the Meta URL Shares Dataset: a case study in data-quality politics

A self-contained methodological sub-literature interrogates one specific DSA-adjacent artifact — Meta's URL Shares Dataset — as a microcosm of how platform-mediated data engineering choices distort findings. [[Allen2021-ai]] shows the 100-public-share censoring threshold, not differential-privacy noise, causes roughly 2x/4x overestimation of news/fake-news prevalence relative to a representative Nielsen panel. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] extends this critique temporally, showing suspiciously synchronized cross-country trends in the dataset that likely reflect undisclosed Feed-ranking changes (Meaningful Social Interactions optimization) rather than genuine social signal — a warning to any longitudinal or comparative user of the dataset, including [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]], whose European extension of [[Guess2021-ym]]'s original US analysis explicitly grapples with these known biases while documenting rising untrustworthy-URL shares in Germany and Italy around elections. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] and [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] push this dataset-critical tradition toward its most consequential finding: using the newer Meta Content Library, they show algorithmic governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures, and the 2021–2025 political content reduction policy) are not stable background conditions but actively fluctuating forces that dampen partisan and political reach — with [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] documenting a 72% reach collapse for Italian MPs that began ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, and asymmetric recovery favoring extremist accounts, which is framed explicitly as a DSA transparency failure. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] complicates the harms narrative from another angle, finding concentrated but low-average exposure to untrustworthy sources with no measurable attitudinal effects — a null result that sits awkwardly against alarm-toned literature elsewhere in this topic.

## Content moderation, reduction, and platform self-narration

Running parallel to the access-and-data-quality debates is a literature on moderation policy itself, particularly the shift from removal to subtler forms of governance. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] provides the conceptual anchor: "reduction" (algorithmic demotion without removal) constitutes an under-scrutinized, largely invisible form of content moderation that concentrates curatorial power precisely by evading the transparency regimes built for removal. This framework illuminates several empirical cases: [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows YouTube's moderation is structurally outpaced by its own recommendation system in sustaining an "ambient" Andrew Tate ecosystem after deplatforming; [[Karo2026-dn]] documents jihadist actors evading TikTok moderation through vernacular camouflage; [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows Meta's narrowing definition of "coordinated inauthentic behavior" — from broad "false amplifiers" to "adversarial threat actors" — has left much manufactured attention (media groups, ad networks, political activists) legally on-platform. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] and [[De2026-ld]] both analyze platforms' self-narration of these moderation shifts: Meta's Newsroom frames problematic content as originating from outside actors while eliding its own affordances (Facebook Groups, hyperpartisan pages), and "changecraft" more broadly describes how Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X discursively naturalize ideological pivots as continuity. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] historicizes election-policy evolution across five platforms into three phases (protection, civic-integrity expansion, rollback), directly bearing on Meta's political content reduction as part of a broader industry-wide "backlash" pattern also diagnosed by [[Moran2025-qn]] (upheaval in the trust-and-safety profession) and [[Donovan2025-ws]] (misinformation-at-scale as structural feature, not bug, of engagement-driven business models).

Community Notes emerges as the flashpoint case for evaluating whether crowdsourced moderation can substitute for professional fact-checking. [[Renault2025-uh]] finds Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under this bridging-algorithm system, undermining claims of anti-conservative bias; [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows the same bridging mechanism structurally *undermoderates* polarizing content by design, creating electoral risks; and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] surveys fact-checkers' own skepticism toward Community Notes as Meta's replacement for professional verification, alongside evidence that Zuckerberg's censorship framing is empirically unfounded. [[Holt2026-zq]] complements this by showing user-flagged "False News" reports skew toward polarized political disagreement rather than genuinely false claims, raising concern about Meta's shift toward user-report-driven moderation. [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines the adjacent question of how fact-checkers rhetorically justify continued platform-funding dependence amid this very backlash.

## Algorithmic power, ideology, and infrastructural politics

A final cluster probes how platform algorithms encode and act on political signals, and situates these dynamics within the larger structural-power literature that frames the whole topic. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] provides striking technical evidence that X's recommender system inadvertently learns a near-perfect linear encoding of users' Left-Right ideology, raising GDPR/DSA-relevant profiling concerns; [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] shows post-Musk algorithmic curation on Twitter rewarded "agitating" content and Elon Musk's own centrality rather than political leaning per se; [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates Facebook's News Feed algorithm is empirically detectable and differentiates hard news from soft content, challenging "black box" framings and supporting DSA Article 40(4)'s audit provisions. [[Votta2025-xz]] extends this to ad-delivery algorithms shaping political campaign reach and cost. Framing works — [[van-Dijck2018-up]], [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] — supply the macro-political stakes: platforms are theorized as privatizers of public discourse whose harms fall disproportionately on the Global South and whose accountability failures are implicated in democratic backsliding, giving normative weight to the empirical and infrastructural battles over access and moderation documented throughout this topic. Adjacent methodological pieces — [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] on Facebook-ad-based survey recruitment, [[Allcott2019-gn]] on cross-platform misinformation diffusion trends, and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] on deepfake-fraud regulation — extend the topic's concerns about platform accountability and evidentiary infrastructure into adjacent empirical and policy domains.
