# Platform Data Governance & Research Access

# Platform Data Governance & Research Access

## The closing and reopening of the data spigot

The organizing tension across this literature is a historical arc: an era of relatively open platform data access gave rise to a distinctive body of empirical social science, which then collapsed under commercial and political pressure, prompting a regulatory response whose adequacy is still being tested. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes this arc most comprehensively, tracing "post-API age" data regimes from laissez-faire APIs through academic cooperation to today's fragmented mix of pay-to-play APIs, "academic walled gardens," and unofficial scraping. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] and Bastos2025-ya narrate the Twitter/X case specifically as a movement from "Wild West" to "walled garden," while [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the consequence bibliometrically: a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 following the $42,000/month Enterprise API pricing. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review corroborates this at the level of the whole social-science literature, showing that the share of papers using social media data tripled and then plateaued/declined in 2022–2024—directly implicating API closures as a shaping force on what social science can know about itself. [[Lukito2026-nb]] frames the resulting landscape as one of "price, proficiency, or permission," cataloguing 72 access tools and showing how each barrier differentially excludes early-career, non-Western, and non-technical researchers, with Meta's own churn through Social Science One, CrowdTangle, FORT, and the Meta Content Library serving as a case study in the instability of platform-led access.

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act—especially Article 40's provisions on vetted-researcher data access—emerges as the central regulatory hope and battleground. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Pierri2025-hm]]/[[Pierri2026-ib]] (companion pieces) all assess what Article 40 changes in practice, converging on a shared diagnosis: the DSA represents a genuine shift from "data philanthropy" or "independence by permission" toward a binding legal right, but implementation is beset by misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, opaque platform responses, and a "circular problem" whereby data access is needed to win grants but grants are needed to sustain data access. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers the most optimistic reframing, arguing that current friction signals the regulation is finally constraining entrenched platform power rather than failing—a "marathon" requiring sustained implementation rather than redesign. [[Peters2026-mo]] complicates this picture by showing that "data quality" itself is a contested concept in the DSA's Delegated Regulation process, mobilized differently by academics (as methodological rigor), platforms (as an excuse for futility, notably Meta), and EU regulators (who largely ignored it until NGO/academic advocacy forced partial inclusion).

## Auditing the new access infrastructures

A cluster of papers empirically stress-tests the concrete tools DSA compliance has produced, generally finding them wanting. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] shows TikTok's expanded Research API remains riddled with unexplained limitations despite DSA-driven changes. [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts a rigorous audit of YouTube's Data API search endpoint, finding it "forgetful by design"—prioritizing freshness over completeness, with findable videos dropping sharply after 20–60 days, undermining retrospective systemic-risk research. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this critique to YouTube's content moderation transparency during EU elections, finding inconsistent application of publisher-context labels and vague removal statements, and calling for "observability" as a richer standard than mere access. [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] focus on Meta's tooling specifically: Bruns dissects the Meta Content Library's "clean room" model, showing it privileges code-literate researchers, forbids cross-platform combination, and mandates data deletion that precludes longitudinal work, while Cullen's "seeing like an API" framework retrospectively analyzes how CrowdTangle's technical design and governance rules shaped what could be known even before its 2024 shutdown. [[Zheng2026-bi]] offers a constructive counterpoint, building TubeStats/TokStats as independent random-sampling infrastructure that sidesteps both platform APIs and their representativeness problems. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA-mandated transparency data itself to reveal a further governance gap: stark disparities in human moderator allocation across languages, with Global South languages starved of oversight relative to English. Collectively these audits suggest that formal compliance with data-access mandates can coexist with—or even mask—substantive opacity, a theme [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] surveys at the level of the whole DSA-era API/tool landscape and [[Schulte2026-df]] frames as a "research infrastructure problem" needing standardization, not just legal mandates.

## Industry influence and the politics of platform-mediated knowledge

A parallel strand interrogates the epistemic dependency researchers have on the very companies they study. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] draw explicit analogies to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel industry science, cataloguing mechanisms—suppressed internal research, selective funding, design bias, performative collaboration—through which platforms shape the evidence base for their own regulation. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers a pointed metascientific critique of the Meta2020 (Facebook/Instagram Election Study) partnership specifically, arguing its null findings reflect low "temporal validity" and underpowered, consensus-driven design as much as genuine absence of effects, and concluding that reactive academic science cannot keep pace with platforms—hence proactive regulation is necessary. [[Allen2025-ot]] situates a methodological response to this problem—Piccardi et al.'s platform-independent, LLM-based reranking experiment—within the same debate, reading its divergence from FIES's null results as evidence that intervention level and platform context matter enormously. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's own public communications (its Newsroom) discursively externalize blame for platform harms onto "inauthentic" outside actors while positioning Meta as capable problem-solver—rhetorical work that, per [[Rogers2025-sl8f]], is enabled by Meta's own narrowing definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" from broad "false amplifiers" to narrow "adversarial threat actors," letting much manufactured attention persist unlabeled. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents fact-checkers' own defensive rhetoric in justifying financial dependence on the same platforms whose content they police, while [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows empirically that Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors is largely unfounded, given how rarely verified content is actually removed.

## Reassessing Meta's flagship datasets and policy changes

Several papers use Meta's semi-official datasets (CrowdTangle, URL Shares Dataset, Meta Content Library) to both generate substantive findings and surface their methodological pitfalls. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies a critical artifact in the URL Shares Dataset's 100-share anonymization threshold, showing platform-wide algorithmic shifts (likely Feed/MSI changes) can masquerade as cross-national social signal. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] use the same or related datasets to show untrustworthy-content exposure is concentrated among older users and, in the latter case, carries no detectable attitudinal effects—consonant with the broader null-effects pattern from Meta-partnered studies. [[noauthor_undated-bm]]'s study of deceptive networks around the 2020 election similarly finds reach highly concentrated and politically-adjusted associations with attitudes largely disappearing. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes back against treating platform governance as a stable backdrop, showing that partisan and quality-based reach penalties/rewards fluctuate sharply in step with known Facebook interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures)—evidence Facebook is an active curator, not neutral conduit. [[McNally2025-dn]] makes the same "not a black box" argument via longitudinal CrowdTangle-based analysis of News Feed algorithm changes affecting Guardian content. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] offers perhaps the sharpest accountability case: using the Meta Content Library itself, it shows Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' reach by 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and was never disclosed in DSA compliance reporting—demonstrating both a transparency deficit and the emerging research value of DSA-enabled infrastructure. [[Holt2026-zq]] uses the URL Shares Dataset to examine what users flag as "false news," relevant to Meta's 2025 shift toward Community Notes, finding user reports track political disagreement more than falsity.

## Political and algorithmic effects, and the wider governance frame

A final cluster examines algorithmic and policy effects with direct bearing on platform accountability debates: [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds X's algorithmic feed causally shifts attitudes rightward and asymmetrically (a persistence mechanism reconciling prior Meta null results); Efstdoi:Efstratiou2025-gs and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] audit algorithmic visibility and profiling on X, the latter showing recommender systems inadvertently encode users' ideology; [[Bouchaud2026-np]] critiques Community Notes' bridging algorithm for structurally undermoderating polarizing electoral content; [[Ventura2026-yc]] examines partisan effects of Brazil's X ban. These sit alongside broader normative and conceptual pieces—[[Gillespie2010-sla2]] on the discursive politics of "platform" as a term that forecloses accountability; [[Bechmann2026-dr]] arguing for collective-behavior rather than individual-effects framings under the DSA; [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] tracing convergence and recent rollback in platforms' election policies; [[Crosset2026-mq]] theorizing DSA-style regulation as "security bureaucratization"; and [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] extending platform-accountability concerns to Global South digital sovereignty. Together with adjacent case studies of moderation failure ([[Karo2026-dn]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[Vincent_undated-re]]) and trust-and-safety institutional erosion ([[Moran2025-qn]], [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Donovan2025-ws]]), this body of work frames the DSA not as a settled solution but as one contested front in an ongoing struggle over who gets to know, and say, what platforms are doing.
