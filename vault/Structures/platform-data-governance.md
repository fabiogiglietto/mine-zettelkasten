# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The arc: from open infrastructure to regulated access

This literature traces a single, contested transition: from an era in which platforms offered (however inconsistently) open or semi-open APIs to researchers, through a traumatic "APIcalypse" of closures and paywalls, to an emergent regime in which the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) attempts to re-establish access as a legal entitlement rather than a corporate favour. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] supply the conceptual groundwork for everything that follows, showing how the word "platform" itself does ideological work—connoting neutrality and openness while masking editorial, economic, and infrastructural power. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the rupture precisely: Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure inaugurated a "post-API age" in which TOS-compliant data extraction simply ceased to exist for many research questions. [[Bruns2019-nr]] extends this into the "APIcalypse," arguing that post-Cambridge-Analytica restrictions were less about privacy than about frustrating critical, public-interest scrutiny—a reading echoed later by [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], whose "Wild West to Walled Garden" periodization of Twitter/X access (open API → Academic API "Golden Age" → Musk-era paywall) generalizes the pattern across Meta, Reddit, and TikTok. [[Freelon2024-sc]] formalizes this into a five-period history and a typology of four official access regimes plus unofficial scraping, while Bastos2025-ya offers an elegiac retrospective on "Twitter studies" as a scholarly subfield built entirely on now-vanished infrastructure, and [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates the broader "death of Twitter" as a story of mismanagement and weaponized disinformation overtaking the platform's prosocial affordances. The scale of the loss is quantified empirically: [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] bibliometrically documents a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications in 2024 following $42,000/month Enterprise pricing, and [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review of 59 journals shows that empirical social-media research—overwhelmingly concentrated on Twitter/X and Facebook—plateaued and declined after 2022 precisely as API restrictions bit, with researchers only beginning to pivot toward TikTok, YouTube, and donation-based methods.

## Auditing the auditors: data quality, thresholds, and hidden bias

A parallel strand interrogates the data platforms *do* provide, showing that even generous-seeming partnerships encode distortions. [[Allen2021-ai]] demonstrates that Meta's Social Science One URL Shares dataset overestimates fake-news consumption roughly 4x because of its 100-public-share censoring threshold, a finding corroborated and extended by [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], who shows that monthly trends across 46 countries move in uncannily similar ways—evidence of a platform-wide algorithmic force (likely "Meaningful Social Interactions" tuning) rather than genuine local dynamics. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] applies the same dataset cross-nationally to Germany, France, and Italy, confirming that methodological artefacts (and US-skewed NewsGuard coverage) complicate any straightforward EU–US comparison. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes this agenda further with the successor Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset, showing that Facebook's share-to-view amplification is neither linear nor stable but fluctuates with governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures—direct evidence that Facebook is an active curator, not a neutral conduit. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] extends the critique of data quality to researcher-side methods, finding that web-scraping environment (not time delay) is the dominant source of distortion in tracking studies of online news consumption. [[Holt2026-zq]] closes the loop by examining what users themselves flag as "false news" in the URL Shares dataset, finding that reports track political disagreement more than falsity—a troubling signal given Meta's pivot toward user-report-driven moderation. A complementary cluster audits platform-provided *research infrastructure* directly: [[Rieder2025-ju]] finds YouTube's Data API search endpoint "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay undermining retrospective research; [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats offer an independent corrective via true random sampling; [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] documents persistent, unexplained limitations in TikTok's DSA-mandated Research API; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency data to reveal systematic underinvestment in moderator coverage for Global South languages; and [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's election-period moderation of authoritative sources, finding inconsistent application of publisher-context labels and opaque removal communications. [[Allcott2019-gn]]'s earlier API-era measurement of Facebook/Twitter misinformation diffusion stands as a baseline this later literature can no longer easily replicate.

## The DSA's Article 40: regulation as re-opening

The DSA's Article 40 researcher-access provision is the field's central regulatory hinge, and a dense cluster of papers tracks its promise and friction in close to real time. [[Ohme2026-nv]] frames Article 40 as a substantive practical change in researcher-platform relations; [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality" itself was a contested, largely absent concern in official DSA text until academic and NGO stakeholders forced partial inclusion into the Delegated Regulation; [[Annabell2026-ev]] goes further, arguing that "data" functions as a "floating signifier" that platforms exploit to narrow researcher access toward aggregated, quantified, pre-structured datasets, marginalizing qualitative and humanities conceptions of evidence. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offer complementary assessments from inside the policy process—Pierri enumerating legal, technical, and resource asymmetries (including the open question of whether LLMs count as covered intermediary services), de Vreese and Tromble arguing that platform resistance should be read as confirmation that the regulation targets real power asymmetries rather than evidence of its failure. [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] survey the resulting landscape of tools empirically: Lukito's "price, proficiency, or permission" framework catalogues 72 access programs and finds consumption-side data (demand, exposure) drastically underrepresented relative to production-side data, with WEIRD-centric eligibility rules compounding global inequity. Several papers apply this regulatory lens to specific domains: [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] to election research and platform election-policy evolution respectively, [[Schulte2026-df]] to a German-election case study arguing that platform observability should be treated as reusable research *infrastructure*, and [[Bechmann2026-dr]] arguing more broadly that DSA-era research should move beyond individual-level causal effects toward conceptualizing platform collective behaviour as democratic infrastructure. [[Crosset2026-mq]] situates the DSA within a comparative security-governance frame, showing that EU "flow optimisation" regulation converges with ostensibly opposite US regimes on a shared logic of "security bureaucratisation" via transparency and auditing mandates.

## Clean rooms and the lived practice of compliance

A more granular set of studies examines what DSA-era and post-CrowdTangle access actually feels like for researchers. [[Cullen2026-cb]] develops a "seeing like an API" framework from interviews with 20 CrowdTangle users, showing how the tool's technical design and governance rules actively shaped (and constrained) what could be investigated before its 2024 shutdown. [[Bruns2026-pn]] extends this into the current "clean room" model exemplified by the Meta Content Library, finding that web-disconnected, code-only analysis environments systematically exclude qualitative researchers, prevent cross-platform or longitudinal work, and widen inequities between well-resourced and Majority World institutions. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] puts this infrastructure to direct empirical use, exploiting the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian parliamentarians' Facebook reach by up to 72%—rolled out roughly ten months before Meta's announced timeline and never disclosed in DSA transparency reports—while extremist accounts compensated through posting volume, a finding that simultaneously indicts Meta's transparency practices and vindicates the research value of DSA-mandated access.

## Industry capture of the evidence base

A metascientific thread asks whether the research literature on platforms is itself compromised by the very companies it studies. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws the tobacco/pharma/food analogy explicitly, cataloguing four channels of platform influence over academic research; [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its empirical companion [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantify this, finding roughly half of high-profile social-media papers in Science/Nature/PNAS have undisclosed industry ties, with ~80% "industrial saturation" once editors and reviewers are included, and systematic topical bias toward user-focused misinformation research over platform-dynamics research. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a methodological verdict on the paradigm case of industry-academic collaboration—the Meta2020 partnership—arguing that one-off, underpowered field experiments on rapidly mutating platforms lack both "temporal" and "poetic" validity, and that only proactive regulation (echoing the DSA model) can substitute for social science's structural inability to keep pace. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds by championing "platform-independent" experimental methods (browser-extension LLM reranking) as a necessary adaptation to closing API access. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]]'s earlier Facebook-ad-based survey-recruitment method for hard-to-reach populations anticipates this same turn toward independent, platform-adjacent methodologies developed precisely because sanctioned access could not be relied upon.

## Platforms narrating themselves

Several papers treat platform communication itself as data. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom discursively externalizes blame for "problematic content" onto inauthentic outside actors while claiming credit as technological problem-solver; [[De2026-ld]] theorizes "changecraft," the strategic discursive management of platform change across Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and X; [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] extends this to Google's AI Overviews, tracing a contest between Google, journalists, and SEO marketers over the meaning of generative search. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s foundational analysis of platform rhetoric underwrites all three. [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] globalizes the critique, arguing that platforms' privatization of public discourse produces acute systemic risk in the Global South, where weak regulatory infrastructure (illustrated by Brazil's failed "Fake News Bill") deepens data-colonial asymmetries unaddressed by Global North frameworks like the DSA. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes "misinformation-at-scale" as a structural feature of engagement-driven business models rather than an aberration, while [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Bruns2026-yv]] both indict platforms' causal role in democratic backsliding. [[Swartz2026-zb]] and [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] push the discourse into adjacent territory—scams and deepfake fraud—arguing that platform governance of "legitimate" versus "illegitimate" economic activity, and the liability regimes needed to police AI-enabled fraud, are continuous with the same infrastructural politics.

## Moderation in practice: reduction, evasion, and crowd governance

A large empirical cluster examines what platforms actually do with the content they claim to govern. [[Gillespie2022-jx]] reframes the entire debate by foregrounding "reduction"—algorithmic demotion without removal—as the dominant, least transparent form of content moderation. [[Rogers2026-zz]] and [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] trace Meta's shrinking, depoliticizing redefinition of "problematic content" (from "fake news" to "borderline") alongside its narrowing data-access regimes, showing that surviving borderline content remains disproportionately right-wing despite official content-agnosticism. [[Simeone2025-vo]] documents the disproportionate "network ripple effects" of Twitter's 2021 deplatforming of election-audit accounts; [[Karo2026-dn]] and [[Rieder2026-pp]] document adversarial evasion and ambient persistence of extremist and misogynist content on TikTok and YouTube despite stated zero-tolerance policies; [[Crichton2026-kn]] extends this to generative-AI "re-generation" of far-right imagery. [[Moran2025-qn]] surveys the broader institutional retrenchment of trust-and-safety work itself. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] turn to the fact-checking ecosystem under threat: the former documenting that Meta's own fact-checked content was rarely removed even before Zuckerberg's 2025 dismantling of the program, the latter analyzing how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform-funded independence. The rise of crowd-sourced alternatives is scrutinized by [[Renault2025-uh]], [[Bouchaud2026-np]], and [[Miller2026-cp]], who collectively show that X's Community Notes exhibits partisan asymmetry, structurally undermoderates polarizing content by design, and is now being scaled via an open AI-writer API that preserves (at least nominally) human arbitration. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] round out this cluster with measurement-driven accounts of misinformation prevalence and algorithmic health-information gatekeeping, while [[Balluff2026-bv]] shows parallel dynamics of covert media capture (the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre") operating through advertising rather than platform code.

## Elections, advertising, and algorithmic effects

A final cluster uses whatever access remains to interrogate platforms' concrete political effects. [[McNally2025-dn]] shows Facebook's News Feed is "not a black box" by detecting lagged, differential algorithmic treatment of hard news via CrowdTangle-era data, explicitly advocating DSA-enabled systemic audits. [[Votta2025-xz]] examines how ad-delivery algorithms shape the cost and reach of political campaigns; [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] proposes an "embedded referrer paradigm" showing platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is 1.5–15x larger than direct-referral measures suggest, with sharply partisan and platform-specific patterns. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] demonstrates that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes users' political ideology in a linear embedding direction, raising profiling concerns under GDPR and the DSA alike, while [[Ahuja2025-ku]] builds a "law-to-design" framework mapping dark-pattern taxonomies onto DSA Article 25's autonomy-violation categories, applying it directly to the Commission's live case against X. Together, this final group exemplifies the volume's governing tension: as official access narrows, researchers increasingly must infer platform power indirectly—through embeddings, lagged correlations, and counterfactual simulation—precisely the kind of work that Article 40 and its successors are meant, however imperfectly, to make unnecessary.
