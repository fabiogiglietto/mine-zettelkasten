# Platform Data Governance & Access

## The closing of the open web and the shape of a discipline

The starting point for this topic is a historical rupture. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes nearly two decades of platform data policy—from API "prehistory" through a brief "academic cooperation" era to today's fragmented regime of pay-to-play APIs, walled gardens, and unofficial scraping—and argues that access is now permanently contingent on corporate discretion rather than a stable research infrastructure. Bastos2025-ya and [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrate the specific case of Twitter/X, tracing how an unusually open, prosocial network became commercially and politically weaponized after Musk's acquisition, while [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] generalizes this arc—"Wild West" to "Golden Age" to "Walled Garden"—across platforms, showing that Meta's CrowdTangle closure, Reddit's pricing, and TikTok's restrictions are instances of the same structural pattern rather than idiosyncratic events. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] supplies the bibliometric evidence for what this rupture cost science: a scientometric "eulogy" showing Twitter-based scholarship growing 25% annually through 2022 before stagnating and then declining 13% in 2024 once Enterprise API pricing took hold. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s broader systematic review confirms the pattern at the level of the whole social-science literature—platform-based empirical research tripled from 2010 but plateaued and fell in 2022–2024—while also showing a chronic mismatch between what platforms researchers study (X, Facebook) and what the public actually uses (YouTube, TikTok), a distortion that access economics itself helps produce. [[Cullen2026-cb]] gives this history an ethnographic register: interviews with CrowdTangle users reveal how the tool's technical design and governance rules constituted a "Seeing like an API" epistemics that shaped—and limited—what could be asked and known before the tool's discontinuation. Zooming out further, [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] situate the stakes of this infrastructural closure in explicitly democratic terms: platforms' privatization of public discourse and their algorithmic governance of visibility are framed as contributors to global democratic backsliding, with [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]] emphasizing how these harms are amplified in the Global South by weak regulatory capacity and "data colonialism."

## The regulatory answer: DSA Article 40 as infrastructure-in-the-making

Against this backdrop, the EU's Digital Services Act—and specifically Article 40's vetted-researcher access provision—emerges as the field's central institutional hope and its most contested terrain. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[Philipp2026-tl]], and the companion Forum piece [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] converge on a shared diagnosis: Article 40 represents a genuine legal shift from platform "philanthropy" to a binding right, but its early implementation is beset by opaque procedures, inconsistent timelines, and strategic platform resistance—friction that de Vreese and Tromble insist should be read as evidence the regulation is working, not failing. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and its twin [[Pierri2026-ib]], written from inside an ERC-supported pilot with DG-CONNECT, catalogue the concrete asymmetries obstructing this promise: a "circular problem of resources" whereby data access is needed to win grants but funding is needed to pursue access, mismatches in expectations between researchers, Data Protection Officers, and Digital Services Coordinators, and a glaring regulatory blind spot around large language models. [[Peters2026-mo]] adds a discursive layer to this account, showing through analysis of EU consultation documents that "data quality" is not a neutral methodological criterion but a contested concept: academics and NGOs pushed the term into the Delegated Regulation while platforms—Meta notably—strategically inverted the argument, claiming poor data quality made access itself pointless. [[Lukito2026-nb]] broadens the diagnostic vocabulary with a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent and access infrastructures systematically WEIRD-centric, while [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] offers a companion survey of the DSA-era API landscape. [[Heiss2026-qv]] connects this regulatory story to the industry-influence literature discussed below, arguing that DSA-style mandated access is one of only two viable levers (alongside stronger research-on-research norms) against a research ecosystem uniquely dependent on the entities it studies. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes the normative implications further, urging the field to move past individual-level causal-effects paradigms toward theorizing "platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure" now that DSA-era data regimes make aggregate, infrastructural analysis feasible.

## Auditing the promise: does the new access actually work?

A cluster of papers empirically stress-tests the tools DSA compliance has produced, and the verdict is consistently sobering. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's expanded Research API riddled with unexplained limitations; [[Rieder2025-ju]] conducts a rigorous six-month audit of YouTube's Data API search endpoint, discovering a "forgetful by design" temporal-decay effect that makes historical retrieval unreliable for exactly the kind of retrospective systemic-risk research the DSA envisions; [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's content-moderation claims around the 2024 EU elections and finds inconsistent application of authoritative-source labeling and opaque removal communications. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] uses DSA transparency-mandated data itself to expose a further inequity: human moderation workforces are disproportionately concentrated in English, leaving Global South languages like Arabic, Spanish, and Portuguese—and in some cases entire EU languages—under- or unmoderated. [[Bruns2026-pn]] examines the "clean room" model (the Meta Content Library, ProQuest TDM Studio) that has replaced open downloads, arguing that web-disconnected, code-only environments systematically exclude qualitative researchers, smaller institutions, and non-English contexts even as they formally satisfy Article 40. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds to this landscape from a different angle, building TubeStats and TokStats as independent, genuinely random sampling infrastructures to counteract the popularity-biased samples that opportunistic, API-constrained research otherwise produces. [[Schulte2026-df]] documents the practical costs of these bottlenecks for a live case—the 2025 German federal election—concluding that platform observability itself must be treated as a reusable research infrastructure rather than a series of one-off scraping projects. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] rounds out this methodological seam by showing that even scraping-based alternatives to platform APIs introduce their own severe, non-randomly distributed measurement error.

## The Meta URL Shares Dataset: a case study in data artifacts

One specific data-access lineage—Meta's URL Shares Dataset, born from the Social Science One partnership and continuing through its Privacy-Protected successor—receives sustained methodological scrutiny as a microcosm of platform-mediated knowledge production. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] identifies a striking artifact: the dataset's 100-public-shares anonymization threshold produces near-identical monthly trajectories across 46 wildly different countries, revealing that platform-wide algorithmic (likely Meaningful-Social-Interactions-related) changes, not local events, drive apparent "signal." [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends Guess et al.'s US-based framework to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising shares of untrustworthy URLs around election years but stable Views, and cautioning that NewsGuard's US-skewed coverage makes transatlantic comparison unreliable. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes furthest, showing that the relationship between shares and views is dynamically moderated by partisan alignment and journalistic quality, with these penalties and rewards fluctuating in lockstep with known Meta governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures)—direct evidence that Facebook operates as an active curator rather than neutral conduit. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] use the same dataset lineage to probe consequences: the former finds untrustworthy-source exposure concentrated among few users with no measurable attitudinal effects from reducing it, while the latter, prompted by Meta's 2025 fact-checking rollback, shows that user "False News" reports mostly flag disagreeable political content rather than genuinely false claims—undermining the wisdom-of-crowds rationale behind Community Notes.

## Political content reduction and the visibility of political actors

The topic's second empirical pole concerns how moderation policy—especially Meta's political-content reduction—reshapes the visibility of political actors, and here the papers form a tight argumentative chain. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] establishes the discursive groundwork, showing how Meta's Newsroom frames "problematic content" as originating from outside actors while eliding the role of hyperpartisan Pages and Facebook Groups—rhetoric [[De2026-ld]] generalizes into a theory of "changecraft," whereby platforms narrate policy shifts (including the fact-checking pivot) as continuity rather than rupture. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] provides the paper's most granular causal test: using the Meta Content Library to detect structural breakpoints, it finds the political-content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72% roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout, with extremist accounts compensating through sharply increased posting volume—a transparency deficit that also validates the DSA-enabled Content Library as a genuine (if limited) research advance. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] contextualizes this single-country finding within a five-platform, two-decade policy history, identifying three phases (protection, civic-integrity expansion, backlash/rollback) and arguing recent rollbacks reflect "platform illiberalism" rather than principled free-speech commitments. [[McNally2025-dn]] offers methodological reinforcement from a different angle, using CrowdTangle-era Guardian engagement data to show Facebook's News Feed is not an unknowable "black box" but a system whose interventions leave detectable, laggedtraces—an argument for exactly the kind of systemic audit the DSA's Article 40(4) is meant to enable. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] complicates the moderation picture further by showing that Meta's own definition of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" has narrowed over time from broad "false amplifiers" to narrow "adversarial actors," letting much manufactured-attention activity persist under looser scrutiny, while [[Bouchaud2026-np]] shows that X's Community Notes bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most relevant to electoral integrity.

## Industry capture of the scientific record

A third strand turns the analytical lens on the research community itself. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together document, with the rigor of an audit, how thoroughly platform industry influence—via funding, collaboration, and undisclosed editorial ties—pervades high-profile social media science: roughly half of papers in top journals have disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, with industry-tied work receiving double the citations and attention while skewing away from platform-level dynamics toward user-blaming misinformation framings. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this dependency structurally, arguing platforms' monopoly on the data needed to study them makes social media research uniquely vulnerable compared to tobacco, pharma, or food science, where independent data generation remains possible. Together these papers cast the DSA's mandated-access provisions not merely as a data-access fix but as a potential correction to a deeper epistemic capture problem.

## Adjacent currents: measurement, fraud, and the wider moderation landscape

A final set of papers extends the topic's concerns outward. [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Karo2026-dn]] document the persistence of harmful content despite moderation commitments—rising disinformation prevalence on TikTok in one case, jihadist "everyday extremism" evading keyword filters in the other—while [[Rieder2026-pp]] shows how deplatforming Andrew Tate produced not erasure but an ambient, decentralized "Tate-space" that recommendation systems outpace moderation efforts to contain. [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] extends platform accountability debates into deepfake financial fraud, arguing regulatory liability should shift from victims to platform gatekeepers. [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] examine fact-checkers' precarious institutional position vis-à-vis platform funding and Meta's 2025 dismantling of third-party fact-checking, finding fact-checkers defend platform partnerships rhetorically while remaining skeptical that Community Notes can substitute for professional verification. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and Bastos2025-ol use computational methods to detect covert media capture and troll-farm visual signatures respectively, both exemplifying the kind of forensic, data-intensive accountability research this whole literature is fighting to keep possible. [[Crosset2026-mq]] supplies a comparative legal-theoretical capstone, showing that EU and US content-moderation legislation—despite different "free circulation," "patrolling," and "flow optimisation" logics—converges on a shared mechanism of transparency-driven "security bureaucratisation," turning platforms into quasi-public infrastructures. [[Moran2025-qn]] closes the loop by documenting the resulting human cost inside platforms: the professional field of "trust and safety" itself is undergoing serious upheaval, driven by political pressure and retrenchment, a fragility that mirrors—and partly explains—the precarious data infrastructure this entire body of work has been built to study. [[Allen2025-ot]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] point to adaptive responses at the individual-study level: platform-independent browser-extension experiments and Facebook-ad-based survey recruitment, respectively, as workarounds researchers have devised while waiting for the DSA-era infrastructure to mature. [[Donovan2025-ws]] provides a longue-durée account of why none of this is likely to be resolved by platforms alone, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is structurally endogenous to engagement-driven business models rather than an external anomaly moderation can fully correct.
