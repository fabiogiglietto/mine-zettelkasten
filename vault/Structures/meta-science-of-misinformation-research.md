# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The field turns the mirror on itself

A growing cluster of papers treats misinformation and online-harms research not as a body of findings to be extended but as an object of critique in its own right. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] sets the tone by importing six lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology — attend to motivation and emotion, not just cognition; measure real behaviour, not proxies; mind contextual heterogeneity; think in systems; be wary of company collaborations; organise collectively against coordinated attacks on the science. This comment functions almost as a table of contents for the rest of the literature reviewed here: nearly every subsequent diagnosis elaborates one of its six points. [[Mahl2026-hc]] complements this with a Delphi study of 47 experts who likewise conclude there is "no silver bullet," that individual-level fixes (prebunking, literacy, fact-checking) are rated less important than platform governance and journalism reform, and — tellingly — that generative AI's disruptive potential was simply not on experts' radar as late as 2022, underscoring how fast-moving the object of study is relative to the field's capacity for synthesis. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extend the self-assessment to the adjacent (and heavily overlapping) field of polarization research, calling respectively for scientists to take applied responsibility for depolarization and for a more disciplinarily integrated, globally representative research agenda. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] offers a reflexive coda to all of this: it historicizes the very "decline narrative" that animates so much harms research, showing how terms like techlash and enshittification do political, affective, and commercial work, risk a Western-centric "corporational determinism," and cast users as passive victims — a caution against letting field-level pessimism calcify into its own unexamined orthodoxy. [[Baym2026-tr]] stands as a case in point from within: revisiting her own decade-old manifesto, Baym finds that the critical diagnoses were correct but mostly inert, concluding that critique-for-its-own-sake must give way to interdisciplinary, cross-sector engagement.

## WEIRD-centrism and the limits of generalization

A central, recurring diagnosis is that the evidence base is geographically and culturally narrow. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around moving misinformation research beyond WEIRD contexts, and the empirical papers filed alongside it bear out the concern: [[Nenno2025-xa]] finds that news-value signatures of flagged misinformation differ, if modestly, between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than representative cases; [[Humprecht2020-gd]] shows that structural "resilience" to disinformation clusters nationally, placing the United States in a category of one rather than treating it as a stand-in for "the West." The same critique recurs outside misinformation proper: [[Bravo2026-ue]] finds climate-visualization research disproportionately trained on Western traditional media, and [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization shows a corpus skewed to the UK, Germany, and the US, with over half the literature not even geographically specified. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] names this pattern explicitly for polarization research. Taken together, these papers suggest the WEIRD problem is not a quirk of any one subfield but a structural feature of how digital-trace and survey infrastructure concentrate around a handful of rich, English-speaking, democratic contexts — a pattern [[Spampatti2026-kx]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]] both flag as a priority for the field to correct.

## From cognitive proxies to real-world, longitudinal evidence

A second major thread interrogates whether the field has mistaken easily measured cognitive proxies (belief change, discernment scores, sharing intentions) for the real-world behavioural and societal harms that motivate public concern. [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s observation that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure high-impact behavioural outcomes is corroborated empirically by a run of large-scale, high-powered studies that find strikingly modest effects once researchers look past cognitive proxies: [[Guess2023-ai]] shows that stripping reshares from Facebook feeds reshapes exposure dramatically but leaves polarization and most attitudes untouched; [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram concentrated among a tiny minority with no detectable attitudinal effect of reducing it; [[Lyons2026-ca]] similarly finds low-credibility health content exposure rare in the aggregate but concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded users; and [[Allen2020-nj]] and its companion [[Allen2021-ai]] recalibrate the entire "fake news" alarm by showing that news of any kind — let alone fake news — is a vanishingly small share of the whole media diet, and that the Social Science One URL dataset's 100-share censoring threshold artificially inflates fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold. [[Budak2024-ef]] synthesizes this empirical turn into an explicit corrective: public and policy discourse has drifted from the evidence on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies a structural explanation for why both citizens and researchers are liable to over-infer from visible content: a small, vocal minority produces most political content, while platform-trace data inevitably oversamples that "tip of the iceberg." Underlying much of this empirical correction are older cognitive-psychology foundations — [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] on the continued-influence effect and the fragility of corrections, [[Kunda1990-cg]] on motivated reasoning, and [[Pennycook2021-jq]]'s influential reframing of discernment failures as a reasoning deficit rather than partisan motivation — whose proxy-heavy methodologies the newer behavioural-measurement papers are implicitly arguing the field has over-relied upon. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] adds a structural twist, arguing mainstream media (not fringe sources or social platforms) may be the primary vector by which fake news reaches mass publics — a finding later echoed empirically in [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study, where Australian participants attributed most everyday "misinformation" to mainstream and alternative news outlets rather than fringe social accounts.

## Industry capture and the political economy of platform research

A distinct but closely related diagnosis concerns who controls the data and funding that makes platform research possible at all. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, empirically, that roughly half of high-profile social-media research carries undisclosed industry ties, that this influence is concentrated among a small set of repeatedly engaged scientists who also populate editorial and review roles, and that industry-tied work is skewed toward user-focused topics (like misinformation sharing) rather than platform-scale dynamics — drawing explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel agnotology. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the structural problem starkly: unlike food or pharmaceutical science, platform research has no independent route to data generation, making researchers uniquely dependent on the very companies they study. [[Munger2025-cz]] turns this lens on the landmark Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing that even the most ambitious industry-academic collaboration produced narrow, rapidly decaying estimates with questionable external and "poetic" validity, and that null results conveniently served Meta's interests given the project's methodological compromises. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] offers a parallel methodological warning from inside a flagship dataset: anonymization thresholds in the Social Science One URL Shares data introduce platform-wide artifacts (likely algorithmic feed changes) that can masquerade as cross-national or diachronic "signal." Collectively these papers argue that methodological rigor cannot be separated from questions of who grants data access and on what terms — a concern [[Matias2025-px]] inverts productively by showing how participatory, lived-experience involvement can strengthen rather than merely legitimate AI and platform evaluation.

## Data infrastructure in crisis: APIs, access, and regulatory remedies

Closely linked is a cluster diagnosing the field's growing infrastructural precarity. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the scholarly cost of Twitter's API closure — a 13% publication decline in 2024 after a "Golden Age" of ~25% annual growth — while [[Yang2026-tq]] shows the broader pattern across social-science disciplines, with Twitter/X and Facebook dominating empirical social-media research disproportionately to their public use, and overall output plateauing post-2022. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodizes this history as a move from "Wild West" data philanthropy through a brief "Golden Age" to a "Walled Garden" of commercial paywalls, arguing the EU's Digital Services Act Article 40 represents an attempted (but unevenly implemented) shift from discretionary platform generosity to regulatory right. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] substantiate this from different angles — the former cataloguing legal, technical, and resource asymmetries that still hamper DSA-based research access, the latter showing that "data quality" itself was almost entirely absent from official EU documents until academic and NGO stakeholders fought to insert it. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API supplies a sobering empirical case: findable videos decay sharply within weeks of publication, undermining both historical research and DSA-mandated systemic-risk assessments. Together these papers argue that much of what looks like empirical disagreement in the field may really be a disagreement about brittle, shifting, and asymmetrically governed data infrastructure.

## Conceptual fragmentation and measurement validity

A parallel diagnostic strand concerns definitional and operational inconsistency within the field's core constructs. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and measurement rather than divergent reality — a structurally identical argument to [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s finding that depolarization recommendations are rarely grounded in the evidence scholars themselves produce, resting instead on unexamined normative ideals of deliberative democracy. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit on NewsGuard, the most widely used source-trustworthiness database, showing that continuous scores are reasonably stable but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can radically distort downstream findings depending on which major outlet happens to cross threshold. Definitional fragmentation also plagues work on coordinated manipulation: [[Mannocci2026-kc]] surveys the field's incompatible industry and academic definitions of coordinated inauthentic behavior and proposes a unifying framework around actors, synergic actions, and intent; [[Thiele2025-ol]] critiques the platform-derived "coordinated inauthentic behavior" concept as conflating inauthenticity with manipulative intent and proposes a rational-choice attribution model instead; and [[Pante2025-pq]] shows that prior claims of inter-state coordination on Twitter/X do not survive more rigorous detection methods and proper control datasets — a cautionary tale about over-claiming from thin behavioral signals. At a more conceptual level, [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that dominant analogies for misinformation (virus, warfare, pollution) each illuminate only part of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive system and can mislead intervention design; [[Sadler2025-vu]] makes a parallel argument about the field's loose, common-sense use of "narrative," proposing a hermeneutic-realist alternative to simple true/false binaries; and [[Freelon2020-yp]] and the foundational [[Lazer2018-mm]] trace how quickly and unevenly the field's core vocabulary (disinformation, fake news, misinformation) was assembled after 2016, with Lazer et al. explicitly flagging the dearth of evidence on prevalence and long-term effects that later papers in this note (Allen, Bergeron-Boutin, Guess) would go on to address empirically.

## Everyday, ecological, and longitudinal blind spots

Several papers argue the field has over-indexed on a narrow slice of phenomena — hot-button political topics, short-term lab exposure, Twitter-centric data — at the expense of everyday, longitudinal, and cross-domain dynamics. [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s diary study finds that lived misinformation encounters span business, celebrity, and crime content far more than canonical "political" misinformation, and are overwhelmingly text-based rather than the audiovisual/deepfake content that dominates scholarly alarm. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country analysis of trust in news similarly complicates a common origin story: trust decline predates the internet, is not universal, and even reverses in "Not Free" media systems — a reminder that many phenomena attributed to digital misinformation have longer and more heterogeneous histories than platform-centric research designs can capture. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s radicalization review makes a structurally similar point about longitudinal and causal evidence: despite an "empirical turn" since 2018, causal and longitudinal work remains rare, with most research relying on exposure or engagement proxies. [[Marwick2025-vx]] pushes back on the field's "exposure equals belief" model from within radicalization studies, showing through qualitative work that most Far-Right adherents describe gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion — directly undercutting single-exposure causal assumptions common in experimental misinformation research. [[Bennett2025-xs]] extends this critique to democratic backsliding writ large, arguing that technocentric, individual-cognition-focused disinformation research (citing roughly $70 million in funding concentrated on correction-based paradigms) has crowded out institutionalist analysis of how networked publics organize as "digital surrogate organizations." [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] both push toward treating conspiracy and disinformation as processes embedded in journalistic and narrative dynamics rather than discrete claims to be debunked, with Ramos et al.'s Valencia flood case study demonstrating concretely how claim-by-claim fact-checking fails against fast-evolving, emotionally resonant narratives. [[Scalco2026-bd]] responds to a specific, previously under-operationalized gap — "information voids" — by building a quantitative detection method rather than relying on qualitative or anecdotal accounts, modeling one route by which the field's conceptual critiques can be turned into usable measurement.

## New tools, new dependencies: AI and methodological reflexivity

Finally, a cluster of papers turns the field's self-critical lens onto its own rapidly expanding use of large language models. [[Balluff2026-if]] warns that LLM adoption in communication research has outpaced reflection on reproducibility, corporate dependency, bias, and environmental cost, advocating a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] offers a constructive instantiation of that advice, showing fine-tuned open-source LLAMA models can match or beat proprietary GPT-4 performance on social-science classification tasks while preserving transparency and reproducibility. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] pushes the question further by benchmarking whether AI coding agents can themselves reproduce published social-science findings — finding surprisingly high accuracy, but also discovering that agents become sycophantic under confirmatory prompting, fabricating "reproductions" of results that cannot actually be reproduced — a sharp, self-reflexive warning about automating the field's own verification infrastructure. [[Matias2025-px]], discussed above in the industry context, closes the loop by arguing that participatory, lived-experience involvement is not just an ethical add-on but a way to materially improve the rigor of AI evaluation.

## Borrowed lessons: what climate psychology offers

Running beneath several of these threads is an explicit appeal to climate psychology and climate communication as a model for a more mature science. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the programmatic statement, but the comparison is substantiated by papers that actually import climate-science methodology: [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages demonstrates the kind of high-powered, preregistered, generalizability-tested design that misinformation research is urged to adopt, finding that even well-cited strategies produce only small attitudinal effects and no movement on costly behaviour — directly illustrating the "cognitive-proxy versus real-world-outcome" gap diagnosed elsewhere in this note. [[Lieu2025-nl]] applies parallel rigor to climate misinformation content itself, finding that argument content (not underlying logical fallacy) drives perceived veracity and political polarization, with implications for how both fields should prioritize intervention targets. [[Bravo2026-ue]]'s review of climate-visualization research, despite diagnosing the field's own Western and traditional-media bias, nonetheless models the kind of systematic, gap-mapping self-assessment that [[Spampatti2026-kx]] calls on misinformation researchers to emulate. Read together, these borrowed lessons suggest that the misinformation field's path toward rigor and generalizability runs not through isolated methodological fixes but through the kind of coordinated, transparent, and collectively organized science that climate research has been forced to build under decades of organized opposition.
