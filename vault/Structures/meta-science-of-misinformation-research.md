# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## A Field Turning Its Instruments on Itself

Misinformation research has reached a reflexive moment: rather than producing another empirical study of belief or sharing, a growing cluster of papers steps back to ask whether the field's evidentiary base, conceptual vocabulary, and institutional arrangements are fit for purpose. The tone is often more self-critical than triumphant. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 international experts captures this mood directly: respondents see current scholarship as fragmented, overly individual-level, and poorly anticipatory, and they are pessimistic that the problem is improving even as new challenges (synthetic media, algorithmic opacity, eroding institutional trust) accumulate. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames the stakes explicitly by importing six lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology, arguing that misinformation research still measures the wrong things, at the wrong scale, in the wrong places.

## WEIRD-Centrism and the Geography of Evidence

A recurring diagnosis is that the field's empirical base is geographically and culturally narrow. [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction names the problem outright — misinformation scholarship is disproportionately WEIRD — and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel case for polarization research, which remains disciplinarily siloed and US-fixated. [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies a concrete empirical test of this worry: analyzing news values in perceived misinformation across twenty-four countries, it finds real but modest WEIRD/non-WEIRD differences, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than as representative cases from which global claims can be extrapolated. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of online radicalization research turns up the same pattern at the level of a cognate field — Western countries dominate, over half of studies do not even specify geographic context, and causal/longitudinal evidence is thin. Taken together, these pieces suggest that the "universal" mechanisms described in canonical studies — the virality findings of [[Vosoughi2018-at]], the correction failures catalogued by [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], the reasoning account of [[Pennycook2021-jq]] — rest on a narrower evidentiary footing than their influence implies.

## Overreliance on Cognitive Proxies, Underreliance on Scale

A second diagnosis concerns measurement: the field has preferred lab-friendly cognitive proxies (belief ratings, sharing intentions) over real-world behavioral outcomes, and this has distorted perceived stakes. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] states the complaint bluntly — fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure high-impact real-world behavior — and [[Budak2024-ef]] argues that public discourse has drifted from what evidence actually shows regarding prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. This corrective line has deep empirical roots: [[Allen2020-nj]] recalibrated the entire "fake news crisis" narrative by showing that fake news is a vanishingly small share (~0.15%) of Americans' total media diet, dwarfed by television; [[Guess2020-rr]] showed that untrustworthy-site exposure during the 2016 election was real but narrowly concentrated rather than pervasive; and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], working with platform-scale Facebook/Instagram data, finds the same concentration pattern and no detectable attitudinal effects from reducing exposure. [[Lyons2026-ca]] extends this logic to health misinformation, finding exposure genuinely rare in the aggregate but sharply concentrated among older, more conspiracist-minded users — a finding that complicates simple "exposure causes belief" stories. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies the structural explanation for why visible online discourse so badly misleads both citizens and researchers: a small, vocal minority produces most content, so platform data inevitably overrepresents extremity. Methodologically, [[Allen2021-ai]] shows this concentration bias can be baked into the data infrastructure itself — Social Science One's URL-censoring threshold inflates apparent fake-news prevalence roughly fourfold relative to a representative panel, a cautionary tale about "big data, biased inference."

## Conceptual Housekeeping: Metaphors, Narratives, and Definitions

Several papers argue that the field's foundational vocabulary is doing more obscuring than clarifying work. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] interrogates the dominant "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies, showing each captures only one level of a multilevel complex system and can license backfiring interventions. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] performs similar surgery on "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles," proposing a feedback-loop framework that reconciles weak empirical support for bubbles with real, observed self-reinforcing dynamics — a conceptual move that [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies empirically motivates, showing that scholarly disagreement often traces to incompatible operationalizations rather than to the phenomenon itself. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] represent an earlier wave of this definitional labor, carving "disinformation" away from "fake news" and "misinformation" and situating the problem within a longer-run "disinformation order" tied to institutional decline rather than mere platform technology — a framing [[Bennett2025-xs]] revisits and extends, arguing that technocentric, individual-cognition-focused disinformation research has crowded out institutionalist analysis of how networked publics organize into "digital surrogate organizations." [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes conceptual critique furthest, arguing that "narrative" has been used naively in disinformation studies and that the true/false binary cannot capture content that is referentially and ethically troubling without being straightforwardly false; [[Marwick2025-vx]] complements this by showing that far-right "redpilling" is better understood as gradual socialization through accumulated "evidence" than as sudden exposure-driven conversion, undercutting simple media-effects models. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] likewise finds that everyday, user-flagged misinformation looks nothing like the hot-button political/health content the literature privileges — it is mostly text, mostly from mainstream outlets, and concentrated in mundane domains like personal finance.

## Industry Entanglement and the Data-Access Crisis

A distinct but intersecting critique concerns who controls the evidence. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] both draw explicit parallels to tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science, arguing that platforms' monopoly on data about their own products creates structural dependency unmatched in other social sciences. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this empirically: roughly half of high-profile social media research has disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeat-collaborator elite who also populate editorial and review roles, with topical bias toward user-level misinformation research over platform-dynamics research. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership makes a related but distinct point — even the most ambitious, best-resourced collaboration produced narrow, low-powered, conceptually incoherent estimates whose "temporal validity" expires before replication is possible, leading him to conclude that reactive empirical social science cannot keep pace with platforms and that proactive regulation is required instead. The infrastructural side of this crisis is documented by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] (a "eulogy" quantifying the sharp decline in Twitter-based scholarship after API paywalling), [[Yang2026-tq]] (showing social-media-based social science has plateaued and declined since 2022, with platform usage misaligned with actual public platform use), and [[Rieder2025-ju]] (an audit finding YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," unsuitable for the longitudinal, representative queries that DSA-era systemic-risk research requires). [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard adds a parallel lesson for source-based measurement: continuous trust scores are reasonably stable, but binary thresholds can flip conclusions depending on which few high-profile sources cross the cutoff — a reminder that widely-reused infrastructure can silently determine findings.

## Borrowed Lessons: Climate Psychology and Adjacent Fields

Several papers look outward for templates of a more mature science. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the most direct of these, explicitly transplanting six insights from climate psychology — on intervention design, behavioral measurement, generalizability, systems thinking, company collaboration, and collective scientific organization. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy exemplifies the kind of rigor being recommended: a preregistered, large-N test of the ten most-cited climate messages finds generally small effects, little partisan heterogeneity, and a stark attitude-behavior gap, modeling exactly the kind of humble, well-powered design misinformation research is urged to adopt. [[Lieu2025-nl]] similarly imports structured taxonomies (CARDS, FLICC) from climate misinformation research to test logic-based versus content-based framings, finding content category — not fallacy type — drives perceived veracity and polarization. These climate-adjacent studies function less as topical outliers than as proof-of-concept for the kind of megastudy, preregistered, cross-ideology-tested science the reflective papers are calling for.

## Evidence-Recommendation Gaps and Normative Transparency

A parallel strand of reflection targets the gap between what research finds and what it recommends. [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s review of 89 polarization papers finds that depolarization recommendations are almost never grounded in the studies' own evidence, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments (chiefly deliberative-democratic ideals) — a critique of scholarly practice itself rather than of any empirical finding. The Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] voices the applied counterpart, urging researchers toward a civic responsibility to test depolarization interventions more rigorously, while [[Ramos2026-qo]], writing from inside a fact-checking organization, argues that claim-level fact-checking is structurally insufficient against adaptive, emotionally resonant disinformation narratives and proposes narrative-monitoring and argument-checking as complements — itself a reflection on the limits of the field's dominant intervention paradigm, alongside the earlier correction-focused psychology of [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] and the motivated-reasoning foundations laid by [[Kunda1990-cg]] and complicated by [[Pennycook2021-jq]]'s "classical reasoning" account. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]] occupy a similar position as early calls for interdisciplinary rigor and platform-academic collaboration whose warnings about evidentiary gaps the present cluster of papers shows have only partly been heeded.

## Methodological Self-Scrutiny in the Age of AI

Finally, a newer set of papers turns reflexive attention to the tools researchers now use. [[Balluff2026-if]] critiques the unreflective adoption of LLMs in communication research — for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulated experiments — citing reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs, and calls for a resource-proportionate, open-science-aligned methodology. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] probe AI's role from the opposite direction, asking whether coding agents and automated review tools can themselves reproduce or evaluate social-science findings — with genuinely strong results but notable failure modes (sycophantic specification search, bias from contextual metadata) that bear directly on how the field might scale rigor. [[Matias2025-px]] closes the loop by arguing that participatory, lived-experience involvement is not merely an ethical nicety but can materially improve the scientific validity of AI evaluation — a fitting coda to a literature whose central message is that misinformation and online-harms research must become more methodologically humble, more globally representative, more behaviorally grounded, and more institutionally self-aware if it is to match the scale and consequence of the problems it studies.
