# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## Naming the malady: field-level diagnoses

A cluster of these papers steps back to ask what kind of field misinformation research actually is, and whether its habits of thought match the problem it studies. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] sets the tone by treating misinformation research as a younger sibling to climate psychology, arguing that both fields face organised, vested-interest opposition and share the same structural dilemmas (individual vs. collective interest, short- vs. long-term incentives), yet misinformation research has not yet absorbed the methodological maturity — attention to real-world behavioural outcomes, systems thinking, cross-country variation — that climate psychology has earned the hard way. [[Mahl2026-hc]] operationalises a similar stock-taking impulse empirically, using a Delphi panel of 47 experts to show that the field is seen as fragmented, overly individualistic in its interventions, and poorly anticipatory (notably, none of its 2022 experts foresaw generative AI as a disruptive force). [[Bennett2025-xs]] pushes the critique further by arguing that the dominant "technocentric" paradigm — individual-level cognitive effects of dis/misinformation — has crowded out institutionalist explanations of democratic backsliding, a bias the authors trace partly to where research funding (~$70 million post-2016) has flowed. [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] bracket this discussion from opposite ends: the former argues public and policy discourse has drifted from what the evidence actually shows about prevalence, algorithms, and harm, while the latter is a call to arms for scientists to take applied responsibility for shrinking political division, treating the research community itself as an underused resource. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] adds a conceptual diagnosis underneath all of this, showing how the field's dominant metaphors — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each illuminate one layer of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive system while obscuring the others, so that interventions built on a single analogy can misfire or even backfire.

## The WEIRD problem and the geography of evidence

A recurring complaint is that the field's evidence base is geographically and culturally narrow. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around this gap, explicitly naming the WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) skew as a threat to generalizability. [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies a concrete empirical test, comparing news-value expressions in flagged misinformation across 24 countries and finding the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divide real but modest — with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than representative cases — which cautions against both over- and under-stating cross-national difference. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the same argument for polarization research, noting that the field is disciplinarily fragmented and disproportionately anchored in the US, and calling for integrated, globally equitable frameworks. [[Rothut2026-wt]] finds an analogous skew in 25 years of online radicalization scholarship, where the UK, Germany, and the US dominate and over half of studies do not even specify a national context. Even studies that seem generic in scope, like [[Hourigan2026-oc]]'s digital-diary study of "everyday" misinformation in Australia, implicitly critique the field's default topical and geographic anchors — showing that ordinary users flag business/economics and celebrity content, not just the health and politics stories that dominate the literature. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s international expert panel is itself a partial corrective to this same imbalance, though its panel composition and blind spots (missing generative AI) illustrate how hard genuinely anticipatory, globally inclusive foresight is to achieve.

## From cognitive proxies to real-world behaviour

A second major thread concerns the field's overreliance on lab-friendly cognitive proxies (belief change, sharing intentions) rather than downstream, real-world outcomes — a gap [[Spampatti2026-kx]] quantifies starkly: fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure high-impact behavioural outcomes. Several papers supply exactly the kind of ecological evidence this critique calls for, with mixed results. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] both use platform-scale trace data to show that exposure to untrustworthy or low-credibility content is low on average but highly concentrated among a small subset of users — and that reducing such exposure produces no detectable attitudinal shift, a sobering counterpoint to alarmist narratives. [[Oswald2025-km]] offers a structural explanation for why visible online discourse might mislead both citizens and researchers: a small minority of highly active users produce most political content, so what looks like "public opinion" online is really the tip of an iceberg. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] complicates the exposure-measurement problem further, showing that the field's dominant "direct referrer" paradigm for counting platform-to-misinformation traffic dramatically underestimates true platform effects once indirect and habitual pathways are modeled counterfactually — a methodological correction with real policy stakes, since platform bans would disproportionately reduce quality news alongside misinformation. Against this backdrop of concentrated, elusive exposure, [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] is notable for actually closing the causal gap between online speech and offline harm, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to a causally estimated toll of vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths — a rare example of the field reaching the causal, mechanistic evidence that critiques like [[Spampatti2026-kx]] and [[van-Eck2026-xg]] say is otherwise missing. Meanwhile [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Voelkel2026-lc]] push in the applied direction, testing whether interventions (prebunking ads, climate messages) that work in the lab retain their punch at scale — with prebunking surviving the transition to an Instagram feed, and climate messaging megastudies revealing that widely-cited strategies mostly produce small effects on attitudes and essentially none on costly behaviour like donations.

## Measurement artifacts and conceptual inconsistency

Several papers turn the field's tools on itself, showing how much of its apparent disagreement is manufactured by inconsistent measurement rather than by conflicting underlying reality. [[Hartmann2025-px]] makes this the explicit thesis for echo-chamber research, arguing that a 129-study systematic review shows divergent conceptualizations and operationalizations — not divergent phenomena — as the main source of contradictory findings. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates the same point quantitatively for partisan-media/polarization research, running a specification-curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media slant and polarization and finding that method choice swings coefficient magnitude by nearly sevenfold, even though direction rarely flips. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of the NewsGuard database that much source-based misinformation research quietly depends on, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can be destabilized by a single source crossing a threshold, while continuous scores are comparatively robust — a cautionary tale for any study built on source-level labels. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends the critique to the normative register, showing that depolarization recommendations in the literature are largely disconnected from the very evidence the studies produce, resting instead on implicit ideals of deliberative democracy. [[Lieu2025-nl]] complicates a popular assumption within climate-misinformation research specifically, finding that logical-fallacy framing (FLICC) does little to shift perceived veracity relative to the underlying rhetorical content (CARDS), suggesting some influential taxonomies may be doing less analytic work than assumed. [[Sadler2025-vu]] goes further still, arguing that the field's core vocabulary — "narrative," "true/false" — is conceptually naïve, and proposes a hermeneutic-realist alternative capable of registering content that is ethically problematic without being straightforwardly false. [[Pante2025-pq]] shows the same self-correcting instinct applied to influence-operations research, re-examining claims of inter-state coordination on Twitter/X with modern detection methods and control datasets and finding earlier claims did not hold up. And [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] uncovers a purely infrastructural artifact — the 100-shares anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset — that has been silently distorting cross-country and longitudinal comparisons built on that dataset.

## Industry capture and the political economy of platform research

A distinct but related set of papers argues that the field's evidentiary problems are not just conceptual but structural, rooted in dependence on the very companies being studied. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] documents this empirically: about half of high-profile social-media research in top journals has disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small set of repeat collaborators who also populate editorial and review roles — yielding an estimated ~80% "industrial saturation" of the corpus and a topical skew toward user-focused questions (like misinformation sharing) over platform-scale dynamics. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] generalizes this into a full framework, drawing explicit parallels to tobacco, fossil-fuel, and asbestos science to catalog mechanisms — suppression, selective funding, design bias, performative collaboration — through which industry shapes the evidence base, and argues existing safeguards like preregistration can paradoxically penalize independent researchers relative to industry-affiliated ones. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the same dependency structurally: because platforms alone control the data needed to study them, the field is uniquely vulnerable compared to fields like pharmaceuticals where independent data generation is possible, and calls for both DSA-style regulatory fixes and a "research on research" ethic. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers perhaps the sharpest single case study, dissecting the Meta2020 partnership to argue that even the best-resourced, most careful collaborative science cannot outpace platform change — its findings lack "temporal validity" and even "poetic validity" (inconsistent operationalization of concepts like echo chamber across papers in the same project) — concluding that empirical social science is structurally unable to hold platforms accountable reactively, and that regulation must instead require platforms to self-characterize their effects. [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] examine the regulatory response itself: the former shows that "data quality" was nearly absent from official DSA texts until academic and NGO stakeholders fought it into the Delegated Regulation (against platform resistance, including Meta's strategic inversion of the argument), while the latter uses DSA transparency data to reveal systemic underinvestment in non-English, Global-South-language content moderation. Two papers extend this political-economy lens to the adjacent AI research ecosystem: [[Vertesi2026-lv]] argues that critiques of AI (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory framings) function as "decoys" that distract accountability researchers from the underlying capital and infrastructure assembly driving AI, while [[Dodds2026-df]] shows how AI hype operates as a structuring force within journalism itself, redistributing legitimacy and resources before technologies even materialize — both offering the misinformation field a mirror for how its own object of study can co-opt its critics.

## Data infrastructure in the post-API age

Closely related is a cluster diagnosing how the collapse of open platform data access is reshaping — and constraining — what the field can even ask. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the scale of loss: after Twitter/X's 2023 API paywall, publications using Twitter data stagnated and then fell 13% in 2024, representing a measurable loss of empirical capacity to study public discourse after nearly two decades of a "Golden Age." [[Yang2026-tq]] situates this within a broader pattern, showing social-media-based social science tripled from 2010 before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024, with research usage badly misaligned with actual platform popularity (Twitter/X overrepresented, YouTube underrepresented). [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Zheng2026-bi]] show that even where access nominally persists, the tools themselves are unreliable: YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," with findable videos decaying sharply within weeks of publication, undermining both historical research and DSA systemic-risk assessments, while [[Zheng2026-bi]]'s TubeStats/TokStats infrastructure is offered as a corrective, using URL-space random sampling to escape the popularity bias inherent in keyword- or API-based samples. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the normative conclusion from this same regulatory moment, arguing that the DSA era demands the field pivot from narrow causal-effects research on individuals toward treating platform collective behaviour as democratic infrastructure in its own right.

## Borrowed lessons and new methodological horizons

Finally, several papers look outward — to other fields, to participatory methods, or to AI tools — for ways to repair the diagnosed weaknesses. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] remains the emblematic case, importing six lessons from climate psychology's more mature engagement with organised denial. [[Matias2025-px]] proposes participatory, lived-experience-driven evaluation as a way to strengthen — not merely legitimate — the science of AI systems, with case studies (the Allegheny Family Screening Tool reanalysis, Chicago police-complaint relabeling) showing how community involvement surfaces validity problems invisible to standard technical evaluation. [[Gillespie2026-aa]] applies a similar sociotechnical lens to AI red-teaming, arguing the field is repeating the under-examined labor and value-setting problems that plagued early content moderation, and calling for a coordinated empirical research network before the practice's opaque, market-driven form hardens into permanence. [[Ramos2026-qo]] offers a comparably concrete methodological extension within misinformation response itself, proposing "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" as complements to claim-by-claim fact-checking, illustrated through the 2024 Valencia floods conspiracy. On the computational-methods front, [[Balluff2026-if]] cautions against unreflective adoption of LLMs in communication research, citing reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs, while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] provides a striking counterpoint, benchmarking AI coding agents' surprising success (and specific failure modes, including sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting) at reproducing published social-science findings — itself a new instrument for auditing the field's own replicability. Taken together with the tangential but structurally similar case of Anthropic's usage-survey report on the economics of AI ([[UnknownUnknown-db]]), these papers suggest the self-critical, meta-scientific instinct driving this whole literature — interrogate your proxies, your data infrastructure, your industry entanglements, your borrowed metaphors — is now being turned, in real time, on the AI tools the field is beginning to adopt as both subject and method.
