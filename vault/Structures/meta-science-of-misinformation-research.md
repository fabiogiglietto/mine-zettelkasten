# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns the Lens on Itself

A distinct genre has emerged within misinformation and online-harms scholarship: work that stops producing new empirical estimates and instead interrogates the evidentiary basis, geographic scope, and institutional conditions of the field itself. Read together, these papers converge on a startling diagnosis — much of what the public and even many researchers believe about misinformation's prevalence, causes, and cures rests on a thin, skewed, and industry-entangled evidentiary base. [[Budak2024-ef]] states this most bluntly, arguing that public discourse has decoupled from the empirical record on three fronts: how much problematic content people actually see, how much algorithms drive that exposure, and how strongly social media causes broader societal harms. [[Allen2020-nj]] supplies the ecosystem-level correction, showing that "fake news" occupies roughly 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet and that television, not social media, dominates news consumption — a scale mismatch with the field's overwhelming research attention to online fakery. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] extend this "concentration" finding into health and 2020-election contexts respectively: exposure to low-credibility content is real but heavily concentrated among small subpopulations, and experimentally reducing exposure does not move attitudes — a sobering empirical counterweight to alarmist narratives. [[Oswald2025-km]] generalizes the mechanism into a "production-consumption gap": a small hyperactive minority generates the visible discourse that both citizens and researchers mistake for public opinion.

## WEIRD-Centrism and the Missing World

A second recurring critique concerns geographic and cultural narrowness. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around the WEIRD bias in misinformation research, and [[Humprecht2020-gd]] operationalizes cross-national resilience empirically, showing the U.S. to be a genuine outlier rather than a representative case — a finding with methodological teeth for any study generalizing from American data. [[Nenno2025-xa]] tests whether Western news-values theory travels, finding that WEIRD/non-WEIRD differences in flagged misinformation are statistically real but modest, with the U.S. and Brazil behaving as distinct outlier clusters. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel argument for polarization research: fragmented across disciplines and concentrated on a handful of (mostly Western) countries. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 international experts is itself an attempt to counteract this narrowness through deliberate cross-national, multi-stakeholder elicitation, concluding that no single-level fix — individual, platform, or institutional — will suffice. [[Mota2026-sl59]] pushes the geographic critique into an adjacent domain (AI discourse), documenting systematically divergent Global North/South framings that mirror the asymmetries the misinformation field is only beginning to reckon with.

## Cognitive Proxies, Behavioral Reality, and the Climate Analogy

A third thread questions whether the field's dominant psychological paradigm — belief change, discernment, debiasing — actually captures what matters. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] represent the cognitive-science core this newer literature is reacting against: careful, robust accounts of truth discernment and the continued-influence effect, alongside [[Kunda1990-cg]]'s foundational motivated-reasoning framework. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] directly challenges this paradigm's centrality by importing lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world, high-impact behavioral outcomes, and urging the field to integrate motivational, emotional, and systems-level analysis rather than relying on cognitive proxies alone. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy is an instructive companion case: even well-replicated persuasion strategies move attitudes only marginally and fail to shift costly behavior (donations), illustrating exactly the attitude-behavior gap Spampatti et al. warn misinformation research to anticipate. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] offers a complementary structural critique, arguing that the field's guiding metaphors (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each illuminate only one level of a multilevel complex system and that portfolio-style, complexity-aware interventions — again borrowed implicitly from climate policy — are needed rather than single-lever fixes. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research echoes this: robust causal and longitudinal evidence remains scarce, proxies dominate, and the online/offline binary that structured a generation of work is no longer conceptually tenable. [[Marwick2025-vx]] similarly dismantles a popular causal shorthand — "exposure equals belief" — showing empirically that redpilling is usually gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion, a finding that should recalibrate how radicalization and disinformation exposure are modeled.

## Data Infrastructure as Epistemic Constraint

A large cluster of these reflections concerns not theory but infrastructure: what data are available, who controls it, and how that shapes what can be known. [[Bruns2019-nr]] names the founding trauma — the post-Cambridge-Analytica "APIcalypse" — arguing that platform data restrictions function to frustrate independent scrutiny rather than protect privacy. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the fallout bibliometrically, documenting a 13% decline in Twitter-based research after 2023 API paywalling, while [[Yang2026-tq]] shows the broader pattern: social-media-based social science tripled and then plateaued, concentrated overwhelmingly and disproportionately on Twitter/X and Facebook relative to actual platform use. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both dissect a specific artifact — the 100-public-shares censoring threshold in Meta's Social Science One dataset — showing it introduces platform-wide distortions (a phantom pre-COVID convergence across 46 countries; a 4x overestimate of fake-news prevalence) that could easily be mistaken for substantive findings. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit on NewsGuard, the workhorse source-trustworthiness database, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications (rather than continuous scores) can flip conclusions depending on which few sources cross threshold. [[Rieder2025-ju]] extends the audit tradition to YouTube's search API, finding it "forgetful by design" with severe temporal decay that undermines DSA-mandated systemic-risk research, while [[Zheng2026-bi]] proposes a partial fix — genuinely random sampling tools (TubeStats/TokStats) to escape popularity-biased opportunistic samples. [[Pante2025-pq]] shows the stakes of weak methodology concretely, re-examining and undercutting prior claims of coordinated inter-state information operations once modern detection methods and control datasets are applied. Collectively these papers argue that a great deal of the field's empirical edifice sits atop under-scrutinized, industry-controlled, and artifact-prone data infrastructure — a concern given new institutional weight by the EU's Digital Services Act, discussed critically in [[Peters2026-mo]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Bechmann2026-dr]], all of which see Article 40 data-access rights as necessary but currently hampered by legal, technical, and power asymmetries between platforms and researchers.

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Collaboration

Closely related is a cluster diagnosing the field's uncomfortable dependency on the very companies it studies. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together mount the most systematic version of this argument, drawing explicit parallels to tobacco, pharmaceutical, and fossil-fuel science: roughly half of high-profile social-media research carries disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged cohort of authors, editors, and reviewers, with an estimated "industrial saturation" near 80% once all roles are combined. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership complements this from the inside, arguing that even the most ambitious industry-academic collaboration produced narrow, low-external-validity estimates, incoherent operationalizations of core concepts like "echo chamber," and a design structurally biased toward null findings — conveniently aligned with platform interests. [[Baym2026-tr]] widens the lens temporally, revisiting a decade-old critique of platform power to argue that critique alone has proven insufficient against accelerating wealth concentration and harm, urging researchers toward interdisciplinary, cross-sector engagement rather than diagnosis-for-its-own-sake. [[Balluff2026-if]] applies a structurally similar corporate-dependency critique to the field's newest tool, LLMs, warning against unreflective adoption that imports opaque, corporately-controlled infrastructure into methodology itself — a concern sharpened by [[Alizadeh2026-es]]'s demonstration that AI coding agents can now reproduce most social-science findings, raising new questions about what "reproduction" even certifies when the reproducer is itself an opaque commercial model.

## Toward Repair: Methodological and Normative Correctives

A final set of contributions moves from diagnosis to remedy. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows that even well-evidenced fields like polarization research routinely issue depolarization recommendations untethered from their own findings, arguing for explicit separation of evidence from normative (typically deliberative-democratic) commitments — a transparency norm this whole topic cluster implicitly calls for more broadly. [[Hartmann2025-px]] performs the analogous service for echo-chamber research, showing that much apparent empirical disagreement is an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than a real phenomenon in dispute. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] argues the field has been too topic- and definition-driven from the top down, missing how ordinary users actually encounter and label misinformation in everyday, non-"hot-button" domains. [[Matias2025-px]] proposes participatory, lived-experience-involving methods as a way to strengthen (not merely legitimate) the science of algorithmic and AI evaluation, offering a template with clear application to misinformation research's own reliance on distant, top-down expert framing. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] adds a journalism-side corrective, suggesting mainstream reporting practices themselves may inadvertently manufacture new conspiracy beliefs, complicating any account that treats media coverage as purely corrective — a point [[Tsfati2020-uo]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]] anticipate in their earlier calls for interdisciplinary, platform-collaborative "science of fake news" building. Read as a whole, this literature does not abandon the empirical project of studying misinformation and online harm; it insists that the project's credibility now depends on confronting its own WEIRD-centrism, cognitive-proxy overreliance, infrastructural dependency, and industry entanglement with the same rigor it has long directed at its ostensible subject.
