# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## Naming the field's blind spots

A recurring move in this literature is diagnostic self-critique: scholars stepping outside their own experimental designs to ask what the field as a whole has been measuring, and what it has missed. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] crystallizes this move by importing six lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology, arguing that misinformation research over-indexes on cognitive proxies (beliefs, sharing intentions) rather than real-world behavior, under-attends to cross-country heterogeneity, and too rarely integrates individual- and system-level analysis. This diagnosis rhymes with [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue framing of the field as WEIRD-centric, with [[Falkenberg2026-ka]]'s parallel complaint about polarization research being disciplinarily fragmented and geographically narrow, and with [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s argument that dominant metaphors—infodemic, information warfare, information pollution—each illuminate only one level of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive phenomenon. [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a specific mechanism for why WEIRD, platform-centric findings mislead: a small, unrepresentative minority of highly active users produces most visible political content, distorting inference for citizens and researchers alike. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] and [[Munger2025-cz]] push this further, arguing that the causal-effects paradigm borrowed from psychology is structurally unsuited to platforms that change faster than science can characterize them—Munger's dissection of the Meta2020 partnership shows how methodologically ambitious, consensus-driven research can still yield underpowered, conceptually incoherent, and rapidly stale findings. Scoping reviews reinforce the pattern empirically: [[Rothut2026-wt]] finds 25 years of online-radicalization research still short on longitudinal and causal evidence and skewed toward a handful of Western countries; [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows depolarization recommendations are almost never grounded in the evidence the same studies produce, resting instead on implicit deliberative-democracy norms; [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows echo-chamber "disagreement" is substantially a conceptual and operational artifact rather than purely empirical variation; and the expert Delphi study of [[Mahl2026-hc]] finds the field itself judged fragmented, present-focused, and overly reliant on individual-level fixes like fact-checking and literacy. [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] sit in productive tension here, the former arguing public discourse overstates algorithmic and exposure harms relative to evidence, the latter offering a rare causally-grounded model tying antivaccine speech to measurable epidemic outcomes—together illustrating how contested "how much does misinformation actually matter" remains even among empirically minded researchers. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] extends the critique methodologically, showing that when researchers stop imposing "hot-button" topic definitions and let users flag what they find misleading in everyday life, misinformation looks far more mundane (business, celebrity, clickbait headlines) than the topic-driven literature assumes.

## The platform-dependency problem

A second cluster interrogates the field's structural dependence on the very companies it studies. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical audit [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, in the tradition of tobacco and pharmaceutical agnotology, that roughly half of high-profile social-media research has undisclosed industry ties, that this research is disproportionately cited and attended to, and that industry-tied authors, editors, and reviewers together produce an "industrial saturation" of the corpus near 80%. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this structurally: unlike food or pharma science, platform research cannot generate independent data, making the field uniquely vulnerable to selective funding and subtle influence—a concern [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Peters2026-mo]] trace directly into the EU's Digital Services Act, where "independence by permission" research models, contested notions of data quality, and platform foot-dragging threaten Article 40's promise. The empirical costs of this dependency are made concrete by the bibliometric eulogies of [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya, which chart the rise and abrupt post-2022 collapse of Twitter-based scholarship, and by [[Yang2026-tq]], which shows social-media research overall has plateaued after tripling, with platform choice (still dominated by Twitter/X and Facebook) badly misaligned with actual public usage. [[Wang2026-ub]] shows the human cost of this infrastructural rupture in miniature: even a highly motivated academic community failed to durably migrate to Mastodon. [[Cullen2026-cb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] extend the critique to the successor regime of platform-hosted "clean rooms," arguing that tools like CrowdTangle and the Meta Content Library don't merely restrict access but actively shape what can be seen, asked, and concluded—privileging code-fluent, well-resourced, English-language researchers and excluding qualitative, longitudinal, and cross-platform work. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes this as a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data almost entirely absent. [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] offers a more optimistic counter-read of the same turbulence, arguing that platform resistance to Article 40 is evidence the DSA is finally constraining real power, not proof of its failure. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] supplies a cautionary methodological footnote to this whole conversation: even ostensibly privacy-preserving dataset designs (Meta's 100-share threshold) can introduce artifacts that masquerade as substantive cross-national findings. [[Tonneau2025-bv]] extends the equity critique from data access to moderation labor itself, documenting that Global South languages receive a fraction of the human moderation resources English does—a structural blind spot with the same WEIRD-centric signature diagnosed elsewhere. [[Vincent_undated-re]] exemplifies the kind of repeated, comparable platform monitoring this literature calls for, tracking rising TikTok mis/disinformation prevalence across successive measurement rounds.

## Methodological reckonings: what are we actually measuring?

A third strand turns inward on measurement validity itself. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard, the field's most widely used source-trust database, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can be swung by single sources crossing a threshold, urging continuous scores instead. [[Schemer2026-mh]] performs an analogous audit via specification-curve analysis across 504 operationalizations of media partisanship and polarization, finding that measurement choices—not just underlying reality—drive much of the field's inconsistent results. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] extends the same logic to sampling: opt-in online panels vary enormously in representativeness and response validity, with consequences specifically for detecting heterogeneous (as opposed to main) treatment effects. [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Brown2025-jk]] bring this scrutiny to the field's newest tool, large language models, cautioning against unreflective adoption for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulation while showing that LLM annotation bias is dataset-specific and dwarfed by item difficulty—useful nuance against blanket claims of LLM unreliability. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] complicates the reproducibility conversation further, showing AI coding agents can now reproduce most published social-science findings, but also that they are sycophantically biased toward "success" under confirmatory prompting, raising new epistemic risks for a field increasingly reliant on automated pipelines. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] reframes a foundational measurement assumption directly: the field's dominant "direct referrer" paradigm for estimating platforms' misinformation contribution undercounts real effects by 1.5–15×, though correcting for this also complicates simple platform-ban policy proposals since removing platforms would disproportionately cut high-quality news too.

## Beyond true/false: narrative, framing, and the limits of the fact-checking paradigm

A distinct but overlapping conversation questions whether the field's foundational unit of analysis—the discrete false claim—is even the right object of study. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] argues, via second-order cybernetics, that creator-intent-based typologies of "fake news" cannot capture how falsehood actually circulates through propagators in a hybrid media system. [[Marwick2026-qd]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Starbird2025-jj]], and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] converge, from different theoretical angles (cultural narrative, narratology/hermeneutics, data-frame sensemaking, deep-story performance), on the claim that disinformation is better understood as identity-affirming, cathartic, collaboratively performed narrative than as isolated false facts—a reframing with real stakes, since [[Goel2025-iq]] shows factually true mainstream articles are routinely repurposed to support misleading narratives, evading source-level detection entirely, and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] finds belief tracks cumulative narrative exposure rather than discrete claims. [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] extend the reframing to coordination and journalism respectively, the former arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" conflates inauthenticity with manipulative intent, the latter examining how journalistic strategies themselves can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] show the "fake news" label itself has become a rhetorical weapon for delegitimization rather than a descriptor of falsity, while [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues generative AI outputs require an entirely new epistemic category ("emergent facts") beyond evidence-based, interpretive, or rule-based notions of truth. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] apply this narrative sensibility to radicalization specifically, showing ConspiracyTok's "generous epistemology" and "redpilling" are gradual, socially embedded processes rather than sudden exposure effects—directly complicating simple exposure-equals-belief models still common elsewhere in the field. [[Wack2026-bt]] shows the same collective-sensemaking logic applies to deepfake reception in Kenya, where audience commentary, not the artifact alone, determines persuasive impact.

## Borrowed lessons from more mature or adjacent fields

Several papers explicitly import methodology or hard-won lessons from outside misinformation research. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] anchors this move, but [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages operationalizes exactly the rigor it recommends—finding small, largely non-heterogeneous effects on attitudes and no effect on costly behavior, a sobering template for misinformation-intervention research generally. [[Lieu2025-nl]] similarly imports the CARDS/FLICC taxonomies from climate-denial scholarship to show that misinformation *content* (attacking climate solutions) matters more for polarization than the logical *fallacy* used to deliver it. [[Matias2025-px]] draws a different lesson from participatory science and medical-ethics equipoise, arguing lived-experience public involvement is not a political concession but can materially improve the rigor of AI evaluation. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] closes the loop with a call to action explicitly modeled on science-for-diplomacy, urging researchers studying division to test and deploy depolarization interventions rather than only theorize them.

## Toward global and comparative accounts

A cluster of empirical papers exists partly to demonstrate—rather than merely assert—the WEIRD-centrism critique, by taking the field's questions outside the US/UK/Germany axis. [[Nenno2025-xa]] tests news-values theory across 24 WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries and finds small but real cross-cultural differences the literature's Western-built typologies miss. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] uses 26-country parliamentary data to show misinformation sharing is driven specifically by radical-right populism rather than populism or right-wing ideology alone. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extend cross-national work to European elections, the latter identifying a "hostile misinformation effect" replicating across Germany, the Netherlands, and Poland. [[Adam2026-tz]], [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Ventura2025-sw]], and [[Wack2026-bt]] push into Switzerland/Germany, Brazil, and Kenya respectively, while [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] widen the aperture further still to comparative GenAI propaganda across six democratically weakened states and Global North/South AI media framing—collectively making the case, empirically rather than programmatically, for the comparative turn the field's diagnostic papers demand.

## From critique to accountability

Finally, several pieces convert diagnosis into demands for action. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]], [[Vertesi2026-lv]], [[Baym2026-tr]], and [[Donovan2025-ws]] each argue, in different registers, that platforms and their political economies—not individual cognitive failure—are the proper locus of accountability, with Vertesi warning that even critical AI scholarship risks being captured by "decoy" debates that stabilize rather than challenge platform power. Applied intervention research supplies a partial answer at the individual level even as the structural critique proceeds: [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] shows prebunking can scale cheaply within Instagram's ad infrastructure, [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Costello2024-bg]] show LLM-mediated dialogue can durably shift belief, [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues fact-checking must be supplemented by narrative monitoring and argument-checking during crises, and [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how fact-checkers themselves rhetorically manage the very platform dependencies this whole literature interrogates. [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Choi2026-bz]], [[Renault2025-uh]], and [[Hameleers2026-mc]] round out the picture with sober correctives to alarmism—about synthetic-media panic, deepfake exceptionalism, and partisan asymmetry under crowdsourced moderation—while [[Lyons2026-ca]] reminds the field that even its favored older-adult vulnerability findings may not generalize cleanly from political to health misinformation. Read together, these eighty-odd papers do not converge on a single verdict about misinformation's severity or mechanism; their real achievement is convergence on the diagnosis that the field cannot yet answer that question rigorously, and on a shared, if contested, roadmap—broader geography, better measurement, less platform capture, and richer conceptual vocabulary—for getting there.
