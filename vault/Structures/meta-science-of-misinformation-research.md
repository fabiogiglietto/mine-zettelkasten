# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns Inward

A striking feature of recent misinformation and online-harms scholarship is its growing willingness to interrogate itself. Rather than producing another empirical study of belief or sharing, a cluster of papers steps back to ask whether the field is asking the right questions, measuring the right things, and drawing on the right cases. This meta-turn is partly a maturation signal — a sign that the field has accumulated enough evidence to detect its own blind spots — and partly a response to mounting institutional pressures: eroding data access, industry entanglement, generative AI, and the political weaponization of "misinformation" itself.

## Borrowed Maturity: Learning from Other Fields

Several contributions explicitly import lessons from more established literatures. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] argues that climate psychology, having weathered decades of organized denial, offers six transferable insights: broaden beyond cognitive proxies to real-world behavior, integrate individual and systems-level analysis, attend to cross-country heterogeneity, and organize researchers collectively against coordinated attacks on the science. This systems-thinking impulse resonates with [[Frischlich2025-vn]], which shows how the field's dominant metaphors — virus, warfare, pollution — each illuminate only one layer of a multilevel complex system, and calls for portfolio-style interventions akin to climate policy's mix of mitigation strategies. Both papers converge on a diagnosis: misinformation research has been too linear and too fixated on individual cognition, missing the social, platform, and institutional levels where interventions might matter more.

## Diagnosing WEIRD-Centrism and Evidentiary Gaps

A second strand catalogs where the empirical base itself is thin or skewed. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's WEIRD bias, a concern echoed empirically by [[Nenno2025-xa]], whose 24-country analysis of news values in flagged misinformation finds that US and Brazilian patterns are outliers rather than the global norm, and by [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], which makes the parallel argument for polarization research's overreliance on US data. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] complicates simple narratives further, showing across nearly half a century of survey data that the "golden age of trust" is a myth, that decline is not universal, and that authoritarian contexts show rising trust — a sobering reminder that intuitive Western narratives don't travel. Similarly, [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research finds robust causal and longitudinal evidence scarce, geographic focus skewed to the UK, Germany, and US, and definitional consensus lacking. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] extends this critique methodologically, showing that when researchers stop imposing pre-set definitions of misinformation, everyday concerns look different from the "hot button" topics the literature usually studies — business, economics, and clickbait-driven mainstream news loom larger than fringe conspiracy content.

Methodological self-scrutiny also targets measurement itself. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows that disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent operationalization rather than divergent reality — a diagnosis paralleled by [[Schemer2026-mh]]'s specification-curve demonstration that how one measures partisan media slant and polarization dramatically shifts effect sizes, and by [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard, which finds binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications distort findings in ways continuous scores avoid. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends this scrutiny to the policy end of the pipeline, showing that depolarization recommendations in the literature are rarely grounded in the very evidence being reviewed, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy.

## Contesting the Alarm: Effects, Proxies, and Public Narrative

A related cluster questions whether the field's alarmist framing matches the evidence. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues public discourse has diverged from empirical findings on exposure prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. [[Oswald2025-km]] shows how the "production-consumption gap" — a small minority generating most visible content — distorts inferences for citizens and researchers alike, since visible discourse is not representative of the silent majority. [[Bennett2025-xs]] pushes back against purely "technocentric," individual-cognition-focused explanations of democratic backsliding, arguing that institutionalist analysis of digital surrogate organizations is needed to explain phenomena like MAGA radicalization — a reminder that overreliance on cognitive proxies can obscure organizational and institutional dynamics. [[Sadler2025-vu]] similarly critiques the field's loose, intuitive use of "narrative," proposing a hermeneutic realist framework that moves beyond simple true/false binaries. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] adds a media-effects angle, suggesting journalistic strategies themselves can inadvertently seed conspiracy belief formation, not merely diffuse it.

## Industry Entanglement and the Politics of Evidence

A distinct but overlapping set of papers turns to the political economy of knowledge production itself. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, respectively theoretically and empirically, how platform control over data and funding creates industry influence analogous to tobacco and pharmaceutical science — finding roughly half of high-profile social media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeat collaborators who also populate editorial boards. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a structural problem unique to platform research (since, unlike food or pharma, independent data generation is impossible) and calls for DSA-style mandated access alongside stronger research-on-research norms. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a granular case study of these dynamics via the Meta2020 partnership, arguing that even the most ambitious collaborative field experiments suffer from low "temporal" and "poetic" validity, concluding that reactive academic science cannot keep pace with platforms and that proactive regulation is required instead. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] broadens the lens to AI accountability research generally, warning that critiques fixating on technical "decoys" (safety, inevitability, disruption) inadvertently legitimize the underlying political economy of AI rather than contesting it. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] proposes a constructive reorientation in the same spirit, arguing that causal-effects paradigms should give way to analyses of platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, particularly as DSA-era data access regimes open new possibilities.

## New Frontiers: AI, Fact, and Method

Generative AI raises fresh epistemological and methodological quandaries. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] argues traditional fact-checking categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) cannot capture AI-generated "emergent facts," proposing new indicators for evaluation. [[Balluff2026-if]] critiques uncritical LLM adoption in communication research itself, cataloging reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs, while [[Jayaram2026-wd]] previews how AI tools might reshape scientific review itself — a reflexive coda on how the research infrastructure studying misinformation may itself be transformed by the technologies it studies.

## Toward Coordinated, Anticipatory Inquiry

Several pieces conclude with calls to action rather than mere diagnosis. The Delphi study in [[Mahl2026-hc]] operationalizes anticipatory, multi-stakeholder thinking, finding experts favor platform governance and journalism-ecosystem interventions over individual-level fixes — and noting, tellingly, that generative AI's disruptive potential went entirely unanticipated in 2022. [[Ramos2026-qo]] proposes narrative monitoring and argument-checking as necessary complements to fact-checking during crises, illustrating how field practitioners are independently arriving at the same complexity-aware conclusions as academic theorists. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] and [[Baym2026-tr]] bookend this collection with calls for researcher responsibility: the former urging scientists to actively design and test depolarization interventions rather than simply diagnosing division, the latter reflecting on a decade of critical platform scholarship that, despite its rigor, has failed to blunt intensifying harms — and urging a shift from critique toward engaged, cross-sector collaboration.

Taken together, these papers trace an arc from diagnosis to prescription: identifying WEIRD-centrism, proxy-overreliance, industry capture, and conceptual looseness as structural weaknesses, and increasingly proposing borrowed frameworks, methodological transparency, and coordinated, anticipatory action as the field's necessary next steps.
