# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns the Mirror on Itself

The papers gathered here mark a distinctive moment in misinformation studies: rather than producing new empirical findings about who believes what and why, they step back and ask whether the field's accumulated evidence base is trustworthy, generalizable, and asking the right questions at all. Three broad diagnostic threads run through this literature — geographic and demographic narrowness, an overreliance on individual-level cognitive proxies at the expense of causal and systemic evidence, and structural entanglements (with platforms, funders, and hype cycles) that may be silently shaping what gets studied and published.

### The WEIRD Problem and the Limits of Generalization

A recurring complaint is that misinformation research has been built almost entirely on WEIRD populations and a handful of platforms. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around this critique, while [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows empirically that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation diverge meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than exemplars. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel argument for polarization research, and [[Bennett2025-xs]] argues that technocentric, individual-level accounts of democratic backsliding have crowded out institutional analysis, obscuring how networked "digital surrogate organizations" actually radicalize parties. Even methodological infrastructure is WEIRD-skewed: [[Lukito2026-nb]] documents that election-research data-access tools are overwhelmingly built for and by Western researchers, and [[Yang2026-tq]] finds that social-science reliance on Twitter/X and Facebook data has been wildly disproportionate to those platforms' actual global usage. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of international experts likewise diagnoses fragmentation and Western-centrism as core field-level failures, alongside a persistent absence of anticipatory, cross-stakeholder thinking.

### Borrowed Lessons and Alternative Framings

Several papers explicitly import frameworks from adjacent fields to correct misinformation research's blind spots. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the clearest exemplar, distilling six lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology — chiefly that behavioral (not merely cognitive) outcomes, cross-national heterogeneity, systems thinking, and organized researcher collectives are underdeveloped in misinformation science. This complements [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy demonstrating that even the most-cited climate messages produce only small attitudinal effects and no behavioral change — a sobering empirical instance of the very overreliance on proxy outcomes that Spampatti et al. warn against, and echoed in [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s finding that logical-fallacy framing (FLICC) matters less than message content (CARDS) for perceived veracity. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] proposes that no single root metaphor — virus, warfare, pollution — captures misinformation's multilevel complexity, arguing for portfolio-style interventions modeled on climate policy's own multi-level approach. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] extend this diversification impulse to depolarization and disinformation-countering practice respectively: the former calling for tested, proximate framings rather than assumed cognitive fixes, the latter arguing fact-checking alone cannot neutralize evolving, emotionally resonant narratives and must be supplemented by narrative- and argument-level monitoring.

### The Evidence-to-Recommendation Gap

A second cluster interrogates the disconnect between what research actually shows and what gets recommended or publicly believed. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] documents this gap directly for polarization: the overwhelming majority of depolarization recommendations in published papers are not grounded in the papers' own evidence but in unexamined normative commitments (chiefly deliberative-democratic ideals). [[Budak2024-ef]] makes the analogous claim for public discourse on misinformation broadly, arguing that journalists and commentators routinely overstate exposure prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm relative to the empirical record — a claim substantiated by several papers in this set showing exposure to low-credibility content is real but highly concentrated in small subpopulations rather than diffuse (Lyons2026-jt, [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], [[Oswald2025-km]]'s "production-consumption gap"). [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complicates the picture further by showing that when researchers stop imposing top-down definitions and instead ask people what they consider misinformation in daily life, the picture shifts toward mainstream/alternative news and mundane topics like personal finance rather than the "hot button" political and health domains that dominate the literature.

### Measurement, Method, and the Fragility of Findings

A substantial contingent interrogates whether the field's operationalizations are even measuring what they claim to. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that much of the field's apparent disagreement is an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and measurement rather than genuine empirical conflict — a diagnosis echoed methodologically by [[Schemer2026-mh]], whose specification-curve analysis across 504 model variants shows that choice of media-partisanship measure alone explains the majority of variance in reported polarization effects. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard ratings similarly warns that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip substantive conclusions depending on database snapshot. Data infrastructure itself comes under scrutiny: [[Rieder2025-ju]] shows YouTube's search API is "forgetful by design," systematically undercounting older content in ways that undermine DSA-mandated systemic-risk research; [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] finds that Meta's own anonymization threshold introduces a platform-wide artifact that can masquerade as cross-national social signal. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argues the field's dominant "direct referrer" paradigm for measuring platform-driven misinformation exposure dramatically undercounts true effects by ignoring indirect and delayed pathways, while [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] identifies a novel, replicable bias — the "hostile misinformation effect" — suggesting that even researchers' dependent variables (perceived targeting) are politically motivated constructs requiring careful theorization.

### Causal Ambition and Its Discontents

Several papers push toward more rigorous causal designs while others question whether such designs can ever keep pace with their object of study. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] exemplify ambitious causal work — linking Twitter antivaccine content to epidemic outcomes and elite-visibility reduction to misinformation susceptibility, respectively — demonstrating what rigorous, mechanism-focused misinformation science can look like. Yet [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership argues that even the most well-resourced, methodologically sophisticated collaboration in the field's history struggled with "temporal validity" and conceptual incoherence, concluding that platforms change faster than academic science can characterize them — a structural problem, not merely a design flaw. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the institutional conclusion, arguing the field should reorient from individual causal effects toward platform collective behavior as "democratic infrastructure," particularly under the DSA's new data regimes.

### Industry Entanglement as a Meta-Problem

A distinct but related cluster treats the field's dependency on platform and corporate data as itself a threat to scientific validity. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extend the tobacco/pharma/fossil-fuel analogy directly to tech, empirically showing that roughly half of high-profile social-media research carries undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeat-collaborating scholars, editors, and reviewers, with topical bias toward user-level (rather than platform-level) questions. [[Heiss2026-qv]] proposes parallel regulatory and norm-based remedies. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes this critique, arguing that even well-meaning accountability research can function as a "decoy" that legitimizes the underlying political economy of AI rather than challenging it. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a constructive counterpoint, arguing that participatory, lived-experience-informed evaluation can strengthen rather than merely democratize the science of AI systems.

### New Objects, New Tools, New Uncertainties

Finally, several papers grapple with how generative AI and LLMs are reshaping both the phenomena under study and the tools used to study them. [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Dierickx2026-tw]] offer companion critiques — one methodological, warning against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research, the other epistemological, arguing GenAI outputs require an entirely new category ("emergent facts") beyond traditional fact-checking frameworks. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] examine how AI hype itself is constructed and geopolitically inflected in journalism and news media. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] turns the lens on research practice directly, showing AI coding agents can now reproduce a striking share of published social-science findings — a development with double-edged implications for both replication and the sycophantic distortion of results under confirmatory prompting. Meanwhile [[Rothut2026-wt]], [[Marwick2025-vx]], [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Wack2026-bt]], and [[Adam2026-tz]] each push back against simplistic "exposure equals belief/radicalization" models from within specific empirical literatures (radicalization, redpilling, disinformation narrative, deepfakes, conspiracy contagion), collectively reinforcing this section's broader message: that the mechanisms connecting online content to belief and behavior are more processual, socially negotiated, and context-dependent than the field's dominant paradigms have assumed. Taken together, these reflections suggest a field increasingly aware that its next advances depend less on more data from the same sources than on rigorous, reflexive reconstruction of its own evidentiary and institutional foundations — a project [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Rossini2026-jn]] gesture toward by insisting that even the platforms researchers study (messaging apps, non-US contexts) remain substantially unmapped.
