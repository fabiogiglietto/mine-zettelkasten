# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns the Mirror on Itself

A growing cluster of papers in this collection do not report new empirical findings so much as ask what the accumulated findings actually add up to — and find the answer wanting. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] opens with the sharpest diagnosis: misinformation research over-relies on cognitive proxies, rarely measures real-world behaviour, and remains parochially fixated on US partisanship, borrowing six correctives from the more battle-tested field of climate psychology (contextual generalisability, systems thinking, coalition-building against vested interests). This diagnosis of overclaiming and misdirected attention recurs almost verbatim in [[Budak2024-ef]], which argues that public and even scholarly discourse about misinformation's harms has drifted from what the evidence actually shows regarding prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal-level effects — a claim [[Oswald2025-km]] sharpens further by showing that the visible "tip of the iceberg" of online discourse, produced by a small hyperactive minority, systematically misleads both citizens and the researchers who treat social media traces as proxies for public opinion.

Geographic and cultural narrowness is a second, related indictment. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around the WEIRD-centrism problem, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalises the critique empirically: news-value analysis of 82,000 flagged items across 24 countries shows genuine but modest WEIRD/non-WEIRD divergence, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than as the default case the field often treats them as. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the same argument for polarization research specifically, calling for globally equitable, disciplinarily integrated frameworks, a call echoed in [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country trust-in-news trend analysis, which dismantles the assumed "Golden Age" narrative and shows the US to be a historical and recent outlier that has wrongly anchored the field's intuitions. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] pushes on a different axis of narrowness: misinformation research's topical fixation on politics and health obscures how ordinary people actually encounter "misinformation" — often as clickbait from mainstream, domestic outlets on everyday topics like economics, not fringe political falsehoods.

## Borrowing Rigor, Testing Assumptions

Several papers model what a more rigorous, adjacent-field-informed misinformation science could look like. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s registered-report megastudy on climate messaging is effectively [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s prescription enacted: high-powered, preregistered, behaviourally grounded testing that deflates a decade of citation-driven consensus about "the most persuasive" climate messages, revealing an attitude-behaviour gap that mirrors misinformation research's own struggles with belief-versus-behaviour outcomes. [[Matias2025-px]] extends the search for rigor outward, arguing that participatory, lived-experience involvement is not a political nicety but a way to catch validity problems (mismeasurement, hidden bias, unmodeled organisational context) that purely technical evaluation misses — a lesson as applicable to AI systems as to misinformation interventions. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]]'s Nature editorial distills this reformist mood into an explicit call to action: scientists studying division have an obligation to test, not merely theorise, depolarization interventions.

## Conceptual Fragmentation as an Explanation for Discord

A second major line of self-critique locates the field's inconsistent findings not in reality but in how researchers measure it. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that contradictory findings about whether echo chambers even exist largely reflect incompatible operationalizations rather than divergent social realities. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this quantitatively for polarization research: running 504 specification variants of partisan-media and polarization measures shows that methodological choice — not underlying relationships — drives most of the variance in reported effect sizes. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs an analogous audit on the NewsGuard database that underlies much source-based misinformation research, showing that continuous scores are fairly stable but binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can flip conclusions entirely depending on which snapshot a study happens to use. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends the critique to the normative layer: depolarization recommendations in published papers are almost never grounded in the papers' own evidence, but instead smuggle in unexamined ideals of deliberative democracy — a gap between evidence and prescription that closely parallels [[Bennett2025-xs]]'s argument that technocentric, individual-cognition-focused disinformation research has drawn disproportionate funding and attention away from the institutional and organizational dynamics (surrogate organizations, connective action) that actually explain democratic backsliding.

Definitional looseness is diagnosed at the level of core constructs, too. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization research finds that fewer than half of studies even define "radicalization," with longitudinal and causal evidence still scarce despite an "empirical turn" since 2018. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that the field's favourite metaphors — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each illuminate one level of a multilevel complex system while obscuring the others, producing interventions (like blunt media-literacy training) that can backfire. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the critique into disinformation studies' treatment of "narrative," arguing the field's true/false binary cannot capture content that is referentially and ethically troubling without being straightforwardly false. [[Marwick2025-vx]] similarly dismantles the field's dominant "exposure equals belief" radicalization model, showing via qualitative work with far-right communities that belief adoption is a gradual socialization process in which disinformation functions as performed "evidence" for a self-image of rational inquiry. [[Lieu2025-nl]]'s experimental test of climate misinformation taxonomies (CARDS content vs. FLICC fallacy) similarly finds that content, not logical form, drives perceived veracity and polarization — a small but telling instance of how untested taxonomic assumptions can misdirect intervention design. [[Ramos2026-qo]] draws the practical conclusion from this cluster: fact-checking single claims cannot keep pace with self-reinforcing, emotionally resonant narratives, and needs to be supplemented by narrative monitoring and argument-checking. And where individual-level effects are studied carefully, as in [[Lyons2026-ca]]'s tracking study of low-credibility health content, the more nuanced picture that emerges — concentrated exposure among older, more conspiracist-minded users, driven by habitual cross-site referral rather than algorithmic push — cautions against generalizing findings from political misinformation (the field's dominant case) to other content domains.

## The Data Infrastructure Crisis as an Epistemic Crisis

A distinct but tightly related set of reflections concerns not what researchers ask but what they can even see. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya jointly eulogize the "Golden Age" of open Twitter data, documenting a measurable, field-wide decline in output following the 2023 API paywall — a a loss of empirical capacity, not just convenience. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] periodize this trajectory across platforms, showing that access has moved from "Wild West" openness through brief "Academic API" cooperation to "Walled Garden" commercial paywalls, with the EU's DSA Article 40 offering the first attempt to convert access from platform philanthropy into regulatory right. [[Peters2026-mo]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] examine that regulatory apparatus in practice, finding that "data quality" was barely mentioned in official DSA text until academic and NGO stakeholders forced its partial inclusion, and that implementation remains hamstrung by resource asymmetries, circular funding problems, and platforms' capacity for bureaucratic delay. [[Bruns2026-pn]] shows what access looks like once granted: Meta's Content Library "clean room" model, while addressing privacy concerns, structurally excludes qualitative and non-programming researchers, forbids longitudinal work by deleting data monthly, and prevents cross-platform analysis altogether — deepening a divide that privileges well-resourced, code-literate, Global North institutions. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s systematic audit of YouTube's search API adds a further wrinkle: even where access exists, the tool itself is "forgetful by design," decaying in retrievability within weeks and undermining the retrospective designs many studies depend on. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows how these infrastructural quirks can masquerade as substantive findings, tracing a striking cross-country similarity in Meta's URL Shares Dataset to an algorithmic ranking artifact rather than genuine social convergence — a cautionary tale about mistaking platform engineering for social signal. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s large-scale bibliometric review confirms the consequences at the level of the whole discipline: empirical social media research is a small, Twitter/Facebook-skewed sliver of the literature that has now begun to plateau and decline post-API-restriction, with usage badly misaligned with actual platform popularity. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the constructive moral: rather than mourning the loss of individual-level causal designs, the field should use the DSA moment to reconceive platforms as democratic infrastructure to be studied collectively and structurally.

## Industry Capture and the Politics of Evidence

A closely allied set of papers interrogates who funds, shapes, and gatekeeps this research. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies the problem starkly: roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged set of authors and editors, with industry-tied work skewed toward user-blaming topics like misinformation sharing and away from platform-level dynamics. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] situates this within the longer history of tobacco- and fossil-fuel-style agnotology, cataloguing mechanisms — suppressed internal research, selective funding, "performative" collaborations, asymmetric statistical standards — by which platforms shape the evidence base used for their own regulation. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the structural root cause: because platforms uniquely control the data needed to study them, researcher independence is far more fragile here than in fields like pharma or food science where independent data generation remains possible. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership makes the case concrete: even the most ambitious, well-resourced academic-industry collaboration to date produced numerically narrow, quickly-decaying, conceptually inconsistent findings, leading Munger to conclude that reactive empirical social science is structurally incapable of keeping pace with platforms — an argument for proactive, disclosure-based regulation instead. [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later retrospective on her own 2015 manifesto extends the pessimism to the discipline's efficacy altogether, arguing that a decade of critical scholarship has coincided with, not curbed, worsening wealth concentration, algorithmic opacity, and data extraction, and calling for research that moves beyond critique toward engaged intervention. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] radicalizes this argument for the AI moment, contending that even critical AI accountability scholarship can function as a "decoy" that inadvertently legitimizes the underlying political economy of AI capital, while [[Stanusch2026-ec]]'s controversy-mapping of the Altman firing shows industry discourse actively using "premediation" and "preclusion" strategies to displace present harms into speculative futures — a rhetorical move that misinformation and AI-harms researchers alike need to learn to recognize rather than reproduce.

## AI as Object, Tool, and Complication for the Field

A final cluster reflects on generative AI's double role: as a new object the field must study and as a set of tools now used to conduct that study. [[Balluff2026-if]] warns against unreflective adoption of LLMs in communication research itself, cataloguing reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs that mirror the field's broader rigor problem. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] show the more optimistic edge of this same trend — AI coding agents reproducing published social-science findings with surprising fidelity, and automated review pipelines catching mathematical errors at scale — while flagging new risks (sycophantic specification search, metadata leakage) that are direct descendants of the field's older concerns about researcher degrees of freedom. [[Unknown2025-qj]] and [[Matias2025-px]] extend the participatory-science argument to AI safety evaluation itself, arguing red-teaming and lived-experience involvement can materially improve scientific rigor, not just legitimacy. [[UnknownUnknown-db]]'s large-scale Claude-user survey exemplifies a further complication: platform-adjacent (in this case AI-company) research increasingly generates its own self-reported evidence base about societal impact, raising exactly the industry-capture concerns this literature has spent a decade diagnosing for social media.

## Toward a More Coordinated, Self-Aware Field

Read together, these papers converge on a shared prescription even as they diagnose different symptoms: the misinformation and online-harms field needs less siloed, more anticipatory, and more globally representative evidence; more transparency about industry entanglement and normative assumptions; sturdier, regulation-backed data infrastructure; and methodological humility about what any single measure, taxonomy, or platform can reveal. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes this coordination ideal directly, assembling 47 international experts to produce an integrative, multi-level, anticipatory assessment that explicitly resists the individual-level, present-focused bias documented elsewhere in this collection — while also revealing, tellingly, that even this expert panel failed to anticipate generative AI's disruptive arrival. Taken as a whole, the collection reads less as a set of isolated complaints than as an emerging, self-organizing meta-discourse: a field old enough to have accumulated real evidentiary gaps, honest enough to name them, and increasingly willing to borrow — from climate psychology, from science studies, from regulatory policy — the tools needed to close them.
