# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns the Lens on Itself

A recognizable genre has emerged within misinformation and online-harms scholarship: work that steps back from data collection to ask whether the field's questions, methods, and evidentiary base are adequate to its ambitions. Across the papers collected here, three worries recur — that findings don't generalize beyond a narrow slice of (mostly American, mostly WEIRD) contexts, that the field over-indexes on cheap cognitive proxies rather than costly real-world behavior, and that structural dependencies on platforms and funders quietly shape what gets studied and published. A parallel, related concern is that the field's core concepts — "fake news," "echo chamber," "misinformation" itself — may be too loosely defined to bear the theoretical weight placed on them.

## Borrowing Rigor from More Mature Fields

[[Spampatti2026-kx]] makes the sharpest version of the "borrow lessons" argument, distilling six insights from climate psychology — a field with a longer history of confronting organized denial — for misinformation researchers: attend to motivational and emotional drivers rather than information alone, prioritize high-impact behavioral outcomes (fewer than 1% of misinformation studies currently do), take context and cross-country heterogeneity seriously, think in systems rather than individuals, guard against corporate capture of company collaborations, and organize collectively as a scientific community. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] operationalizes exactly this kind of rigor within climate communication itself: a registered-report megastudy testing the ten most-cited climate messages finds mostly small, non-heterogeneous attitude effects and no impact on costly donation behavior, modeling the credibility-revolution standard (preregistration, large N, replication of "canonical" findings) that misinformation research is urged to adopt. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] extends the same "researchers-have-a-responsibility" framing to depolarization work, calling for more rigorous testing of interventions such as proximate policy framing.

## The WEIRD Problem and the Push for Global Equity

Several pieces diagnose a geographic and cultural narrowness in the evidence base. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around moving political misinformation research beyond WEIRD contexts, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies a concrete empirical test of that concern, showing that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation differ — modestly but significantly — between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil as outliers rather than templates. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel argument for polarization research, which remains disciplinarily fragmented and disproportionately concentrated on the United States. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s international Delphi study is itself a corrective, assembling 47 scholars and practitioners across countries to produce an anticipatory, multi-level assessment rather than another single-country case study. [[Mota2026-sl59]] widens the lens further, showing that even meta-level discourse about AI is asymmetric: Global North media frame AI through risk and threat, Global South media through opportunity and development, a divergence with real implications for whose "problem" misinformation and AI harm are assumed to be.

## Cognitive Proxies, Real Behavior, and the Attention Gap

A related critique targets what the field measures rather than where it looks. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues that public discourse about misinformation's harms has drifted from the empirical evidence, overstating average exposure, algorithmic causation, and societal-scale effects. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Oswald2025-km]] supply complementary empirical grounding: exposure to low-credibility content is concentrated in a small subset of (often older) users rather than broadly diffused, and the tiny minority who produce most visible political content distorts both citizens' and researchers' inferences about "public opinion" from social media traces. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] pushes in a different direction, arguing the field has been too narrowly topic-driven (health, politics, science) and too abstracted from lived experience, missing everyday misinformation about business, economics, and celebrity that mainstream — not fringe — outlets propagate. Together these papers suggest the field's cognitive-proxy default (belief change, sharing intentions) obscures both how rare and how behaviorally consequential misinformation actually is, echoing Spampatti et al.'s call to prioritize real-world outcomes.

## Conceptual Fog: Metaphors, Definitions, and Missing Feedback

A cluster of papers argues that imprecise root metaphors have distorted the field's theoretical imagination. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] shows how the dominant "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies each illuminate only one level of a multilevel complex system, producing incomplete — sometimes counterproductive — interventions. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] makes an analogous move for "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles," proposing a feedback-loop framework that reconciles weak empirical evidence for bubbles with the real existence of self-reinforcing dynamics, while [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies finds that much of the field's apparent disagreement is an artifact of inconsistent operationalization rather than contradictory reality. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] traces the same conceptual slippage back to "fake news" itself, arguing that creator-intent-centered typologies cannot capture how falsehoods actually propagate through a hybrid media system, and proposing a micro/meso/macro model centered on propagators rather than originators. [[Sadler2025-vu]] extends this critique to "narrative," arguing disinformation studies treats stories as self-evidently true or false when a hermeneutic-realist and ethical framework is needed to assess content that is misleading without being straightforwardly false. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds a structurally similar gap between evidence and prescription in depolarization scholarship: recommendations in discussion sections are overwhelmingly disconnected from the studies' own findings, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the causal-effects paradigm itself needs replacing with an infrastructural, collective-behavior lens suited to the DSA-era data landscape.

## Industry Entanglement and the Political Economy of Platform Research

A second major thread interrogates the field's structural dependence on the very companies it studies. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together document this most systematically: the latter's empirical audit of high-profile social media papers finds roughly half have disclosable industry ties, mostly undisclosed, concentrated among a small repeatedly-engaged set of authors, editors, and reviewers, with industry-tied work skewing toward user-focused topics (like misinformation sharing) at the expense of platform-dynamics research and receiving roughly double the citations and public attention. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a problem unique in degree to social media research — since platforms uniquely control the data needed to study them — and draws on tobacco/pharma/food-industry parallels also invoked by [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]]. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a granular case study of one such collaboration, the Meta2020 project, arguing that even at its most methodologically ambitious, platform-mediated research produces estimates with low "temporal validity" (the platform changes too fast to replicate) and inconsistent "poetic validity" (core concepts like echo chamber are operationalized differently paper to paper), concluding that reactive empirical social science cannot keep pace with platforms and that proactive regulation is the only remedy. [[Bennett2025-xs]] extends the critique at the theoretical level, arguing that individual-level "technocentric" disinformation research — heavily funded and therefore disproportionately influential — has crowded out institutionalist analysis of how digital networks organize scattered extremists into electorally consequential surrogate organizations.

## Data Access, Regulation, and the DSA as Both Opportunity and Constraint

Closely related is a set of papers assessing the EU Digital Services Act as an infrastructural fix for industry-dependent research. [[Pierri2025-hm]] and its near-duplicate [[Pierri2026-ib]] report from direct involvement in DSA policy discussions, cataloguing misaligned incentives, resource asymmetries, and a "circular problem" whereby researchers need data access to win grants but funding to pursue data access, while flagging LLMs as a regulatory blind spot. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows empirically that "data quality" — central to researchers' concerns — was largely absent from official EU deliberation until academic and NGO stakeholders pushed it into the Delegated Regulation, revealing data quality itself as a politically contested rather than purely methodological construct. [[Heiss2026-qv]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] converge with these accounts in treating the DSA as a necessary but so-far-insufficient counterweight to platform control over evidence.

## Methodological Self-Scrutiny: Measurement, Tools, and Reproducibility

A more technical strand audits the field's specific instruments. [[Luhring2025-od]] stress-tests NewsGuard, the widely used source-trustworthiness database, finding that continuous scores are fairly stable since 2022 but that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can dramatically distort findings depending on which single major outlet crosses the threshold. [[Schemer2026-mh]] runs a specification-curve analysis across 504 ways of operationalizing media partisanship and polarization, showing that methodological choices — not underlying reality — drive much of the variance researchers report about partisan media effects. [[Yang2026-tq]] steps back further, documenting that empirical social-media research itself is a small, platform-skewed sliver of social science (dominated by Twitter/X and Facebook, misaligned with actual platform popularity) that is now plateauing as API access closes. [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] turn the same scrutiny toward the field's newest tools: the former warns against unreflective LLM adoption for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulation given reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs, while the latter finds that purpose-built AI coding agents can reproduce a striking share of published computational social-science findings — a capability with implications for both replication auditing and the automation of research itself. [[Gagrcin2024-dl]] and [[Rothut2026-wt]] perform more traditional scoping work in this spirit, mapping fragmented conceptualizations of algorithm literacy and 25 years of online-radicalization research respectively, both concluding that longitudinal and causal evidence remains thin relative to the volume of published work.

## A Field (and Its Neighbors) in Flux

A final cluster reflects on how quickly the terrain itself is shifting, threatening to outdate the field's accumulated toolkit. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues the "social media" paradigm underlying two decades of scholarship is dissolving into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, each demanding distinct methods. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits a decade-old manifesto to find its diagnosed harms worsened rather than resolved, urging researchers toward engaged collaboration over critique-for-its-own-sake. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Vertesi2026-lv]] extend meta-critique to AI coverage and AI accountability research specifically, arguing that hype narratives and "decoy" debates (about AI's ontology, inevitability, safety) function to redirect scrutiny away from the political economy and infrastructural power actually driving the technology. [[Matias2025-px]] counters with a constructive proposal — that participatory, lived-experience involvement can improve rather than merely legitimate AI evaluation science. Rounding out the set, papers such as [[Marwick2025-vx]], [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], [[Ramos2026-qo]], [[Fletcher2026-lv]], and [[UnknownUnknown-db]] each reframe a specific empirical puzzle — radicalization as gradual socialization rather than sudden "redpilling," journalism's inadvertent role in seeding conspiracy belief, the limits of fact-checking against evolving narratives, the non-universal and pre-internet origins of declining trust in news, and workers' own accounts of AI's economic effects — in ways that collectively insist the field's inherited frameworks (media effects, single-cause radicalization, claim-level correction, a mythical trust "golden age") need revision in light of more careful, often longitudinal or cross-national, evidence.
