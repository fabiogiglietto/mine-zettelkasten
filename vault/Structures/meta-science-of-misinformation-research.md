# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## Diagnosing the Field's Biases

A recurring diagnosis across this literature is that misinformation and online-harms research has been built on a narrow evidentiary base — geographically, methodologically, and conceptually. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's WEIRD-centrism, and [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies empirical teeth to that critique, showing that news-value patterns in perceived misinformation differ meaningfully (if often modestly) between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than representative cases. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the same argument for polarization research, calling it disciplinarily fragmented and disproportionately anchored to a handful of Western countries. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization scholarship echoes this: over half the literature treats "online" radicalization only as a secondary concern, definitions are inconsistent, and causal/longitudinal evidence remains scarce despite an "empirical turn" after 2018. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] adds a conceptual critique, arguing that the field's guiding metaphors — infodemic, information warfare, information pollution — each capture only one level of a multilevel complex system, systematically obscuring others and licensing interventions (like blunt media-literacy campaigns) that can backfire. [[Budak2024-ef]] and the closely related [[Lyons2026-ca]], [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]], and [[Oswald2025-km]] converge on a different but related bias: public and even scholarly discourse has overstated the prevalence, algorithmic drivers, and societal impact of misinformation exposure, which turns out to be low on average and heavily concentrated among small subpopulations (older adults, heavy partisan-news consumers) rather than broadly distributed — a pattern with real consequences for how "the field" imagines its object of study.

## Borrowing from More Mature Fields

Several papers explicitly look outside misinformation research for correctives. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the clearest statement of this move, distilling six lessons from climate psychology — attend to motivational and emotional drivers beyond cognition, measure real behavior rather than cognitive proxies (currently under 1% of studies do), take context and cross-country heterogeneity seriously, think systemically, guard against corporate capture of company collaborations, and organize collectively against coordinated denial. Its companion pieces make good on parts of this agenda: [[Voelkel2026-lc]] applies megastudy rigor to the ten most-cited climate messages, finding consistently small attitudinal effects that rarely translate into costly behavior (donations), while [[Lieu2025-nl]] dissects climate misinformation using both content-based (CARDS) and logic-based (FLICC) taxonomies to show that message content, not fallacy type, drives perceived veracity and polarization — a methodological template misinformation researchers are urged to emulate. [[Matias2025-px]] draws a parallel lesson from participatory science more broadly, arguing that lived-experience expertise can materially improve — not just legitimate — the rigor of AI and platform evaluation at each stage from equipoise to interpretation. Taken together, these papers suggest the field's next methodological maturation may come less from inventing new tools than from importing ones already stress-tested elsewhere.

## The Political Economy of Platform Research

A second cluster interrogates who controls the evidence base itself. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] extend agnotology's "merchants of doubt" framework to tech, documenting that roughly half of high-profile social-media research has undisclosed industry ties, that these ties concentrate among a small set of repeatedly-engaged scientists who also dominate editorial gatekeeping, and that industry-adjacent work skews toward user-blaming topics (misinformation sharing) while under-studying platform-level dynamics. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within the longer history of tobacco, pharma, and food-industry science, arguing that platforms' exclusive control of data makes this asymmetry structurally worse than in those precedents. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers the sharpest single case study: even the most ambitious platform-academic collaboration, Meta2020, is shown to lack both "temporal" and "poetic" validity — its numerical estimates decay too fast to generalize, and its core constructs (e.g., "echo chamber") are operationalized inconsistently across the very papers that comprise it — leading Munger to conclude that reactive social science cannot keep pace with platforms and that proactive, disclosure-mandating regulation is the only durable fix. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] supplies a technical demonstration of how such dependency plays out invisibly: a simple anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset generates near-identical monthly trends across 46 countries, an algorithmic artifact that can be mistaken for substantive cross-national convergence. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] pushes the political-economy critique furthest, arguing that even well-intentioned accountability research on AI risks becoming a "decoy" that legitimizes the underlying capital-and-infrastructure project it seeks to check.

## Data Access, Infrastructure, and Its Collapse

Closely related is a wave of papers documenting the infrastructural fragility underlying the last decade of empirical findings. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya eulogize the Twitter API "golden age," quantifying a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications following 2023 pricing changes and warning of a lasting loss of empirical capacity. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes this concern across platforms and disciplines, showing social-media-based social science tripled from 2010 before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024, with research attention persistently misaligned with actual platform usage (Twitter/X overstudied, YouTube understudied). [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] turn the critique toward the platform-mediated alternatives meant to replace open APIs: YouTube's search endpoint is shown to be "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and irreproducibility, while Meta's clean-room environments (successor to CrowdTangle) are shown to systematically exclude non-programming researchers, block longitudinal and cross-platform work, and widen inequities favoring well-resourced Global North institutions. [[Peters2026-mo]], [[Pierri2025-hm]], and [[Pierri2026-ib]] (near-duplicate perspectives from overlapping author teams) examine the EU Digital Services Act's Article 40 as a regulatory response to this crisis, finding that data-quality concerns were nearly absent from official EU deliberation until academic and NGO stakeholders pushed them in, and that resource asymmetries, circular funding problems, and unaddressed blind spots (notably LLMs) still threaten the mechanism's promise.

## Measurement, Reflexivity, and the Limits of Method

A parallel body of work turns the critical lens onto specific measurement practices within the field. [[Hartmann2025-px]] argues that decades of disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist owes more to inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization than to divergent underlying reality. [[Schemer2026-mh]] demonstrates this concretely for polarization research: running 504 specification variants of media-partisanship and polarization measures on the same panel data, the authors show that methodological choices swing coefficient magnitude nearly sevenfold, though not direction — a sobering brief for specification-curve transparency. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a comparable audit of the NewsGuard database widely used in source-based misinformation research, finding continuous scores reasonably stable since 2022 but binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs highly sensitive to a handful of borderline sources (Breitbart, Bild) crossing threshold. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] extends this reflexivity to the normative register, showing that depolarization recommendations in the literature are usually disconnected from the very evidence the studies produce, resting instead on unexamined commitments to deliberative-democracy ideals. [[Pante2025-pq]] applies the same self-correcting spirit to disinformation-operations research, re-testing claims of coordinated inter-state influence operations with more rigorous, control-calibrated methods. And two papers turn reflexivity toward the field's newest tools: [[Balluff2026-if]] cautions against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research (reproducibility loss, corporate dependency, demographic bias in synthetic data), while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] offers a more optimistic counterpoint, showing that frontier coding agents can reproduce a substantial share of published social-science findings — with important caveats about sycophantic behavior under confirmatory prompting.

## Synthesizing Assessments and Calls to Action

Finally, several pieces attempt field-wide synthesis or exhortation. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 international experts explicitly diagnoses the field as fragmented and overly individual-level in its interventions, converging on the "no silver bullet" conclusion that platform governance and journalism-ecosystem reform matter more than the debunking/prebunking work that dominates academic output — notably, experts in 2022 failed to anticipate generative AI as a coming disruptor. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complements this top-down assessment with a bottom-up one, using diary methodology to show that lay perceptions of misinformation extend well beyond the "hot button" topics (politics, health) the field fixates on, into everyday domains like business and celebrity news — a reminder that the field's topical scope may itself be a WEIRD-adjacent, elite artifact. [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] offers a rare example of successfully scaled, ecologically valid intervention research (a prebunking ad reaching 375,597 real Instagram users), suggesting a template for the "high-impact behavioral outcomes" Spampatti and colleagues call for. At the broadest level, [[Baym2026-tr]] and [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] close the loop with exhortation: Baym's decade-later reassessment argues that critique alone has not moved the needle against consolidating platform power, and the Nature editorial insists researchers of division bear responsibility to test — not just diagnose — interventions against polarization. Read together, the 39 papers in this collection trace an arc from cataloguing what is wrong with the field's evidentiary base (WEIRD samples, cognitive proxies, industry entanglement, collapsing data infrastructure, inconsistent measurement) toward increasingly concrete proposals — borrowed frameworks, regulatory mechanisms, specification transparency, and collective organization — for making misinformation and online-harms research more rigorous, representative, and consequential.
