# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Looking in the Mirror

A distinct genre has emerged within misinformation scholarship: papers that step outside the empirical grind to ask whether the field itself is asking the right questions, of the right people, with the right tools. This body of reflection converges on a handful of recurring diagnoses — WEIRD-centrism, cognitive overreach, industry capture, infrastructural fragility, and conceptual confusion — each of which has its own cluster of critiques and remedies below.

### The WEIRD Problem and the Limits of Generalization

Perhaps the most persistent self-critique is geographic and cultural narrowness. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's disproportionate focus on Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic contexts, and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel argument for polarization research specifically, calling for disciplinary integration alongside global equity. [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes this concern empirically, showing that news-value signatures of misinformation differ — modestly but significantly — between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than exemplars. [[Humprecht2020-gd]], an earlier but foundational entry in this vein, demonstrates that even among Western democracies, resilience to disinformation clusters unevenly, with the US standing apart as a uniquely vulnerable case that should not anchor general theory. [[Fletcher2026-lv]] extends this comparative impulse to trust in news, using nearly half a century of global data to show that the "golden age" of trust is a myth and that the US's trajectory is exceptional rather than representative. Together these pieces argue that single-country (usually American) findings have been silently universalized, and that correcting this requires not just more countries but better frameworks for comparison — a concern [[Rothut2026-wt]] echoes in its scoping review of radicalization research, which finds Western-skewed samples and calls for hybrid online/offline conceptualizations rather than geographically narrow models.

### Cognitive Proxies, Behavioral Outcomes, and the Scale Question

A second cluster interrogates what the field actually measures. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] crystallizes this critique most directly, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world, high-impact behavioral outcomes, and proposing that climate psychology's hard-won lessons — attending to motivation, emotion, systems dynamics, and cross-country heterogeneity — offer a corrective. This resonates with a lineage of "scale skepticism" papers: [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Guess2020-rr]] both show that fake news occupies a vanishingly small share of the actual media diet, concentrated among a narrow slice of consumers, while [[Budak2024-ef]] argues that public and policy discourse has drifted far from this evidence base, overstating algorithmic culpability and societal harm. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] adds a causal twist: even concentrated exposure to untrustworthy sources produced no measurable attitudinal effects, reinforcing doubts about whether exposure-based proxies translate into real persuasion. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]] complicate the folk narrative that partisanship drives susceptibility, redirecting attention to inattention and reasoning failures, or to identity-expressive sharing — distinctions [[van-Eck2026-xg]] shows are rarely carried through into policy-relevant depolarization recommendations, which instead lean on unexamined normative ideals like deliberative democracy rather than the evidence these same studies generate.

### Climate Psychology and Other Borrowed Maturity

Several papers explicitly seek methodological borrowing from more mature adjacent fields. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the clearest case, but [[Voelkel2026-lc]] and [[Lieu2025-nl]] model what rigor transplanted from climate communication looks like in practice — the former a registered-report megastudy revealing that even the most-cited climate messages yield only small, largely non-behavioral effects, the latter showing content-based (CARDS) rather than logic-based (FLICC) framings better predict perceived veracity and polarization. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] pushes the metaphor-borrowing further, arguing that "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies each illuminate only part of a complex multilevel system, and that climate-style portfolio interventions are needed rather than single-lever fixes. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] gestures at a similar cross-disciplinary responsibility, urging polarization researchers toward rigorous, field-tested interventions.

### Industry Capture and the Political Economy of Evidence

A sharp and increasingly urgent strand concerns who controls the data and who funds the science. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies the problem starkly: roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small set of repeat-collaborators who also populate editorial boards, biasing the literature toward user-focused (misinformation-sharing) questions and away from platform-dynamics questions that might implicate platform design. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] generalize this into explicit analogy with tobacco, pharma, and fossil-fuel science, cataloguing mechanisms — suppressed internal research, selective funding, performative collaboration — through which industry shapes the evidence base, while [[Pierri2026-ib]] documents how even regulatory correctives like the DSA's Article 40 face platform resistance and resource asymmetries that reproduce the same dependency. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a granular case study of this dynamic via the Meta2020 partnership, arguing that even the most ambitious industry-academic collaboration produced narrow, non-replicable, temporally fragile estimates — a verdict echoed methodologically by [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]], who each show that a simple anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset silently distorts prevalence estimates and cross-country comparisons, inflating apparent fake-news consumption by 2-4x. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the constructive implication, arguing the DSA era demands a shift from causal-effects paradigms toward treating platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure in its own right.

### Infrastructural Precarity: The Post-API Age

Closely related is a cluster diagnosing the field's growing dependence on — and vulnerability to — unstable data infrastructures. [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes two decades of platform data policy to show access has become permanently contingent on commercial and regulatory whim, a thesis empirically confirmed by [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]], whose bibliometric eulogy documents a 13% decline in Twitter-based research following API paywalls, and by [[Yang2026-tq]], who shows social-media-based social science publishing plateaued and declined in 2022-2024 after years of Twitter/Facebook dominance misaligned with actual platform usage. [[Luhring2025-od]] turns this infrastructural concern toward source-rating tools themselves, auditing NewsGuard and warning that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications (rather than continuous scores) can distort findings disproportionately. [[Pierri2026-ib]] and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] both tie this precarity to democratic stakes, arguing that the capacity to study platforms is itself a political good under threat.

### Conceptual Housekeeping: What Are We Even Studying?

A final strand steps back from empirics entirely to interrogate core vocabulary. [[Freelon2020-yp]] traces the short, conceptually tangled history of "disinformation" versus "misinformation" versus "fake news," arguing the field's terminology surged reactively after 2016 without settling foundational distinctions — a terminological fragility [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study confirms still troubles experts, who rate ongoing definitional debates as a distraction from more pressing priorities even as they anticipate worsening trends (generative AI's current centrality was notably unanticipated as recently as 2022). [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows the same conceptual heterogeneity explains contradictory findings in echo-chamber research, while [[Sadler2025-vu]] argues disinformation studies has used "narrative" naively, collapsing it into true/false binaries and missing the ethical and hermeneutic dimensions of how stories mislead without being false. [[Mosca2026-yh]] reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as rhetorical weapons rather than objects of correction, and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] shows that when researchers stop imposing a priori definitions and instead ask ordinary users what counts as misinformation, the picture shifts toward mundane, text-based, mainstream-news-sourced content rather than the fringe, audiovisual, politically hot-button material the literature foregrounds. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] adds a further wrinkle: even perceptions of *who* is targeted by misinformation are systematically biased by partisan identity, complicating any claim to neutral measurement. [[Ramos2026-qo]] argues that fact-checking's claim-level unit of analysis is itself conceptually too narrow, proposing narrative- and argument-level monitoring as necessary complements. And [[Marwick2025-vx]] challenges the field's default "exposure equals belief" model of radicalization with a processual, socialization-based alternative — a conceptual move paralleled in [[Bennett2025-xs]]'s critique of "technocentric" disinformation paradigms, which argues individual-level correction research has absorbed disproportionate funding (~$70 million 2016-2021) while missing the institutional and organizational dynamics that actually transform fringe movements into electorally consequential forces.

### Emerging Frontiers: AI as Both Object and Instrument

A final, rapidly developing set of reflections concerns generative AI's dual role as new subject matter and new research tool. [[Emilio2026-ik]] reframes AI harm beyond deepfakes toward "synthetic realities" that erode the very possibility of evidentiary trust, while [[Vertesi2026-lv]] warns that critiques of AI often function as "decoys" distracting from the underlying political economy of capital and infrastructure. On the methods side, [[Balluff2026-if]] cautions against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research itself, citing reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs, even as [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] explore AI's growing capacity to reproduce and even police scientific findings — suggesting the meta-scientific reflexivity chronicled throughout this topic may soon need to encompass the research process itself.

### The Arc

Read together, these papers trace a field maturing through self-doubt: from early foundational warnings about sticky misinformation and weak corrections ([[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Kunda1990-cg]]) and the first empirical reality-checks on fake news's actual reach ([[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Tsfati2020-uo]]), through a reckoning with platform dependency and industry capture, toward an emerging consensus that rigor now requires global comparison, behavioral (not just cognitive) measurement, infrastructural independence, and conceptual humility — lessons the field is still, unevenly, learning to apply to itself.
