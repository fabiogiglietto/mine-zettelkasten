# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## A field auditing itself

The four papers gathered here share a common gesture: instead of adding another data point to the misinformation literature, they turn around and ask whether the literature's accumulated data points actually mean what everyone assumes. This is a field old enough now to have habits, blind spots, and a public mythology — and each paper attacks a different piece of that mythology. [[Budak2024-ef]] targets the *public narrative*, arguing that journalists and commentators have drifted from what the evidence actually says about exposure, algorithms, and societal harm. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] targets the *research practice itself*, diagnosing an overreliance on cognitive proxies and underuse of real-world behavioural outcomes. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] targets the *geographic scope* of the evidence base, naming its WEIRD-centrism directly. And [[Rothut2026-wt]], while framed as a scoping review of radicalization rather than misinformation per se, performs the same diagnostic move for an adjacent field, finding the identical symptoms: Western skew, definitional confusion, and a scarcity of longitudinal, causal work.

## The gap between evidence and narrative

[[Budak2024-ef]] is the most direct correction: it isolates three specific misperceptions — that problematic content is pervasive, that algorithms are the primary engine of exposure, and that social media straightforwardly causes societal harm — and shows each to be an overreach relative to the data, which instead point to concentrated exposure among small subsets of users and contested causal chains. This paper functions as a kind of reality check that the other three implicitly build on: if the empirical base is already more modest and heterogeneous than public discourse assumes, then the calls in [[Spampatti2026-kx]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] for *better* and *more varied* evidence become all the more urgent, since thin or narrow evidence is exactly what allows folk narratives to fill the vacuum.

## Diagnosing structural biases: WEIRD, cognitive, Western

[[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] converge on a shared complaint even though one is an editorial introducing a special issue and the other a cross-field comment: misinformation research has been built overwhelmingly on US-centric, English-language, partisan-politics data, and this narrowness undermines claims to generalizability. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames the fix as comparative and cross-platform empirical expansion — literally commissioning studies outside WEIRD contexts. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] frames the same problem more diagnostically, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world, high-impact behavioural outcomes, and that even well-established predictors (like political ideology's grip on climate attitudes) weaken outside their country of origin. Both papers treat generalizability not as a nice-to-have but as a precondition for policy relevance. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review independently confirms the pattern in a neighbouring domain: radicalization research remains skewed toward the UK, Germany, and the US, with over half of studies not even country-specific, suggesting this is a structural feature of internet-and-society research broadly rather than an idiosyncrasy of misinformation studies alone.

## Borrowed maturity: what other fields can teach

Where [[Humprecht2025-ml]] responds to the WEIRD problem by widening the empirical net directly, [[Spampatti2026-kx]] takes a different tack — importing lessons from a field it considers further along the same road. Climate psychology, having weathered decades of organised denial from vested interests, offers six transferable insights: attend to motivational and emotional drivers beyond cognition, integrate individual- and system-level analysis, treat corporate collaborations as necessary but requiring safeguards, and organise researchers into resilient networks akin to the IPCC or ManyLabs consortia. This is a notably institutional diagnosis — the problem isn't just what researchers measure but how they organise themselves against political and commercial pressure. [[Rothut2026-wt]] arrives at a structurally similar prescription from within radicalization studies: it calls for integrating macro, meso, and micro levels of analysis (mirrored in Spampatti et al.'s individual/system integration) and explicitly rejects the online/offline binary that has long organised the field, arguing that hybrid socio-technical framing is now the only tenable one.

## Convergent gaps: causality, longitudinality, definition

Read together, the papers triangulate on the same evidentiary deficits. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s review is the most quantified on this point — only 34 of 382 studies are longitudinal, less than half define their central concept explicitly, and most work relies on proxies (exposure, engagement) rather than causal designs, for reasons that are partly ethical and partly access-driven. This mirrors [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s complaint that misinformation research overleans on cognitive/attitudinal proxies rather than downstream behaviour, and echoes [[Budak2024-ef]]'s point that causal claims about societal harm outrun what the data can support. The consistency across two adjacent but distinct literatures — misinformation and radicalization — suggests these are not idiosyncratic failures of individual studies but structural features of how online-harms research has been forced to operate: constrained data access, ethical limits on manipulation, and a scholarly incentive structure that rewards rapid, cross-sectional, platform-of-convenience studies over slower comparative or longitudinal designs.

## Toward a more rigorous field

Taken as a set, these four pieces sketch an emerging self-corrective moment in the field. The direction of travel is consistent: broaden geographic and cultural scope ([[Humprecht2025-ml]]), correct the public record against the actual evidentiary base ([[Budak2024-ef]]), diversify outcome measures and organisational strategy by learning from more mature adjacent fields ([[Spampatti2026-kx]]), and resist artificial conceptual boundaries (online/offline, single-cause models) in favor of multi-level, hybrid frameworks ([[Rothut2026-wt]]). None of the four claims the underlying phenomena are unimportant — quite the opposite, each insists that getting the science right matters more, not less, given the stakes of democratic erosion and political violence. The shared subtext is a field asking whether its methodological habits and geographic default settings have kept pace with the global, cross-platform, and long-term nature of the problems it studies.
