# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## A field turning the mirror on itself

Misinformation and online-harms research has reached a reflexive moment: rather than producing another empirical study of belief or sharing, a growing cluster of papers steps back to ask whether the field's accumulated evidence base is trustworthy, generalizable, and properly causal. Delphi panels of international experts ([[Mahl2026-hc]]), systematic reviews of polarization recommendations ([[van-Eck2026-xg]]), echo-chamber conceptualizations ([[Hartmann2025-px]]), and radicalization scholarship spanning 25 years ([[Rothut2026-wt]]) all converge on a similar diagnosis: the literature is fragmented, inconsistently defined, dominated by individual-level cognitive framings, and only weakly connected to the policy recommendations it generates. Editorial and programmatic pieces ([[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Unknown2026-sl3d]], [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], [[Bechmann2026-dr]]) explicitly call for reorientation — toward comparative, infrastructural, and globally representative inquiry rather than one-off, WEIRD, individual-effects studies. Read together, these works treat the field itself as an object of study, applying the same scrutiny to its evidentiary practices that it has long applied to public belief.

## The WEIRD problem and the limits of generalization

A central and recurring complaint is that findings generated in the US, UK, and a handful of Western European democracies have been treated as universal. [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction names this directly, and empirical work substantiates it: [[Nenno2025-xa]] shows that news-value signatures of "fake news" differ meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than exemplars; [[Humprecht2020-gd]] demonstrates that societal "resilience" to disinformation is a structural, cross-nationally variable property, with the US again standing apart as an idiosyncratic case that should not anchor general theory; and [[Fletcher2026-lv]], tracing trust in news across 115 countries since 1981, finds the "Golden Age of trust" to be a myth and shows that trajectories diverge by media-freedom regime, with autocracies showing rising (not falling) trust. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends this critique to polarization research broadly, arguing for integration across disciplines and geographies. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] adds a case in point — far-right electoral performance shaping disinformation concern across EU states — illustrating the kind of comparative, context-sensitive evidence the field needs more of. Even infrastructural access to data is WEIRD-skewed: [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Bruns2026-pn]] both show that platform data-access regimes (API tools, clean rooms) are built for well-resourced, English-language, Global North institutions, compounding the empirical bias with an infrastructural one. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complicates the picture from within a single country, showing that even "WEIRD" misinformation looks different once researchers stop imposing a priori hot-button topic categories and instead let users define what feels false.

## Cognitive proxies, prevalence inflation, and the proxy-outcome gap

A second strand interrogates whether the field has been measuring the right things. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] crystallizes the critique: fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world, high-impact behavioral outcomes, with the field overwhelmingly reliant on self-reported belief, sharing intentions, or lab-based cognitive proxies. This concern is corroborated by a lineage of prevalence studies that found actual exposure to fall far short of public alarm: [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Allen2020-nj]] show fake news constitutes a vanishingly small share of Americans' media diets, dwarfed by television; [[Guess2020-rr]] finds untrustworthy-site consumption concentrated in a narrow, already-partisan slice of the public; [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds platform-wide exposure to untrustworthy sources low and concentrated, with no detectable attitudinal effects from experimentally reducing it; and [[Vincent_undated-re]] offers continuing measurement evidence that such exposure, while real and platform-specific (especially on TikTok), remains a minority phenomenon. [[Budak2024-ef]] synthesizes this pattern explicitly as a mismatch between public/scientific discourse and the evidence, challenging assumptions about algorithmic amplification and societal harm. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies a structural explanation: a small, highly active minority produces most visible political content, so both citizens and researchers studying "public opinion" from online traces are really studying an unrepresentative "tip of the iceberg." Underneath much of this sits a methodological hazard: [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] independently show that privacy-preserving thresholds in platform-provided datasets (notably Meta's URL Shares Dataset) introduce systematic, algorithm-driven distortions — inflating apparent fake-news prevalence or masquerading as cross-national "events" that are actually artifacts of feed-ranking changes. Together these papers suggest that much of what the field has believed about scale and harm has been shaped as much by measurement artifacts and platform opacity as by social reality — a critique sharpened by [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard, which shows that common binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications (rather than continuous scores) can flip substantive conclusions.

## Causal ambition versus platform reality

A third set of papers asks whether the field's causal infrastructure can keep pace with its subject matter. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership is emblematic: even the best-resourced, most rigorous platform collaboration to date produced underpowered, narrowly-scoped estimates whose "temporal validity" expired almost as soon as they were published, because platforms change faster than peer review. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] draws the natural conclusion, arguing the field should shift from chasing individual-level causal effects toward theorizing platforms as collective, infrastructural, democratic systems — a reorientation made newly feasible (if still difficult) under DSA-era data regimes. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] documents an adjacent gap in the depolarization literature: recommendations in discussion sections are almost never grounded in the studies' own evidence, instead reflecting unexamined normative commitments (especially deliberative-democratic ideals). [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s radicalization review finds the same causal thinness — persistent reliance on exposure/engagement proxies given ethical and access constraints, with genuinely longitudinal designs still rare. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] reframes the causal-inference aspiration itself as somewhat misguided, arguing misinformation is a multilevel complex adaptive system that single-analogy, linear-causal thinking (virus, warfare, pollution) cannot capture, and that portfolio-style, climate-policy-like interventions are more appropriate than isolated causal fixes.

## The data-infrastructure crisis

Running beneath these substantive critiques is an infrastructural one: the platforms that generate the phenomena under study also gatekeep the data needed to study them, and that gate has been closing. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the resulting "Golden Age" and its collapse, documenting a 13% decline in Twitter-based publications following 2023 API paywalling; [[Freelon2024-sc]] periodizes two decades of shifting access regimes across six platforms, arguing access is now permanently contingent on platform discretion; [[Yang2026-tq]] shows the resulting plateau and decline empirically across social-science disciplines, with research concentrated on Twitter/X and Facebook despite their declining share of public attention. [[Rieder2025-ju]] extends the critique to YouTube's search API, finding it "forgetful by design" and poorly suited even to DSA compliance research; [[Bruns2026-pn]] documents how Meta's "clean room" successor to CrowdTangle excludes non-programmers and qualitative researchers, while [[Peters2026-mo]] and [[Pierri2026-ib]] analyze the DSA's Article 40 regime itself, showing that "data quality" and genuine researcher access remain contested, under-specified, and vulnerable to platform resistance despite regulatory promise. [[Lukito2026-nb]] ties these threads together with a "price, proficiency, permission" framework for understanding who gets to do election research at all. Collectively, these pieces argue that methodological rigor is not simply a matter of better study design but of structurally precarious access to the empirical substrate itself.

## Industry entanglement and the political economy of platform and AI research

Closely related is a growing literature on whether the field's dependence on platforms compromises its independence. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this directly: about half of high-profile social-media research has disclosable industry ties, mostly undisclosed, concentrated among a small set of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, and skewed toward user-focused topics (like misinformation sharing) that deflect attention from platform-level dynamics. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] situates this within the longer history of tobacco, fossil-fuel, and food-industry science, cataloging mechanisms — suppression, selective funding, performative collaboration, asymmetric statistical standards — through which industry shapes findings. [[Heiss2026-qv]] draws the same parallel and stresses that open-science practices like preregistration are insufficient safeguards when platforms still control data and question framing. [[Munger2025-cz]] again serves as a bridging case study of these dynamics in action. The critique extends to the AI-safety adjacent literature: [[Gillespie2026-aa]] shows that red-teaming of generative AI echoes the labor and value-setting pathologies of early content moderation, while [[Vertesi2026-lv]] argues that even well-intentioned accountability research can be captured by "decoys" that obscure the political economy underlying the AI industry. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a counter-model, arguing that participatory, lived-experience-involving methods can strengthen rather than merely legitimate the science of AI evaluation.

## Conceptual fragmentation: when disagreement is definitional

A further diagnosis is that much apparent empirical disagreement is actually conceptual. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows that divergent findings on echo chambers stem largely from inconsistent operationalizations rather than from the phenomenon itself varying. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] makes an analogous point about competing metaphors for misinformation. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] trace how the "fake news"/"disinformation"/"misinformation" vocabulary emerged hastily after 2016 and remains conceptually unsettled, with Bennett and Livingston urging scholars to see disinformation as symptomatic of institutional legitimacy crisis rather than a media-technological anomaly. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes further, arguing the field's true/false binary cannot capture content that is referentially and ethically problematic without being straightforwardly false, proposing narrative-theoretic tools as a corrective. [[Guo2026-fi]]'s conceptual review of the "non-political" label, while adjacent rather than squarely about misinformation, models the same kind of boundary-critique — showing how taken-for-granted categories (political/nonpolitical, true/false, online/offline) are often theoretically under-examined artifacts of disciplinary convention rather than natural kinds. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s finding that fewer than half of radicalization studies even define their central concept underscores how widespread this problem is.

## Borrowed lessons and methodological ways forward

Finally, several papers look outward and forward for remedies. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] explicitly imports lessons from the more mature field of climate psychology — behavioral measurement, systems thinking, cross-national generalizability, and collective scientific organization against vested interests — and [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy exemplifies the kind of large-scale, preregistered, multi-outcome design the field is urged to adopt. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study models a multi-stakeholder, anticipatory alternative to siloed single-study research, while [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] frames depolarization research as a civic responsibility demanding rigorous, cross-context testing. On the methods side, a parallel literature interrogates new computational tools before they become entrenched infrastructure: [[Balluff2026-if]] warns against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research, citing reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs; [[Carammia2024-sl90]] argues fine-tuned open-source models can match proprietary LLMs while preserving transparency and reproducibility; and [[Alizadeh2026-es]] shows that AI coding agents can now reproduce much published computational social science, raising new questions (and new risks of sycophantic specification search) for how the field verifies its own findings. Read as a whole, this body of work does not abandon the classic empirical and cognitive paradigm established by foundational studies — [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]], [[Kunda1990-cg]], [[Bail2018-fk]], [[Tsfati2020-uo]] — so much as insist that the field now needs to test, globalize, and operationally sharpen what that paradigm merely gestured toward.
