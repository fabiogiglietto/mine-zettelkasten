# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The field turns the mirror on itself

The papers gathered here mark a genre shift in misinformation and online-harms research: rather than adding another empirical data point about who believes what, they ask whether the accumulated evidence base is trustworthy, generalizable, or even asking the right questions. Taken together they trace an arc from foundational cognitive-science accounts of belief persistence ([[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]]) and early "science of fake news" agenda-setting ([[Lazer2018-mm]]) toward a much more self-critical, structurally aware second generation that interrogates measurement artifacts, industry capture, geographic parochialism, and the very metaphors the field uses to think.

## Diagnosing scale, bias, and blind spots in what counts as evidence

A recurring move is to check whether the field's empirical picture matches reality. [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Budak2024-ef]] argue that fake news and problematic content occupy a vanishingly small share of the actual media diet, and that public and scholarly alarm has outpaced what the evidence supports—[[Oswald2025-km]] extends this by showing that the visible "tip of the iceberg" of online political content, produced by a hyperactive minority, systematically distorts both citizens' and researchers' inferences about public opinion. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] quantifies this directly: fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world behavioral outcomes, a diagnosis echoed by [[Lyons2026-ca]]'s finding that low-credibility health exposure is rare but concentrated, complicating simple prevalence narratives. Several papers show that measurement infrastructure itself manufactures apparent findings: the 100-share censoring threshold in Meta's URL dataset creates spurious cross-country convergence ([[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]]) and inflates fake-news prevalence estimates fourfold ([[Allen2021-ai]]); NewsGuard's ratings are usable but only if researchers avoid brittle binary cutoffs ([[Luhring2025-od]]); YouTube's search API "forgets" content within weeks, undermining any retrospective study of platform events ([[Rieder2025-ju]]). Conceptual measurement is equally fraught: echo-chamber research disagrees largely because studies operationalize the phenomenon inconsistently ([[Hartmann2025-px]]), and claims of inter-state coordinated influence operations may not survive rigorous benchmarking against control data ([[Pante2025-pq]]).

## WEIRD-centrism and the false universality of findings

A second cluster interrogates geographic and cultural narrowness. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting the field's WEIRD bias, a concern empirically cashed out by [[Nenno2025-xa]], who shows news-value expressions in flagged misinformation diverge meaningfully between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than templates. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] operationalizes this insight into a comparative framework, showing that societal resilience to disinformation is a structural, macro-level property that clusters Northern/Western Europe apart from Southern Europe and marks the US as a genuinely exceptional, non-generalizable case. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the parallel argument for polarization research, and [[Bennett2025-xs]] critiques technocentric, individual-level disinformation paradigms for missing the institutional and organizational dynamics that actually drive democratic backsliding, proposing a "communication-as-organization" alternative grounded in comparative cases beyond the US.

## Borrowed lessons and better analogies

Rather than only diagnosing problems, several papers import solutions from more mature fields. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the clearest exemplar, distilling six lessons from climate psychology—on behavioral measurement, systems thinking, and collective scientific organization—directly transferable to misinformation research; [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy on climate messaging supplies the kind of large-scale, null-inclusive rigor being called for. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that the field's guiding metaphors (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each capture only part of a genuinely complex adaptive system and calls for portfolio-style interventions analogous to climate policy. [[Sadler2025-vu]] imports narratology and hermeneutic philosophy to move disinformation research beyond crude true/false binaries, while [[Ramos2026-qo]] proposes narrative monitoring and argument-checking as necessary complements to fact-checking. [[Matias2025-px]] draws on participatory science to argue that public involvement can strengthen, not just legitimate, the rigor of AI evaluation. [[Marwick2025-vx]] similarly complicates simple "exposure causes belief" radicalization models with a processual socialization account, a critique paralleled at scale by [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of online-radicalization research, which finds persistent gaps in longitudinal and causal evidence despite an "empirical turn."

## Industry entanglement and the politics of data access

A substantial subset interrogates the field's structural dependency on platforms. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, respectively, the mechanisms and the empirical extent of industry influence—finding roughly half of high-profile social media research has undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of scientists, editors, and reviewers, with citation and topic-selection consequences. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within the commercial-determinants-of-research literature (tobacco, pharma), while [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a granular case study of the Meta2020 partnership itself, arguing that even the most ambitious platform-academic collaboration produced narrow, non-generalizable estimates. The regulatory response—the EU's Digital Services Act—receives sustained scrutiny: [[Pierri2026-ib]] catalogs the legal and organizational barriers to Article 40 access, [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how "data quality" itself became a politically contested regulatory object, and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the empirical cost of the Twitter API's closure, documenting a measurable decline in scholarly output as a direct consequence of platform power.

## Methodological self-scrutiny and the discipline's future tools

Finally, a forward-looking strand asks how the field should study itself going forward. [[Yang2026-tq]] shows that social-media-based social science is a small, platform-skewed niche now contracting under API restrictions. [[Balluff2026-if]] cautions against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research, urging trade-off-aware, open-science-compatible methodological choices, while [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] explore AI's emerging role in reproducing and reviewing science itself—raising new questions about what "rigor" means when verification is automated. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study crystallizes much of this collective self-assessment, finding expert consensus that no single-level fix (individual, platform, institutional) suffices and that the field must anticipate rather than merely react to future harms. Together with reflective pieces like [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later retrospective and [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s finding that depolarization recommendations routinely outrun their evidentiary basis, and prompted by exhortations like [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] for scientists to actively engage rather than merely diagnose, this corpus collectively argues that misinformation research's next maturation step is epistemic housekeeping: confronting its own biases, industry entanglements, measurement artifacts, and cultural parochialism before it can credibly claim to explain—or fix—the problems it studies.
