# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The Field Turns the Mirror on Itself

A recurring move across this literature is disciplinary self-audit: rather than producing another effect-size or another case study, these papers ask what the accumulated body of misinformation and online-harms research actually licenses us to believe. The diagnosis is remarkably convergent. [[Budak2024-ef]] opens by cataloguing where public and even scholarly discourse has drifted from the evidence — on prevalence, on algorithmic causation, on societal-scale harm — a theme echoed by [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] and [[Lyons2026-ca]], whose platform-scale data show exposure to untrustworthy or low-credibility content is real but heavily concentrated in small subpopulations rather than diffuse and catastrophic. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies the structural explanation: a production-consumption gap means the loud, visible slice of online discourse that researchers and citizens alike treat as "public opinion" is itself an artifact of a skewed few. Together these pieces push back against alarmist framings while insisting the corrective is not complacency but better-specified claims about *who* is exposed, *how much*, and *with what consequence*.

## Measurement, Metaphor, and Conceptual Housekeeping

A second cluster interrogates the field's basic constructs. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows that decades of disagreement over whether echo chambers exist is substantially a byproduct of incompatible operationalizations rather than incompatible realities, a diagnosis [[Trilling2024-slc8]] radicalizes by arguing the echo-chamber/filter-bubble metaphor itself should be retired in favor of a feedback-loop framework that can accommodate self-correcting as well as self-reinforcing dynamics. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] performs a parallel critique at the level of root metaphor, showing how "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" each illuminate one level of a multilevel complex system while obscuring others — and that policy built on a single analogy can backfire. [[Sadler2025-vu]] extends this conceptual scrutiny to "narrative," arguing the field's intuitive, plot-summary use of the term collapses ethical and referential questions into a crude true/false binary. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds an analogous disconnect on the normative side: depolarization recommendations in the polarization literature are overwhelmingly unmoored from the studies' own evidence, resting instead on unexamined deliberative-democracy ideals. [[Mosca2026-yh]] adds that even the object "fake news" itself is sometimes better modeled as a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization than as a misinformation phenomenon per se. [[Luhring2025-od]] rounds out this thread with a rare methodological audit of infrastructure researchers take for granted — the NewsGuard database — showing how binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs, not underlying ratings, can silently distort published findings.

## Geographic, Demographic, and Topical Narrowness

A dominant meta-finding is WEIRD-centrism, named explicitly by [[Humprecht2025-ml]] as the frame for an entire special issue and empirically documented by [[Nenno2025-xa]], whose 24-country analysis shows that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation from the US and Brazil are outliers rather than representative baselines. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the identical argument for polarization research, and [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of online-radicalization scholarship finds the same skew toward UK/Germany/US contexts alongside persistent gaps in longitudinal and causal designs. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] argues the topical narrowness runs deeper than geography: misinformation research's fixation on health and politics misses how everyday users actually encounter falsehood, often via mainstream clickbait rather than fringe sources. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] converge on a related corrective from the qualitative side — that content- and exposure-centric models of belief adoption (the "redpill" as instant conversion, conspiracy belief as algorithmic capture) obscure the slower, identity- and community-based socialization processes that actually produce extremist or conspiracist commitment. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] and [[Bennett2025-xs]] extend the critique of individual-level, cognitive-proxy-heavy research: the former shows partisan-motivated *perceptions* of who is targeted by misinformation are themselves a a systematically biased phenomenon, while the latter argues that technocentric, individual-cognition paradigms have crowded out institutionalist analysis of how digital "surrogate organizations" reorganize party politics toward illiberalism.

## Borrowed Lessons and Prescriptions for Rigor

Several papers look outward for correctives. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the most direct: climate psychology, having weathered organized denial for decades, offers six transferable lessons — behavioral (not just attitudinal) outcome measurement, contextual generalizability, systems thinking, and collective scientific organization against vested interests. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] and [[Lieu2025-nl]] supply exactly the kind of large-scale, rigorously registered climate-adjacent evidence that thread calls for, showing modest, heterogeneous persuasion effects and no easy translation from attitude change to costly behavior — a sobering empirical anchor for claims made elsewhere in the misinformation literature. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes the "no silver bullet" lesson directly, producing an expert consensus that structural, multi-stakeholder interventions (platform governance, journalism ecosystems) outrank individual-level fixes like prebunking or fact-checking. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] and [[Tornberg2026-lc]] push the methodological reset further, arguing that under the DSA and amid the dissolution of the "social media" paradigm itself (algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, AI-mediated communication), causal-individual-effects frameworks are increasingly the wrong tool altogether, and infrastructural/collective-level analysis must take their place.

## Data, Industry, and the Political Economy of Knowledge Production

A distinct but overlapping set of papers interrogates the material conditions under which misinformation knowledge is produced. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, in parallel with tobacco- and fossil-fuel-industry precedents, how platform companies shape the evidence base through undisclosed funding, selective data access, and design bias — with roughly half of high-profile social media research carrying industry ties, mostly undisclosed. [[Munger2025-cz]] examines the Meta2020 partnership as a limit case, arguing that even the most ambitious industry-academic collaboration produced narrow, rapidly decaying estimates whose external validity cannot outrun the platforms' own pace of change, and that regulation—not more experiments—is the only durable fix. [[Yang2026-tq]] quantifies a related structural bias: empirical social-science reliance on Twitter/X and Facebook is wildly disproportionate to actual platform usage, a dependency now destabilized by post-API data restrictions. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes this political-economy critique to AI accountability research broadly, warning that critiques fixating on algorithmic particulars can function as "decoys" that legitimize, rather than challenge, underlying concentrations of corporate power. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Donovan2025-ws]] connect these infrastructural dynamics explicitly to democratic backsliding, framing platform business models and moderation retreats as structural — not incidental — drivers of harm. [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] contribute methodological correctives for specific subfields (attribution of coordinated manipulation; narrative-level rather than claim-level disinformation exposure), while [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] shows that even a foundational measurement convention — the "direct referrer" model of how platforms expose users to misinformation — has systematically undercounted platform effects by an order of magnitude, complicating simple platform-ban policy prescriptions.

## Generative AI as Both New Challenge and Meta-Scientific Risk

Finally, a growing subset reflects on how generative AI complicates the field's tools and its object of study simultaneously. [[Emilio2026-ik]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] extend the harms taxonomy toward "synthetic realities" and modality-congruent carryover effects, arguing that the very concept of discrete "fake" artifacts is becoming obsolete. Methodologically, [[Balluff2026-if]] cautions against unreflective LLM adoption in communication research itself, warning of reproducibility loss, corporate dependency, and demographic bias — concerns partially tempered by [[Brown2025-jk]]'s finding that LLM annotation bias is dataset- rather than model-specific and modest relative to item difficulty. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] close the loop by turning AI tools onto the scientific process itself, showing coding agents can now reproduce published social-science findings with striking accuracy (raising new questions about verification and sycophancy) and that automated review pipelines are already being piloted at major venues — suggesting that the future of "reflecting on the state of the field" may itself become partially automated.
