# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## Diagnosing a Field in Crisis of Confidence

A striking number of these papers share a common move: rather than adding another empirical data point to misinformation research, they turn the field's own tools back on itself. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] opens this self-examination most explicitly, arguing that misinformation research is a younger, less mature sibling of climate psychology and should borrow its hard-won lessons about vested interests, systems thinking, and behavioral (not merely cognitive) outcomes. [[Mahl2026-hc]] complements this with an anticipatory, Delphi-based assessment showing that experts themselves see the field as fragmented, overly individualistic, and poorly equipped for what is coming (deepfakes, "burner" accounts, a shift from misinformed citizens to disengaged cynics). Together with the Nature editorial [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] urging polarization researchers to take applied responsibility for their findings, these pieces frame the entire topic area: this is a field stepping back to ask whether it is asking the right questions, in the right places, with the right tools.

## The WEIRD Problem and the Limits of Generalization

A recurring diagnosis is that findings are disproportionately drawn from the US, UK, and a handful of wealthy democracies, then silently assumed to travel. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] names this directly as the organizing rationale for a special issue correcting WEIRD-centrism, and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the same argument for polarization research specifically, calling for disciplinary integration alongside geographic equity. [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies some of the clearest empirical teeth for this critique: analyzing user-flagged misinformation across twenty-four countries, it finds that the news-values typologies built from Western newsrooms do not neatly capture non-WEIRD media environments, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than representative cases. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] offers an earlier structural corrective, showing that "resilience" to disinformation is a property of national media systems—and that the US is a poor stand-in for "Western democracies" generally, since it forms its own cluster distinct even from other polarized, low-resilience states like those of Southern Europe. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research echoes the pattern at the level of an entire adjacent literature: Western-skewed samples, under-theorized online/offline boundaries, and a near-total absence of non-English and non-country-specific work.

## Overreliance on Cognitive Proxies and the Mismatch with Real-World Harm

A second thread interrogates what the field has chosen to measure. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] reports that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure real-world, high-impact behavioral outcomes—an indictment echoed by [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], whose foundational reviews of truth discernment and the "continued influence effect" remain primarily grounded in laboratory belief and recall measures rather than downstream behavior. [[Budak2024-ef]] goes further, arguing that public discourse has drifted from the actual evidence base: exposure to problematic content is concentrated in small subpopulations, algorithms are not the dominant driver commonly assumed, and broad societal harms attributed to social media rest on weaker causal footing than alarmist narratives suggest. [[Allen2020-nj]] operationalizes this skepticism at ecosystem scale, showing that "fake news" occupies roughly 0.15% of Americans' daily media diet once television is included—suggesting the field's intense focus on online fakery may be disproportionate to its share of the actual information diet, with ordinary news bias and news avoidance as plausible rivals for public misinformedness. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] reframes the proxy problem from the audience side: when researchers stop imposing topic-driven definitions and instead ask what people themselves flag as misinformation in daily life, "hot button" political/health content turns out to be only part of the picture, with everyday economic anxieties and mainstream clickbait dominating lived experience. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] diagnoses a parallel conceptual problem: the dominant virus, warfare, and pollution analogies for misinformation each capture only one level of a multilevel complex system, and interventions built on a single analogy (e.g., simplistic media-literacy training) can backfire by increasing generalized distrust.

## Evidence Gaps: Causality, Measurement Inconsistency, and Instability

A third cluster catalogs methodological fragility beneath the field's empirical claims. [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review of 129 echo-chamber studies concludes that disagreement about whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization, not a reflection of divergent underlying reality. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds an analogous disconnect in depolarization scholarship: recommendations in discussion sections are almost never grounded in the papers' own evidence, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits a widely used infrastructure of measurement itself—the NewsGuard database—showing that while continuous trust scores have stabilized since 2022, binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can swing substantive conclusions dramatically depending on which snapshot a researcher happens to use. [[Allen2021-ai]] delivers a similarly technical but consequential warning: privacy-preserving censoring thresholds in the Facebook URLs dataset inflate apparent fake-news consumption by roughly fourfold relative to a representative panel, a bias orthogonal to the differential-privacy noise researchers more commonly worry about. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership crystallizes the deeper issue: even extraordinarily well-resourced, pre-registered collaborative science produces narrow, quickly decaying estimates on platforms that change faster than social science can characterize them, undermining both "temporal" and "poetic" validity and raising doubts about whether reactive empirical study of platforms is viable at all.

## Industry Entanglement and the Political Economy of Knowledge

Several papers argue that the field's evidentiary gaps are not merely methodological accidents but structural consequences of who controls the data. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Heiss2026-qv]] both draw the tobacco/pharma/fossil-fuel analogy explicitly, arguing that platforms' monopoly over the data needed to study them creates asymmetric, often undisclosed, industry influence unlike anything in those older fields' independent data environments. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] substantiates this empirically at scale: roughly half of high-profile social media research in top journals carries disclosable industry ties, most undisclosed, concentrated among a small set of repeatedly-engaged scientists who also populate editorial and review roles, yielding an estimated "industrial saturation" of about 80% once authors, editors, and reviewers are combined—with topical bias toward user-level misinformation research and away from platform-dynamics research that might implicate platforms more directly. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] extends this critique to AI accountability scholarship, arguing that even well-intentioned fairness and transparency research can function as a "decoy" that legitimizes the underlying political economy of AI rather than challenging it. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a partial counter-model, proposing that participatory, lived-experience-driven evaluation can strengthen rather than merely democratize the rigor of AI science—suggesting one route out of the data-dependency trap is structural inclusion rather than platform concession.

## A Field Overtaken by Its Object

A final set of papers asks whether the field's foundational unit of analysis is becoming obsolete. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues that "social media" itself is dissolving into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private micro-communities, and AI-mediated communication, each demanding different concepts and methods than the network-analytic toolkit built for the social-graph era. [[Balluff2026-if]] raises parallel concerns about LLM adoption in communication research methods, warning against uncritical reliance on corporate models that introduce reproducibility, bias, and environmental costs the field has not reckoned with. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] illustrates the scale of disruption from the opposite direction, describing an AI system built to keep pace with a submission volume increasingly generated by AI itself—a vivid instance of the meta-scientific pressures these reflective papers are trying to diagnose. [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later retrospective ties these technological shifts to political economy, arguing that the harms she named in 2015 have intensified rather than abated despite a decade of critical scholarship, and that critique alone has proven insufficient.

## Rethinking Concepts, Not Just Methods

A smaller but important group interrogates the field's core concepts rather than its data or incentives. [[Sadler2025-vu]] contends that "narrative" has been used too loosely in disinformation studies, proposing a hermeneutic-realist and narrative-ethics framework to assess content that is misleading without being straightforwardly false. [[Marwick2025-vx]] challenges the popular "exposure equals belief" model of radicalization with qualitative evidence that extremist belief adoption is typically a gradual socialization process in which disinformation functions as legitimating "evidence" rather than a triggering cause. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]], as earlier framing statements, show how much conceptual work (defining disinformation against misinformation and propaganda, retiring "fake news" as an analytic category) has already had to be done and redone—evidence that terminological churn is a long-standing, not merely contemporary, feature of this research area. [[Oswald2025-km]]'s account of the production-consumption gap rounds out this conceptual cluster, warning that both citizens and researchers risk mistaking the loud, unrepresentative "tip of the iceberg" of visible online discourse for public opinion writ large—a distortion that undercuts inferences drawn throughout the literature surveyed here.

## Toward a More Rigorous, Reflexive Field

Read together, these papers trace an arc from complaint to constructive proposal. The complaints converge: WEIRD-centrism, cognitive-proxy myopia, unstable measurement infrastructures, undisclosed industry entanglement, and concepts (social media, narrative, radicalization, fake news) outpaced by their objects. The proposed remedies are less uniform but cluster around a few moves: import rigor and organizational resilience from more mature adjacent fields such as climate psychology ([[Spampatti2026-kx]], with [[Voelkel2026-lc]] offering a model of the large-scale, pre-registered megastudy this borrowing could look like); demand structural reform of data access and disclosure ([[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]]); broaden the empirical base geographically and methodologically ([[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Falkenberg2026-ka]], [[Nenno2025-xa]]); and reorient the field's basic vocabulary toward the sociotechnical systems people now actually inhabit ([[Tornberg2026-lc]], [[Frischlich2025-vn]]). The through-line is a field asking, with unusual candor, whether its first fifteen years of post-2016 urgency produced generalizable knowledge or primarily a well-cited but narrow and self-reinforcing research tradition—and concluding that meaningful progress will require not just more studies, but different studies, asked differently, by a more organized and more scrutinized research community.
