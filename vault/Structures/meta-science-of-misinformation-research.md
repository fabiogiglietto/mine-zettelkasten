# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## A field turning its gaze inward

A growing cluster of papers treats the misinformation/online-harms field itself as the object of study rather than any particular platform, belief, or intervention. Several take explicit stock of where the field has come from and where it has gone wrong. [[Lazer2018-mm]] remains an early landmark here, diagnosing the "science of fake news" as institutionally fragmented and short on evidence of prevalence and long-term effects. A decade on, [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Allen2020-nj]] argue that public and even scholarly discourse has drifted from what the evidence actually shows: fake news is a vanishingly small share of the media diet, algorithms are not the primary driver of exposure, and the causal link between social media and societal harm is weaker than alarmist narratives suggest. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] performs a parallel corrective at the psychological level, showing that inattention and failures of reasoning—not partisan motivated cognition—best explain belief and sharing of false news, challenging a dominant explanatory framework from within.

## Diagnosing structural and geographic bias

A second strand catalogues *where* the field's evidentiary gaps lie. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] and [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] both indict WEIRD-centrism—in misinformation and polarization research respectively—calling for comparative, cross-national designs. [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes this critique empirically, showing that "news values" findings from Western media systems do not transfer cleanly to non-WEIRD contexts, with the US and Brazil emerging as outliers rather than exemplars. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century, 115-country analysis of trust in news similarly dismantles a US-centric "golden age of trust" narrative, showing divergent trajectories by media-freedom regime. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] extends the critique methodologically: topic-driven, politically/health-focused misinformation research misses how ordinary people actually encounter contested content in everyday domains like personal finance. Together these papers argue that the field's empirical base is doubly narrow—geographically and topically—undermining claims to generalizable knowledge.

## Measurement, proxies, and the evidence base

Closely related is a cluster interrogating core methodological proxies. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows that disagreement over whether echo chambers even exist is substantially an artifact of inconsistent conceptualization and operationalization rather than divergent realities. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds that depolarization recommendations in the polarization literature are largely disconnected from the evidence researchers themselves produce, resting instead on unexamined normative commitments (chiefly deliberative-democratic ideals). [[Luhring2025-od]] offers a granular audit of a widely used measurement tool (NewsGuard), showing how binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can distort downstream findings even when underlying data are stable. [[Allen2021-ai]] demonstrates a parallel measurement pathology in big data itself: privacy-preserving censoring thresholds in the Social Science One Facebook dataset inflate fake-news prevalence estimates several-fold relative to representative panels. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research flags the same shortfall at a conceptual level—robust causal and longitudinal evidence remains scarce, with most work substituting exposure or engagement proxies for genuine causal process. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s bibliometric mapping shows how disproportionately the entire empirical social-media literature has clustered on Twitter/X and Facebook relative to actual platform usage, a distortion now destabilized by API closures. Collectively, these pieces argue that the field has mistaken convenient proxies—cognitive measures, specific platforms, binary labels—for the underlying phenomena of interest.

## Industry entanglement as an evidentiary threat

A distinct but related line of self-critique concerns the field's structural dependence on the very companies it studies. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion piece [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document, with Oreskes/Proctor-style parallels to tobacco and fossil-fuel science, how platforms' monopoly over data access produces pervasive undisclosed industry ties among authors, editors, and reviewers, skewing both topic selection (toward individual-level "misinformation sharing" rather than platform dynamics) and citation/attention patterns. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this as a general problem of platform-exclusive data control and proposes DSA-style regulatory remedies alongside stronger research-on-research norms. [[Pierri2026-ib]] examines the practical difficulties of the DSA's Article 40 data-access regime from the inside, cataloguing the asymmetries—legal, technical, financial—that still favor platforms over independent researchers. [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific reading of the Meta2020 partnership makes the sharpest version of this argument: even well-resourced, methodologically ambitious academic-industry collaboration cannot keep pace with platforms that change faster than science can validate, exposing a structural mismatch between empirical social science's temporal rhythms and its object of study.

## Conceptual and theoretical stock-taking

Several papers step back even further, questioning the field's organizing metaphors and theoretical commitments. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies each illuminate only one level of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive system, producing policy interventions that backfire when applied in isolation. [[Sadler2025-vu]] contends that "narrative" has been used naively in disinformation studies, collapsed into true/false binaries that cannot capture content that is referentially and ethically problematic without being straightforwardly false. [[Bennett2018-ys]] and its sequel [[Bennett2025-xs]] press a related institutionalist critique: individual-level, cognitively-focused "technocentric" paradigms (which large funding streams have incentivized) elide how disinformation becomes organizationally consequential, pulling parties toward illiberalism—a gap addressed by integrating "connective action" and surrogate-organization theory. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] extends this political-economy critique to AI research broadly, arguing that accountability scholarship is repeatedly diverted into "decoy" debates (ontological, inevitability, disruption, safety, regulatory framings) that obscure the financialized networks of power actually driving AI's deployment. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] historicizes the field's own declinist mood, showing how "internet decline narratives" function rhetorically and politically while risking a US-centric, Manichean history that erases user agency and non-Western experience. [[So2026-up]] makes an analogous conceptual move for generative AI content, arguing the real scarcity produced by "slop" is not content but reliable evaluative signals, a reframing meant to replace inadequate detection/spam/misinformation categories. [[Emilio2026-ik]] similarly argues the GenAI threat is best reconceived not as discrete deepfakes but as "synthetic realities" undermining epistemic security at an institutional level, a conceptual expansion mirroring the field's broader move from artifact-level to systems-level diagnosis. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] presses the same systems-level reorientation onto political communication research directly, urging a shift from individual causal-effects paradigms toward platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, enabled by DSA-era data regimes. [[Oswald2025-km]]'s "production-consumption gap" argument offers a complementary caution: both citizens and researchers risk inferring public opinion from a small, unrepresentative slice of vocal online producers.

## Borrowing from more mature fields, and charting forward

Facing these diagnoses, several papers look outward for templates of rigor. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] is the most systematic example, distilling six lessons from climate psychology—on behavioral (not just cognitive) measurement, contextual generalizability, systems thinking, and collective researcher organization against vested interests—explicitly because misinformation research is younger and less battle-tested against organized opposition. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy exemplifies the kind of large-scale, preregistered, real-world-outcome design that Spampatti et al. recommend, revealing that even the most-cited persuasion strategies produce only small, behaviorally inert effects—a sobering empirical complement to the field's aspirations for rigor. [[Bravo2026-ue]] shows a comparable Western-centric, methodologically narrow pattern in climate-visualization research, reinforcing that these structural problems are not unique to misinformation studies. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes a consciously anticipatory, multi-stakeholder corrective to the field's historical siloing, deriving research and policy priorities from expert consensus across civil society, platforms, and science rather than individual-level fixes alone. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] closes the normative loop, arguing that researchers who diagnose societal fracture bear some responsibility to test and deploy depolarizing interventions rather than only documenting decline.

## An emerging consensus on direction

Read together, these papers converge on a shared diagnosis: the misinformation/online-harms field has overrelied on cognitive proxies, Western and platform-specific data, individual-level causal snapshots, and industry-mediated access, while underinvesting in longitudinal, cross-national, systems-level, and real-world-behavioral evidence. The prescriptions vary—borrow from climate psychology, demand regulatory data access, rebuild conceptual categories, organize scientifically against vested interests—but they share a conviction that the field's next maturation step is genuinely reflexive: not better answers to old questions, but better-specified questions, more honest evidentiary standards, and clearer accounting for whose interests (corporate, national, disciplinary) have shaped the evidence base so far.
