# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The inherited paradigm and its discontents

The field's working assumptions were set early and have proven durable: misinformation is a discrete, identifiable artifact; it corrupts belief through identifiable cognitive mechanisms; and correcting belief is the natural unit of intervention. [[Lazer2018-mm]]'s "science of fake news" crystallized this agenda, calling for interdisciplinary study of why people believe fabricated content and how corrections might work, while candidly admitting how little was known about prevalence or downstream political effects. [[Allcott2017-yz]] supplied an early empirical corrective, modeling fake news as a market equilibrium and showing that average exposure during the 2016 election was far too modest to plausibly explain Trump's victory—an argument radicalized by [[Allen2020-nj]], who recontextualized "fake news" against the entire media diet and found it a vanishing fraction (~0.15%) of daily consumption, dwarfed by television and by ordinary news bias or avoidance. [[Budak2024-ef]] later generalized this skepticism into a programmatic claim: public and policy discourse has drifted away from the evidence base on prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal harm. Underneath these prevalence debates sits a cognitive-psychological engine—[[Kunda1990-cg]]'s account of motivated, directionally biased memory search, and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]]'s catalog of why corrections so often fail to dislodge belief (the continued-influence effect)—that supplied the theoretical vocabulary for decades of correction-centered research. [[Pennycook2021-jq]] partially overturned this consensus from within, arguing that poor truth discernment reflects inattention and lazy reasoning rather than partisan motivated cognition, exposing a live fault line in how the field explains susceptibility. Meanwhile, [[Bennett2018-ys]], [[Freelon2020-yp]], and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] pushed back against the "isolated fake article" framing altogether, arguing that disinformation is systemic (a "disinformation order" rooted in institutional distrust) and that mainstream media themselves are a chief amplifier of the falsehoods they try to debunk—an irony that individual-level correction research is poorly equipped to capture.

## Measuring what matters: behavioral, everyday, and real-world evidence

A second line of self-critique targets measurement rather than theory: the field has relied on cognitive proxies (recognition, recall, belief ratings) far more than on real-world behavior. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] makes this explicit by importing lessons from climate psychology, noting that fewer than 1% of misinformation studies measure high-impact behavioral outcomes, and arguing for motivational and systems-level analysis alongside cognitive ones. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages operationalizes this lesson directly, showing that even well-replicated attitudinal effects evaporate when the outcome is costly behavior (donations), exposing an attitude–behavior gap the field has often glossed over. [[Lyons2026-ca]] and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] answer the call empirically: linking survey data to passive web-tracking reveals that low-credibility health exposure is rare but concentrated among older, conspiracist-leaning users via non-social, non-search referral pathways, while a diary study of Australian adults shows that lay perceptions of misinformation extend into "boring" everyday domains—business, celebrity, crime—dominated by mainstream clickbait rather than fringe sources, unsettling the topic-driven (health/politics/science) default of the literature. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s audit of NewsGuard similarly interrogates a widely used behavioral proxy—source-level trustworthiness scores—showing that binary classification choices, not just underlying reality, can swing findings. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] and [[Bravo2026-ue]] round out this cluster by diagnosing conceptual and methodological narrowness from different angles: Frischlich argues that virus/warfare/pollution analogies each capture only one level of a multilevel complex system and recommends climate-style portfolio interventions, while Bravo's review of climate visualization research finds the same disciplinary habit of favoring traditional media and Western cases over social media and computational image analysis—suggesting that "borrowing from climate" cuts both ways, since climate communication research has its own gaps to confront.

## WEIRD-centrism and the push for cross-national, cross-cultural generalizability

A recurring diagnosis across the corpus is that the evidentiary base is disproportionately American, English-language, and Western. [[Humprecht2025-ml]]'s special-issue introduction names this directly, calling for comparative, cross-platform, interdisciplinary work; [[Nenno2025-xa]] operationalizes the critique empirically, finding that Western news-value typologies only partially travel to non-WEIRD Facebook contexts, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than exemplars. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] offers an earlier structural counterpart, building a macro-level "resilience" framework across eighteen Western democracies and showing the US itself is a poor proxy for "the West," let alone the globe. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends this argument to polarization research generally, calling for integration across disciplines and genuinely global data, a concern [[Fletcher2026-lv]] substantiates at scale: fifty years of trust-in-news data show declines are real but not universal, with "Not Free" regimes sometimes bucking the trend entirely—undercutting any single global narrative of institutional collapse. [[Mota2026-sl59]] extends the WEIRD-bias critique to a newer object, showing Global North and Global South media frame AI in starkly different emotional registers (threat versus opportunity), while [[Tonneau2025-bv]] demonstrates that linguistic inequities are baked into platform infrastructure itself: content moderation workforces are allocated disproportionately to English over Global South languages, meaning entire user populations are effectively unmoderated—a structural, not merely a sampling, form of WEIRD bias.

## Conceptual fragmentation: defining, modeling, and reconceptualizing the phenomena

Many contributions diagnose not bias in sampling but disorder in the concepts themselves. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] argues that creator-intent-based definitions of "fake news" cannot capture how falsehood actually propagates through a hybrid media system and proposes shifting attention to propagators' judgments instead. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows the same disorder in echo-chamber research, where disagreement about whether the phenomenon even exists stems largely from incompatible operationalizations rather than diverging realities—a diagnosis [[Trilling2024-slc8]] answers by proposing to retire the echo-chamber/filter-bubble metaphor altogether in favor of a feedback-loop framework that can explain reinforcing dynamics without predicting runaway radicalization. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes the reconceptualization further still, arguing the field should move past individual-level causal effects entirely toward studying platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure, a call that resonates with [[van-Eck2026-xg]]'s finding that depolarization recommendations in the polarization literature are rarely grounded in the evidence those same papers generate, instead reflecting unexamined normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research makes a parallel point about definitional discipline: fewer than half of studies even define "radicalization," and online/offline separation is increasingly untenable, yet causal and longitudinal evidence remains scarce. At the level of content itself, [[Sadler2025-vu]], [[Marwick2025-vx]], and [[Prochaska2026-pr]] argue that disinformation studies has treated "narrative," "exposure," and "rumor" too loosely—proposing, respectively, hermeneutic-narratological tools to evaluate stories beyond true/false binaries, a "processual" rather than conversion-event model of extremist belief adoption, and a structural "grammar" of recurring rumor tropes that could make the field's qualitative content analysis more systematic and predictive.

## The political economy of platform research: industry capture and data dependency

A sharply critical strand argues the field cannot be read independently of the industry it studies. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] together document, conceptually and empirically, how platforms shape the evidence base: roughly half of high-profile social-media papers have undisclosed industry ties, a small cluster of repeatedly engaged scientists (and editors) dominate the literature, and industry-tied work skews toward user-blaming topics like misinformation sharing while platform-dynamics research is underrepresented—mirroring historical industry capture in tobacco, pharma, and food science. [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames this structurally: because platforms exclusively control the data needed to study them, researcher independence is uniquely fragile compared to other regulated industries, a vulnerability [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific critique of the Meta2020 partnership illustrates concretely—underpowered, conceptually incoherent, and irreproducible precisely because the platform being studied changes faster than peer review can keep pace. Methodological artifacts compound the problem: [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Allen2021-ai]] both show that privacy-preserving engineering choices in Meta's URL Shares Dataset (the 100-public-shares threshold) introduce systematic, non-obvious biases—inflating fake-news prevalence estimates and masking or mimicking cross-country "trends" that are really algorithmic artifacts—meaning that even well-intentioned industry-academic collaborations can quietly distort what the field believes it has learned.

## The post-API infrastructure crisis and the regulatory turn

Closely related is a wave of papers documenting the collapse of the open-API research infrastructure and assessing whether regulation can rebuild it. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] quantifies the toll: a "Golden Age" of Twitter-based scholarship, growing 25% annually, stagnated after 2023's API paywall and declined in 2024, while Bastos2025-ya offers an elegiac disciplinary history of "Twitter studies" as a research infrastructure now gone. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes the pattern across social science, showing social-media-based empirical research plateaued and declined in 2022–2024 as platform access tightened, with research attention persistently misaligned with actual platform popularity (Twitter/X overstudied, YouTube understudied). Into this vacuum steps the EU's Digital Services Act, evaluated ambivalently: [[Peters2026-mo]] shows that "data quality" was barely mentioned in official DSA texts and had to be fought for by academic and NGO stakeholders against platform resistance, while [[Bruns2026-pn]]'s audit of Meta's and ProQuest's "clean room" access models finds that even where access exists, it is so restrictive—web-disconnected, code-only, data-deleting—that it structurally excludes qualitative, longitudinal, and under-resourced researchers. [[Rieder2025-ju]]'s audit of YouTube's search API supplies a cautionary case study of exactly the kind of infrastructure DSA compliance research will depend on: the API is "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay and irreproducibility that undermine both historical research and systemic-risk assessments the DSA itself requires.

## New tools, new uncertainties: LLMs, AI agents, and computational methods

A final methodological reckoning concerns the rapid, largely unreflective adoption of large language models within the field's own toolkit. [[Balluff2026-if]] catalogs the hidden costs—reproducibility fragility, corporate dependency, demographic and linguistic bias, environmental cost—of using commercial LLMs for text analysis, synthetic data, and simulated experiments, counseling a "least resource-intensive" default and open-weight alternatives. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] offers a partial technical answer, showing that small, fine-tuned open-source models can match or beat proprietary giants on social-science classification tasks while preserving transparency and reproducibility. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] raises the stakes further by showing AI coding agents can now reproduce published social-science findings with striking accuracy, but also that they are vulnerable to "sycophantic" specification search under confirmatory prompting—suggesting LLMs could become as much a threat to as a tool for research integrity. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a different, more constructive model for rigor: public and lived-experience involvement in AI evaluation improves, rather than merely legitimizes, scientific assessment. [[Emilio2026-ik]] widens the lens to the harms these tools enable, arguing that generative AI's deepest risk is not isolated deepfakes but "synthetic realities" that corrode the very possibility of verification—a problem the field's existing toolkit, built for an earlier media environment, is not yet equipped to study.

## Zooming out: historiography, democratic stakes, and calls for collective science

The collection closes on essays that step back furthest, asking what twenty-plus years of research and critique have actually bought, and what should come next. [[Baym2026-tr]] revisits her own decade-old manifesto to conclude that the threats she identified have only worsened, and calls for interdisciplinary, collaborative intervention rather than critique for its own sake. [[Hegarty2026-qr]] historicizes this very impulse, analyzing "internet decline narratives" as a genre that risks reinstating US-centric corporate determinism and erasing user agency even as it names real harms. [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Bennett2025-xs]] locate the field's stakes squarely in democratic backsliding, with Bennett and Livingston arguing that the dominant "technocentric" paradigm—focused on individual cognitive effects and consuming the bulk of mis/disinformation research funding—has obscured the institutional and organizational dynamics (digital surrogate organizations) that actually transformed a major party; [[Oswald2025-km]] adds a methodological wrinkle to this democratic story, showing that the production-consumption gap on social media distorts inferences about public opinion for citizens and researchers alike. Finally, [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]] gesture toward what a more mature, coordinated field might look like: a Nature editorial urging scientists to treat depolarization as a civic responsibility requiring rigorous testing, and a 47-expert Delphi study concluding that no single intervention—individual, platform, or institutional—suffices, and that misinformation research's future depends on exactly the kind of cross-sector, multi-level coordination that [[Spampatti2026-kx]] argued climate psychology has already been forced to learn.
