# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The field turns the mirror on itself

A striking feature of recent misinformation scholarship is its growing willingness to interrogate its own foundations rather than add another empirical data point. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] crystallizes this turn by borrowing explicitly from climate psychology — a field that matured faster under organized denial — to argue that misinformation research over-relies on cognitive proxies, under-measures real-world behavioural outcomes (fewer than 1% of studies do so), and remains parochially WEIRD. This critique does not stand alone: it resonates with [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study of 47 experts, who likewise conclude that individual-level fixes (prebunking, literacy, fact-checking) are rated less important than platform- and institution-level interventions, and that the field is fragmented and insufficiently anticipatory. Together these pieces mark a shift from "what do people believe and why" toward "what should researchers actually be measuring, and at what level."

## Geographic and topical narrowness

A recurring diagnosis is that the evidence base is geographically and topically skewed. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting WEIRD-centrism, while [[Nenno2025-xa]] empirically demonstrates that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation differ — if modestly — between WEIRD and non-WEIRD countries, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than archetypes. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] extends this critique to polarization research, arguing for integration across disciplines and genuine global representation rather than US-centric generalization. [[Humprecht2020-gd]]'s earlier comparative framework for "resilience to disinformation" already showed empirically that the US is a uniquely vulnerable outlier, undermining the common practice of treating American findings as universal. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] pushes the critique in a different direction, showing via diary methodology that everyday misinformation is dominated by mainstream/alternative news and mundane topics like economics — far from the hot-button, fringe-source imaginary that structures most research agendas. [[Fletcher2026-lv]]'s half-century cross-national analysis of trust in news similarly punctures a Western myth (the "Golden Age" of trust) and shows authoritarian contexts moving in the opposite direction from liberal democracies, a finding invisible to single-country designs.

## Conceptual and methodological fragmentation

Several papers argue that disagreement in the literature reflects conceptual sloppiness as much as empirical reality. [[Hartmann2025-px]] shows that contradictory echo-chamber findings stem largely from inconsistent operationalizations rather than divergent underlying phenomena. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] finds an analogous disconnect in polarization research: depolarization recommendations are almost never grounded in the evidence presented, resting instead on implicit normative commitments to deliberative democracy. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] diagnoses a related problem at the level of metaphor, arguing that "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" analogies each illuminate only one layer of a multilevel complex system and can produce backfiring interventions when taken as totalizing frames. [[Sadler2025-vu]] makes a parallel move for "narrative," arguing disinformation studies treats story meaning as self-evident and collapses it into true/false binaries, missing referential and ethical nuance that a hermeneutic-realist approach could recover. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] represent earlier attempts to discipline the field's vocabulary — distinguishing disinformation from misinformation and propaganda, and reframing "fake news" as a symptom of institutional decline (a "disinformation order") rather than a discrete technological pathology — groundwork that the later conceptual critiques build on and complicate.

## Questioning the evidence of harm and its causal basis

A parallel strand asks whether the field has correctly characterized the scale and causal structure of the problem at all. [[Allen2020-nj]] and [[Budak2024-ef]] both argue that public and even scholarly discourse exaggerates prevalence and algorithmic causation relative to what data actually show — fake news is a vanishingly small share of the whole media diet, and exposure/sharing is concentrated among tiny minorities of users, a point reinforced quantitatively by [[Lyons2026-ca]] for health misinformation and by [[Tsfati2020-uo]], who shows mainstream media, not fringe sites, are the primary vector by which most citizens encounter false stories. [[Allen2021-ai]] offers a methodological corrective underpinning these claims: widely used "big data" (the Facebook URLs dataset) is itself systematically biased by privacy-driven censoring thresholds, inflating apparent fake-news prevalence by roughly 4x — a cautionary tale about taking industry-mediated data at face value. [[Munger2025-cz]] extends this skepticism to causal-experimental gold standards themselves, arguing that the Meta2020 partnership's field experiments, however methodologically careful, cannot achieve temporal or "poetic" validity on platforms that change faster than science can study them — a structural argument for regulation requiring platforms to self-disclose effects rather than relying on episodic academic study. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] pushes further, proposing that the field abandon its narrow causal-effects paradigm altogether in favor of studying platform collective behaviour as democratic infrastructure, newly tractable under the EU's DSA data-access regime. The foundational cognitive-science work of [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Kunda1990-cg]], and [[Pennycook2021-jq]] anchors much of this debate: the shift away from politically-motivated-reasoning accounts toward attention/reasoning-deficit accounts of susceptibility ([[Pennycook2021-jq]]) and the documented difficulty of correcting misinformation ([[Lewandowsky2012-vn]]) are precisely the individual-level, cognitively-focused paradigms that later papers (Spampatti, Bennett) argue have been overextended relative to institutional and structural explanations.

## Industry entanglement as a meta-problem

A distinct but increasingly central critique concerns the research infrastructure itself. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical audit [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] document that roughly half of high-profile social-media research has undisclosed industry ties, that this influence is concentrated among a small clique of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, and that it skews research topically toward user-focused questions (e.g., misinformation sharing) while platform-level dynamics go understudied — with industry-tied work receiving roughly double the citations and policy attention. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within a broader comparative frame (tobacco, pharma, food industries), arguing that open-science safeguards like preregistration are insufficient when platforms control both data access and research framing. [[Luhring2025-od]]'s granular audit of NewsGuard — showing how binary trustworthy/untrustworthy cutoffs can drastically distort downstream findings depending on database snapshot — exemplifies the kind of infrastructural vulnerability this literature warns about, even for tools treated as neutral. [[Yang2026-tq]]'s systematic review quantifies the resulting fragility directly: empirical social-media research has plateaued and declined since 2022 as API access has closed, concentrating a generation of findings on Twitter/X and Facebook data that is no longer representative of actual platform use. [[Vertesi2026-lv]] generalizes the warning to AI research, arguing that critiques fixated on "decoy" debates (ontology, inevitability, safety, regulation) inadvertently legitimize the political economy they aim to hold accountable — a cautionary parallel for misinformation researchers at risk of debating definitions while structural power consolidates. [[Matias2025-px]] offers a constructive counterpoint, arguing that participatory, lived-experience-involving methods can improve rather than merely democratize the rigor of sociotechnical evaluation (including of AI systems), suggesting one path out of purely extractive platform dependency.

## Emerging frontiers: scale, automation, and adjacent fields

Several papers gesture toward where the field's infrastructure and scope might expand next. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization research finds the same causal and longitudinal evidence gaps diagnosed elsewhere, alongside a useful conceptual move — treating online and offline radicalization as inseparable — that could inform misinformation research's own online/offline divide. [[Marwick2025-vx]] complicates simplistic "exposure equals belief" models with qualitative evidence of gradual socialization, cautioning against the same proxy-driven shortcuts Spampatti and colleagues warn about. [[Bennett2025-xs]] argues for integrating technocentric (disinformation/algorithm-focused) and institutionalist (party/elite-focused) explanations of democratic backsliding, directly criticizing the individual-level cognitive paradigm's dominance and its outsized research funding relative to institutional analysis. Meanwhile [[Alizadeh2026-es]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] point to a different kind of reflexivity: as AI coding agents begin reproducing social-science findings and automating peer review, the field's own evidentiary infrastructure — replication, review, verification — is itself being reshaped by the same technologies it studies. [[Oswald2025-km]]'s "production-consumption gap" argument and [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later reassessment of social media's structural harms both serve as bookending reminders that the visible, researchable slice of online behaviour — whether vocal minorities or critiqued platforms — may systematically mislead both citizens and the scholars studying them, reinforcing the collection's overarching message: the next phase of rigor requires turning as much scrutiny on the research enterprise as on the phenomenon it studies.
