# Reflections on the State of Misinformation and Online Harm Research

## The self-critical turn

A striking number of papers filed here are not studies of misinformation but studies of the misinformation-research enterprise itself. [[Spampatti2026-kx]] sets the tone by asking what a younger, embattled field can learn from a more mature one: climate psychology has already weathered organized denial and can offer six lessons about intervention design, behavioral (rather than merely attitudinal) outcomes, and cross-national generalizability. This diagnostic impulse — stepping back to ask "are we asking the right questions, with the right evidence, from the right places?" — runs through nearly every paper below, whether the object is polarization, radicalization, platform data, or generative AI.

## WEIRD-centrism and the geography of evidence

Several papers converge on a specific bias: misinformation and polarization research is disproportionately about the United States and a handful of Western democracies. [[Humprecht2025-ml]] frames an entire special issue around correcting this, arguing that comparative, cross-platform work is needed to understand how misinformation functions outside WEIRD contexts. [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] makes the same argument for polarization research specifically, calling for integration across fragmented disciplines and genuinely global evidence. [[Nenno2025-xa]] supplies an empirical test of the claim at scale — comparing news values in flagged misinformation across twenty-four countries — and finds that WEIRD/non-WEIRD differences are statistically real but often small, with the US and Brazil behaving as outliers rather than as representative "Western" cases, complicating any simple WEIRD/non-WEIRD binary. [[Lukito2026-nb]] extends the geographic critique to research infrastructure itself, showing that the tools available for studying elections are priced, designed, and permissioned in ways that structurally favor well-resourced, Western researchers. [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] gesture toward correctives — messaging-platform dynamics in Brazil, journalistic strategies in non-US contexts — that the field's Anglophone, feed-centric imagination has tended to miss, a point [[Hourigan2026-oc]] makes from a different angle by showing that everyday, non-"hot-button" misinformation (business, celebrity, crime) dominates lived experience far more than the topics the literature privileges.

## Borrowing from more mature or adjacent fields

Beyond climate psychology, the corpus repeatedly looks sideways for methodological maturity. [[Voelkel2026-lc]] operationalizes one of Spampatti et al.'s six lessons directly, running a registered-report megastudy of the ten most-cited climate messages and finding mostly small, non-heterogeneous effects on attitudes and no effect on costly behavior — a cautionary tale about the gap between attitudinal and behavioral outcomes that misinformation researchers are urged to avoid replicating. [[Matias2025-px]] borrows from participatory and citizen science to argue that public involvement — especially from people with lived experience — is not merely a legitimacy exercise but can materially improve the rigor of AI evaluation, a lesson with obvious analogues for misinformation interventions designed without input from those most exposed. [[Unknown2026-sl3d]] issues a parallel call for scientists studying societal division to take applied, interventionist responsibility seriously, while flagging that depolarization research is still "in its infancy."

## The evidence-recommendation gap

A distinct methodological critique targets the disconnect between what studies find and what they recommend. [[van-Eck2026-xg]] conducts a systematic review showing that depolarization recommendations in the discussion sections of 89 papers are almost never grounded in the papers' own evidence, but instead reflect implicit normative commitments — chiefly to deliberative-democratic ideals. This mirrors [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study, which finds expert consensus that no "silver bullet" individual-level intervention exists and that platform- and journalism-level interventions are underweighted relative to their assessed importance — suggesting the field's practical recommendations lag behind its own diagnostic consensus. [[Bennett2025-xs]] pushes this further, arguing that the dominant "technocentric," individual-cognition paradigm of mis/disinformation research — shaped by an estimated $70 million in funding directed at correction strategies — has structurally crowded out institutionalist explanations of democratic backsliding, leaving the field with an impoverished theory of how platforms interact with parties and organizations.

## Reassessing how big the problem is

A cluster of papers directly interrogates whether the field has overstated harms. [[Budak2024-ef]] argues explicitly that public and scholarly discourse has diverged from the evidence on exposure prevalence, algorithmic causation, and societal-scale harm. This claim is stress-tested empirically by several papers: [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram in 2020 concentrated among few users with no measurable attitudinal effect from experimentally reducing it; [[Lyons2026-ca]] finds the same concentration pattern for low-credibility health content, disproportionately among older adults; and [[Oswald2025-km]] frames this concentration as a general "production-consumption gap" that distorts both public and scholarly inference from visible online content. Yet the picture is not uniformly deflationary — [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] uses a causal epidemic model to estimate that antivaccine tweets caused thousands of vaccine refusals and measurable excess mortality, and [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argues that the dominant "direct referrer" measurement paradigm itself systematically underestimates platforms' role in misinformation exposure by 1.5–15x, while [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] shows that elite-centered exposure architecture, not just individual susceptibility, causally shapes belief accuracy. Together these papers suggest the "are harms overstated or understated" debate is less about ideology than about measurement — which paradigm, referrer definition, or outcome variable one adopts fundamentally changes the conclusion, a point made most systematically by [[Schemer2026-mh]], whose specification-curve analysis of 504 operationalizations shows that methodological choices about media-partisanship measures explain the bulk of variance in reported partisan-media/polarization relationships.

## Conceptual foundations under strain

Several papers argue the field's core concepts are undertheorized. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] contends that "fake news" typologies wrongly center creator intent rather than the propagation dynamics that actually produce cascades, while [[Sadler2025-vu]] extends this by showing that narrative content can be referentially and ethically problematic without being straightforwardly "false," undermining the field's reliance on binary true/false judgments. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] pushes the same epistemological question into the generative-AI era, arguing that none of fact-checking's traditional categories of factuality (evidentiary, interpretive, institutional) capture the probabilistic, "emergent" nature of AI outputs. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] diagnoses a related problem at the level of metaphor: "infodemic," "information warfare," and "information pollution" each illuminate only part of a genuinely multilevel, complex-adaptive system, and interventions built on a single analogy can backfire. [[Thiele2025-ol]] makes an analogous move for coordinated manipulation research, arguing that "coordinated inauthentic behavior" conflates inauthenticity with intent and proposing a rational-choice attribution framework instead — a critique empirically sharpened by [[Pante2025-pq]], which reassesses claimed inter-state coordination using more rigorous control-dataset methods. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] similarly argue for shifting units of analysis — toward recurring narratives and journalistic co-production rather than isolated claims — while [[Trilling2024-slc8]] proposes replacing the vague "echo chamber"/"filter bubble" metaphors with a feedback-loop framework that can explain reinforcement without inevitable radicalization, and [[Hartmann2025-px]]'s systematic review shows empirically that much of the field's disagreement about echo chambers stems from inconsistent operationalization rather than contradictory reality.

## Mapping the field: scoping and systematic reviews

Beyond conceptual critique, several papers offer empirical cartography of research itself. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s 25-year scoping review of online radicalization research finds a persistent scarcity of longitudinal and causal evidence despite an "empirical turn" since 2018. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study provides an anticipatory, multi-stakeholder map of challenges and interventions that explicitly counters the field's siloed, present-focused tendencies. [[Yang2026-tq]] quantifies the field's platform dependence directly, showing that Twitter/X and Facebook dominate empirical social-science research far out of proportion to their public usage, and that this concentration is now declining post-API-restriction. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers perhaps the sharpest single verdict on the field's structural limits: even the best-resourced academic-industry collaboration in the field's history (Meta2020) produced findings whose external and "temporal validity" decay too fast to matter, because platforms change faster than social science can study them — a conclusion that motivates his call for proactive regulation over reactive research.

## Industry entanglement and the politics of access

A dense cluster of papers documents how platform control over data has become the field's central epistemic vulnerability. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and its companion empirical study [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] show, respectively, the mechanisms (suppression, selective funding, design bias, performative collaboration) and the scale (roughly half of high-profile social media papers have undisclosed industry ties, with ~80% "industrial saturation" once editors and reviewers are counted) of this dependency, drawing explicit parallels to tobacco and pharmaceutical research. [[Heiss2026-qv]] situates this within the same comparative frame and proposes DSA-style regulatory reform plus stronger "research on research" norms. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes the problem, arguing that misinformation-at-scale is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models rather than a correctable bug, which explains platforms' preference for behavioral moderation over truth-adjudication. The infrastructural consequences of this dependency are documented from multiple angles: [[Freelon2024-sc]], [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]], and Bastos2025-ya chronicle the "post-API age" and its costs to Twitter-based scholarship specifically; [[Lukito2026-nb]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] show how tools like CrowdTangle actively shaped — and, in their "seeing like an API" framing, constrained — what researchers could know; and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] provides a granular technical case study of how a single anonymization threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset introduced a platform-wide artifact that could be mistaken for substantive cross-country findings. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] argues the discipline should respond to the DSA moment not just tactically but conceptually, reorienting from individual causal effects toward platform collective behavior as democratic infrastructure.

## Measurement rigor as a recurring theme

A parallel set of papers polices the field's empirical foundations at a finer grain. [[Stagnaro2025-pz]] shows opt-in online samples vary enormously in representativeness and validity, with consequences for detecting heterogeneous treatment effects. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] demonstrates that web-scraping methodology itself — not just time delay — introduces large, non-random distortions into measures of news consumption. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits the NewsGuard database widely used for source-based misinformation research, finding that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications can badly distort findings even when continuous scores are stable. [[Hinck2026-yj]] raises a more foundational alarm: survey researchers routinely assume, without evidence, that respondents understand questions as intended. Collectively these papers argue that much apparent disagreement in the field is a measurement artifact rather than a substantive one — the same lesson [[Schemer2026-mh]] and [[Hartmann2025-px]] draw at the level of whole literatures.

## The generative-AI reckoning

The newest wave of self-reflection concerns how generative AI destabilizes the field's existing frameworks and methods. [[Tornberg2026-lc]] argues the entire "social media" paradigm is dissolving into algorithmic broadcasting, semi-private spheres, and AI-mediated communication, demanding new concepts altogether — a claim echoed in [[Baym2026-tr]]'s decade-later retrospective on worsening platform harms and generative AI's appropriation of user data. [[Balluff2026-if]] and [[Vertesi2026-lv]] offer parallel warnings about uncritical AI adoption: the former targets communication researchers' unreflective use of LLMs for text analysis and synthetic data, the latter argues that AI-accountability discourse itself gets captured by "decoys" (ontological, inevitability, safety, regulatory framings) that obscure the underlying political economy. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] examine how journalism produces and is shaped by AI hype, with the latter showing systematic North-South divergence in optimism versus risk framing. Methodologically, [[Alizadeh2026-es]] tests whether AI coding agents can be trusted to reproduce social-science findings (largely yes, with important caveats about sycophancy and PDF-induced bias), while [[Hackenburg2025-dj]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], and [[Gilardi2026-hw]] turn to substantive questions — persuasion mechanisms, inoculation delivery, and audience quality-perception — that will need to be folded into the field's next generation of theory. Read together, these papers suggest the discipline's habitual self-critique (WEIRD bias, cognitive overreliance, industry capture, measurement fragility) is now being reenacted, in compressed time, around generative AI — reinforcing [[Spampatti2026-kx]]'s founding insight that a field which learns from its own history, and from others', stands the best chance of getting ahead of the next crisis rather than perpetually diagnosing the last one.
