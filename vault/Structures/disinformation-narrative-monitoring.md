# Disinformation Narrative Monitoring

## The narrative turn: rethinking what disinformation *is*

A cluster of theoretical papers filed here converge on a single reorientation: disinformation should not be modeled as a set of discrete false claims but as *narratives* — recurring, cross-platform, emotionally resonant stories that outlive any individual fact-check. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes this argument most forcefully, showing across three U.S. case studies that successful disinformation behaves like folklore — it has characters, settings, plots, and catchphrases (textual or visual) that let audiences recognize and retell it across genres, from memes to late-night comedy to Kickstarter campaigns. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes the same intuition empirically, finding that cumulative, repeated exposure to a narrative across the hybrid media ecosystem — not any single false claim — is what drives belief. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes the theoretical apparatus further, arguing that disinformation studies has used "narrative" naively, and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological toolkit (drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan) to show that content can be referentially and *ethically* problematic — reductive, monological, Manichean — without containing any strictly false statement, as demonstrated in a case study of pro-Russian framings of NATO enlargement. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates this shift from a systems-theory angle, arguing that fake-news typologies fixated on creator intent miss how propagators' successive truth-judgments actually generate the emergent cascades we call disinformation — a micro/meso/macro model that foreshadows much of the narrative-tracking agenda. Together these pieces justify why a monitoring project like PROMPT should track *narratives* rather than *claims*.

This reorientation also reframes what counts as a targetable unit for correction. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written from inside a fact-checking organization (Maldita.es), documents how the 2024 Valencia floods produced a disinformation narrative that outran claim-by-claim debunking, mutating from rumor to full conspiracy even after official correction. The authors propose "narrative monitoring" (tracking clustered claims in real time) and "argument-checking" (targeting the underlying rhetorical/logical structures) as complements to fact-checking — effectively a blueprint for the kind of dashboard PROMPT aims to build. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] supplies a complementary meta-critique: dominant analogies for misinformation (virus, warfare, pollution) each capture only one level of a multilevel complex system, and effective interventions require a "portfolio" approach spanning micro, meso, and macro levels rather than a single silver-bullet analogy or metric — a caution against any monitoring system that reduces narratives to a single score.

## Methods for detecting and tracking narratives at scale

Several papers supply the computational infrastructure this narrative-first agenda demands. [[Waight2025-al]] is the methodological anchor: it formally distinguishes "narrative similarity" from lexical overlap, topic similarity, and semantic similarity, and builds an LLM-based pipeline (concept-guided summarization + SBERT candidate generation + GPT-4o annotation) that outperforms exact-text-reuse, topic modeling, and Relatio baselines by a wide margin. Applied to the Ukraine "biolabs" narrative, it shows that low-quality U.S. outlets shared Russian-state narratives far more than mainstream outlets did — a distinction invisible to n-gram-based detectors, which is a strong argument for LLM-based narrative extraction as the technical backbone of any monitoring dashboard. [[Gerard2025-br]] extends this to network structure with Cluster Affiliation Network Embedding (CANE/t-CANE), modeling users as distributions over latent narrative clusters rather than via interaction data; applied to the 2024 U.S. election, it identifies a tiny set of "bridge users" (0.33% of accounts) who seed ~70% of narratives migrating between Truth Social and X — a structural, platform-agnostic account of cross-platform diffusion directly relevant to tracking narrative flow across the fragmented European platform ecosystem. [[Goel2025-iq]] complicates source-level detection from another angle: using co-sharing patterns, it shows that factually accurate mainstream articles are routinely repurposed to support misinformation narratives (on vaccines, on voter fraud), reaching roughly twice the audience of "fake news" proper — evidence that domain-blocklist approaches to monitoring systematically undercount the phenomenon. [[Gardam2025-er]] extends the methodological argument to modality, proposing a visual-first framework for image-centric platforms like Instagram, arguing that text-centric pipelines (including, implicitly, much of the LLM-based narrative-extraction machinery above) miss where the persuasive work is actually done.

## Elites, platforms, and the political economy of narrative spread

A second thread examines *who* propagates narratives and under what institutional incentives. [[Tornberg2025-ir]], analyzing 32 million tweets from parliamentarians in 26 countries, finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing — only their conjunction, radical-right populism, robustly does, tying the phenomenon to a specific elite political strategy rather than a generic platform pathology. [[Tai2026-qk]] narrows in on U.S. elected officials, showing institutional role and ideology act as differential "checks" on misinformation sharing. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] offers a granular case (Brazilian parliamentarians during the Manaus COVID collapse) of how politicians contest journalistic gatekeeping directly, deploying three distinct epistemic-authority strategies — derivative reporting, direct authority claims, and locally-grounded brokerage — that map onto broader patterns of elite narrative production identified across this literature. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] moves from elected officials to covert state-adjacent actors, using leaked CVs to reconstruct the Internet Research Agency's organizational structure, showing it functioned less as an ideologically driven black-ops unit than as an ordinary media-labor-market firm — recruitment spiking with the 2014 Crimea annexation rather than the 2016 U.S. election, a finding that should recalibrate assumptions about troll-factory motives feeding into Ukraine-war narrative monitoring. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes the flip side of state narrative production: a dual-objective model in which authoritarian regimes (illustrated by Russia in the Russo-Ukrainian war) deliberately diverge domestic and foreign propaganda tracks. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] adds that such campaigns are not episodic but persistent, adaptively appropriating new emerging events to sustain relevance — a temporal dynamic directly relevant to longitudinal dashboard design. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] provides the clearest LGBTQI+-relevant case: a decade-long study of CitizenGO shows a centrally coordinated, Spain-headquartered network using anti-gender narratives as transnational "symbolic glue," amplified by a homophilic international network of right-wing, religious, and pro-life accounts — direct evidence of coordinated narrative mainstreaming on the exact axis (LGBTQI+ rights) the topic description flags.

## Platforms, modality, and measurement of prevalence

Several entries address platform-level prevalence and affordances directly. [[Vincent_undated-re]] reports repeated-measurement data showing TikTok's mis/disinformation exposure rising from ~20% to ~25% between rounds, with persistent engagement advantages for low-credibility accounts — exactly the kind of longitudinal, cross-platform monitoring output PROMPT-style dashboards aim to produce. [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[Marwick2025-ov]] both dig into TikTok's platform-specific affordances: the former identifies *sound* as an under-examined vector for Ukraine-war disinformation, the latter shows "ConspiracyTok" is driven by young, non-white, and/or female creators whose "generous epistemology" — populist, evidentiary, identity-affirming — both empowers marginalized standpoints and channels bias and disinformation, complicating simple demographic assumptions about who spreads conspiratorial narratives. [[Marwick2025-vx]] extends the same authorial team's interest in socialization, showing that Far-Right "redpilling" is usually gradual community socialization rather than sudden conversion, with disinformation functioning as cumulative "evidence" legitimizing extremist identity — reinforcing the volume/repetition mechanism found in [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]]. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] tests the specific claim that visual/AI-generated disinformation is inherently more powerful than text, finding effects are issue-dependent: video disinformation only outperforms text for the high-salience Ukraine-war issue, and largely because plausible authentic footage exists to recontextualize, not because of generative AI sophistication — a useful corrective for monitoring systems tempted to over-weight synthetic media over cheap recontextualization. [[Zhao2025-ny]] rounds out the platform-mechanics thread with "hashtag hijacking" as a strategic tactic combining attention capture, narrative substitution, and bot amplification.

## Reception, belief, and downstream democratic effects

A final cluster examines how narratives land with audiences and what happens politically once they do. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]], both drawing on Klein's data-frame sensemaking theory and Hochschild's "deep stories," analyze U.S. election-integrity rumors as a joint product of (often accurate) evidence and partisan framing rather than false facts per se — showing how influencers strategically leave frames implicit, letting partisan audiences supply meaning via quote-tweets and comments, and how the "deep story" of 2020 election fraud was reactivated with minimal explicit argument in 2022. [[Nenno2025-xa]] examines cross-national news-value patterns in Facebook-flagged misinformation across 24 countries, finding conflict framing consistently distinguishes flagged from unflagged content, though effects are generally small and the U.S./Brazil pair stands out as outliers — a caution against assuming uniform narrative "signatures" of disinformation across markets, directly relevant to a pan-European monitoring tool. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines the fact-checking side of the same coin, finding that fact-checks themselves lean on emotional rather than purely objective framing to drive engagement — an ironic mirroring of the persuasive techniques found in the narratives they debunk.

Downstream, several papers ask what believing (or merely discussing) disinformation narratives does to democratic attitudes. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] documents a "hostile misinformation effect" across three 2024 European Parliament election contexts (Germany, Netherlands, Poland): citizens systematically perceive their own political side as more targeted by misinformation than their rivals, an effect amplified by political interest, partisan identity, and right-wing orientation, with knock-on associations to lower media trust and perceived electoral integrity. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] shows experimentally that even *discourse about* election interference — independent of whether interference campaigns work — can itself heighten perceived societal division and affective polarization when the reported beneficiary is an out-group party, a finding with direct implications for how narrative-monitoring dashboards communicate findings publicly. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] examine the meta-discursive dimension: how the term "fake news" itself is weaponized across the ideological spectrum to redraw Hallin's spheres of legitimacy, and how journalistic reporting strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs rather than merely transmitting existing ones.

## Synthesis and open questions for monitoring

Read together, these papers sketch an arc: from a theoretical case (Marwick, Sadler, Giglietto, Suau_Martinez) for treating disinformation as narrative rather than claim, through a rapidly maturing methodological toolkit for detecting and tracing that narrative at scale (Waight, Gerard, Goel, Gardam), to empirical accounts of who produces and amplifies narratives (Tornberg, Rodarte, Poliakoff, Makeev, Righetti, FitzGerald) and how platforms shape their circulation (Vincent, Bosch, Marwick2025-ov, Hameleers, Zhao), and finally to the psychological and democratic consequences of exposure and belief (Starbird, Prochaska, Van_Erkel, Karlsson, Nenno). The Delphi study [[Mahl2026-hc]] usefully sits above all of this, warning that no single-level intervention — platform regulation, journalism reform, prebunking, or fact-checking — will suffice, and that expert consensus expects the situation to worsen absent coordinated, multi-level action. For PROMPT, the collective lesson is that a credible narrative-monitoring dashboard must combine LLM-based narrative-similarity detection (Waight), cross-platform structural tracing (Gerard), attention to co-opted "true" content (Goel), and calibrated, non-alarmist communication of findings (Karlsson, Van_Erkel) — while resisting the temptation, flagged repeatedly across this literature, to reduce narratives back down to simple true/false claim counts.
