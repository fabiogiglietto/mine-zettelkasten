# Disinformation Narrative Monitoring

# Disinformation Narrative Monitoring

## From claims to stories: a conceptual pivot

The papers in this collection converge on a foundational argument: disinformation is poorly understood as a set of discrete false claims and much better understood as *narrative* — recurring, cross-platform, emotionally resonant stories that circulate independently of any single false statement. [[Marwick2026-qd]] states this most directly, arguing that "successful" disinformation behaves like narrative with characters, settings, plots and catchphrases, spreading because it satisfies desire and produces catharsis rather than because it is believed to be factually true. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this further into narrative theory proper, showing via a hermeneutic-realist reading of pro-Russian tweets that content can be referentially and ethically suspect — reducing complexity, disnarrating agency, deploying monologic multivocality — without containing any demonstrably false statement, which exposes the limits of the true/false binary that underwrites most fact-checking. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] anticipates this shift from a cybernetics angle, arguing that "fake news" typologies fixate on creator intent when the real analytic object should be the chain of propagator judgments that let heterogeneous content cascade as if it were unified. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] operationalizes the narrative view empirically, showing survey evidence that cumulative cross-outlet exposure to a narrative — not single exposures to single claims — predicts belief. Together these papers justify why "narrative monitoring," rather than claim-level fact-checking, is the organizing concept of this topic.

## Narrative as collective sensemaking

A cluster of CSCW/political-communication papers gives this narrative turn empirical texture by studying how narratives are *built collaboratively* rather than injected top-down. [[Starbird2025-jj]] introduces an evidence-frame framework showing that misleading election rumors emerge from the interaction between often-accurate evidence and partisan frames, with quote-tweet structures making visible how audiences add, echo, escalate, or counter frames. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] extends this into "deep storytelling," showing that between 2020 and 2022 U.S. election rumors shifted from explicit narrative elaboration toward bare artifact-sharing that relies on audiences already possessing the "deep story" — a shorthand economy of allusion (Box 3, Sharpie, DeJoy) that single-post analysis cannot capture. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this by showing that mainstream, factually accurate journalism is itself co-opted into misleading narratives via headline framing and selective co-sharing, meaning domain-level reliability scores miss a large share of narrative-level harm. [[Waight2025-al]] offers a methodological answer to the measurement problem these studies raise, proposing an LLM-based pipeline for quantifying "narrative similarity" (shared claims about shared events) that outperforms text-reuse, topic-modeling, and semantic-similarity baselines, and demonstrating on the Ukraine "biolabs" story that lexical methods miss narrative diffusion into mainstream U.S. outlets entirely.

## Case studies: war, elections, gender and platform ecosystems

Several papers instantiate narrative monitoring around PROMPT's core case studies. On the war in Ukraine: [[Bosch2024-hj]] documents TikTok's platform-specific propaganda affordance — sound — as a vector for war disinformation at massive scale; [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a dual-objective model of Russian state propaganda targeting domestic and foreign audiences differently; [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] provides an organizational history of the IRA "troll factory," showing its origins predate the 2016 US election and trace to the Crimea annexation, reframing disinformation production as embedded in Russia's media labor market rather than a covert intelligence operation; and [[Hameleers2026-mc]] experimentally shows that decontextualized *video* of the Ukraine war outperforms textual and even AI-generated disinformation in credibility — but only because authentic footage exists to be repurposed, tempering fears about generative AI's unique persuasive power. On elections, the Brazilian pair [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] track how belief in electoral fraud narratives erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, implicating messaging apps like WhatsApp as amplifiers, while [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links European far-right electoral performance to rising public concern about disinformation. On LGBTQI+/anti-gender narratives, [[Righetti2025-slf9]] maps CitizenGO's decade-long transnational network, showing centralized coordination from Spain and anti-gender framing serving as "symbolic glue" amplified by a homophilic, partially astroturfed international network — a structural analogue to [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]]'s finding of coordinated forwarding among pro-government Telegram channels in Russia and Belarus.

## Radicalization, mainstreaming, and platform vernaculars

A related thread examines how narratives migrate from fringe to mainstream and how platforms shape their form. [[Rothut2026-or]] introduces "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to explain how Germany's Querdenken movement became a conduit linking ordinary anti-lockdown sympathizers to organized far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks via shared anti-elite framing. [[Marwick2025-vx]] complicates the "redpilling as sudden conversion" story with evidence of gradual socialization via accumulated pseudo-evidence, while [[Marwick2025-ov]] shows on TikTok that conspiracy content is produced by a demographically unexpected creator base practicing "populist knowledge production" under a "generous epistemology" that can be simultaneously empowering and disinforming. [[Hollingshead2026-vx]] and [[Gardam2025-er]] extend the platform-vernacular lens to TikTok immigration discourse and Instagram climate denial respectively, both arguing that visual- and affordance-first methods are necessary because meaning is constructed multimodally rather than textually. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] and [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] round out this cluster by pointing to the temporal persistence of manipulation campaigns (which latch onto emerging events) and to journalism's own inadvertent role in seeding new conspiracy beliefs.

## Countermeasures: LLMs, dashboards, and fact-checker practice

The final arc turns to interventions relevant to PROMPT's applied mission. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] benchmarks LLMs as fact-checking tools, finding that reasoning and web search alone barely help, while curated retrieval-augmented context boosts accuracy dramatically — with a caveat that search-enabled models cite left-leaning sources disproportionately. [[Ramos2026-qo]], written by Maldita.es fact-checkers analyzing the Valencia flood conspiracy, argues explicitly for supplementing fact-checking with narrative monitoring and argument-checking, since claim-level correction cannot keep pace with a narrative's mutation across escalating claims. [[Rogers2026-cy]], from the vera.ai project, offers a network-mapping methodology for locating "post-truth spaces" for fact-checkers, demonstrated on Moldovan Facebook ahead of the 2024 elections — showing that peripheral post-truth clusters exert influence chiefly by bridging into more central "borderline" nodes. Finally, [[Farkas2026-lr]] examines the institutional conditions of this counter-disinformation infrastructure itself, showing how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their platform-funded partnerships as a pragmatic necessity, while [[Vincent_undated-re]] supplies the platform-level baseline these efforts respond to, documenting persistently high and rising misinformation exposure on TikTok and a durable engagement advantage for low-credibility accounts across major platforms in Europe.
