# Disinformation Narrative Monitoring

## Overview

This cluster gathers the intellectual scaffolding around PROMPT-style narrative monitoring: what a "disinformation narrative" actually is, how to detect and track it computationally, how fact-checkers and platforms institutionally cope with its persistence, and how audiences might be inoculated against it. Read together, the papers trace an arc from conceptual critique (what counts as a narrative, and is fact-checking even the right unit of analysis?) through methodological innovation (LLM-based narrative similarity, network mapping of "post-truth spaces") to applied and institutional case studies (Ukraine, anti-gender mobilization, Meta's fact-checking retreat) and finally to audience-facing interventions (prebunking, emotional framing).

## Rethinking the unit of analysis: from claims to narratives

Two papers directly interrogate the conceptual foundation that narrative monitoring rests on. [[Sadler2025-vu]] mounts the most fundamental challenge, arguing that disinformation studies has treated "narrative" as self-evident and reducible to a true/false binary, when in fact stories can be referentially and ethically problematic without containing a single false statement — his case study of NATO-blame tweets about the Ukraine invasion shows how sideshadowing, disnarration, and monological quotation produce misleading meaning through form rather than falsehood. This is a direct challenge to any monitoring architecture built purely on claim verification. [[Ramos2026-qo]] responds, in effect, from inside a working fact-checking organization: drawing on Maldita.es's experience of the 2024 Valencia floods, it argues that claim-by-claim fact-checking cannot keep pace with narratives that mutate and regroup (from missing-persons rumors to cover-up conspiracy in a matter of days), and proposes "narrative monitoring" and "argument-checking" — targeting the rhetorical scaffolding (fallacies, warrants) rather than individual claims — as necessary complements. Where Sadler supplies the theoretical vocabulary for why narratives resist truth-functional treatment, Ramos supplies an applied protocol for operationalizing narrative-level (rather than claim-level) intervention — precisely the shift PROMPT-style dashboards need to make.

[[Waight2025-al]] provides the methodological bridge between these positions and computational monitoring: it formally distinguishes "narrative similarity" (same claims about same events) from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and shows that naive estimators (text reuse, topic models, Relatio) systematically undercount narrative diffusion. Applied to the Russian "biolabs" story, its LLM-based pipeline detects narrative overlap between Russian state media and low-quality (and even some mainstream) U.S. outlets that n-gram methods miss entirely — a direct demonstration that estimator choice determines whether cross-border narrative contagion is visible at all, with obvious implications for any dashboard's underlying detection engine.

## Mapping and monitoring infrastructures

[[Rogers2026-cy]], written for the sister vera.ai project, is the paper most structurally analogous to PROMPT's own dashboard ambitions: it proposes "post-truth spaces" as a mappable object — clusters of mutually referencing, hard-to-verify accounts — situated among adjacent categories (alternative influence networks, coordinated inauthentic behavior, participatory propaganda), and demonstrates via Moldovan Facebook data ahead of the 2024 elections that betweenness centrality, not raw cluster size, reveals which "borderline" nodes actually broker disinformation into mainstream discourse. Its key methodological payoff — that monitoring tools should surface *leads* for human fact-checkers rather than attempt automatic fake detection — echoes across the collection. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] performs a comparable network-mapping exercise on the anti-gender organization CitizenGO, showing centralized coordination from a Spanish HQ radiating through homophilic, multilingual amplification networks that function as transnational "symbolic glue" — a case study of exactly the kind of coordinated, boundary-spanning narrative structure that monitoring dashboards for LGBTQI+-related disinformation would need to track. [[Copland2025-em]] extends this network logic to Sky News Australia, revealing a dual-track propaganda strategy (parochial content for domestic Facebook audiences, conspiracist content funneled into the US YouTube pipeline) that illustrates how the same monitoring logic — tracing content across platforms and audiences — applies equally to elections- and war-adjacent right-wing media ecosystems.

## War narratives as a proving ground

The Ukraine war furnishes this topic's most concentrated empirical testbed. [[Bosch2024-hj]] identifies sound as an under-theorized, platform-specific vector for TikTok disinformation about the invasion, complementing [[Gardam2025-er]]'s parallel argument (developed for climate denial on Instagram) that visual-first and multimodal methods are needed wherever text-centric analysis under-captures how conspiracy narratives actually circulate — both papers push narrative monitoring beyond text mining toward audiovisual analysis. [[Makeev2026-ma]] supplies the strategic-communication layer above these platform studies, theorizing how authoritarian regimes like Russia's run *dual-track* propaganda — domestic narratives stressing high stakes/low costs to sustain legitimacy, foreign-facing narratives designed to erode adversaries' resolve — which helps explain why the same war generates such different narrative clusters depending on audience and platform, a distinction [[Waight2025-al]]'s biolabs case operationalizes empirically.

## LLMs as monitoring and verification infrastructure

[[DeVerna2025-dl]] delivers a sobering methodological check on the current wave of LLM-based fact-checking tools that underpin many monitoring pipelines: standard and even reasoning-enabled models perform poorly on PolitiFact claims, web search yields only moderate gains, and only curated retrieval (a maintained database of prior fact-checks) produces reliable results — with search-enabled models additionally showing a left-leaning citation skew. This has direct implications for PROMPT-adjacent tooling: automated narrative detection is only as good as its curated evidence base, echoing [[Waight2025-al]]'s finding that fine-tuning on hand-coded boundary cases, not raw model capability, drives performance gains in narrative-similarity detection.

## Fact-checkers' institutional predicament

Two papers turn from technical infrastructure to the political economy sustaining (or threatening) it. [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents how European fact-checkers rhetorically defend their dependence on platform funding (Meta, Alphabet, ByteDance) through differentiation and transcendence strategies, revealing an emergent field whose boundaries around acceptable funders remain contested and inconsistent. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] extends this into the post-Zuckerberg moment, empirically debunking the "censorship" framing behind Meta's abandonment of third-party fact-checking — showing that removal was always rare (~30%) and mostly reflects platform, not fact-checker, decisions — while documenting fact-checkers' near-universal skepticism toward Community Notes as a substitute. Together these papers frame the institutional fragility underlying any fact-checker-support tool: the very partnerships that fund monitoring infrastructure are also its chief source of reputational vulnerability.

## Vernacular epistemics and audience-facing interventions

[[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates any monitoring approach premised on a single "disinformation narrative" origin, showing how ConspiracyTok's young, non-White, and female-dominated creator base builds "populist knowledge production" and a "generous epistemology" that blends legitimate standpoint critique with antisemitic and transphobic tropes — narrative monitoring here must contend with narratives that are neither monolithic propaganda nor simple falsehood but hybrid, identity-inflected sensemaking. [[Xue2025-bp]] adds a message-design dimension, showing fact-checks themselves are often emotionally charged, complicating the normative self-image of fact-checking as dispassionate correction and dovetailing with [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]]'s field experiment, which demonstrates that scalable, ad-delivered prebunking against *emotional manipulation* specifically (rather than factual content) produces durable, measurable gains in Instagram users' resistance — the clearest evidence in this set that narrative-level, technique-focused inoculation (echoing Ramos's argument-checking) outperforms claim-level correction as an educational strategy.
