# Information Disorder and Disinformation Narratives

## Information Disorder and Disinformation Narratives

This pillar spans everything from the definitional battles over "fake news" to the computational detection of coordinated bot networks, but its unifying thread is a steady migration away from treating disinformation as a problem of discrete false claims and towards understanding it as a *systemic, narrative, and infrastructural* phenomenon embedded in hybrid media ecosystems.

### From claims to cycles: the hybrid media system as foundation

The theoretical bedrock here is Chadwick's hybrid media system [[Chadwick2013-ns]] [[Chadwick2015-ii]], which displaces old/new media dichotomies with an account of adaptation, interdependence, and the "political information cycle." This framework is explicitly operationalised for disinformation by [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], who use second-order cybernetics to argue that the creator-centric focus on intent (satire vs. deception vs. error) misses how *propagators* — not injectors — drive the emergent, composite cascades that actually constitute "fake news" in circulation. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] extends this logic empirically, showing that cumulative, cross-outlet exposure to recurring disinformation *narratives* (not isolated claims) predicts belief in the US context. [[Marwick2026-qd]] pushes the narrative turn furthest, reconceiving disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *stories* — complete with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes — that evade fact-checking precisely because they are not reducible to true/false propositions. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the hermeneutic philosophy for this move, criticizing the field's naïve use of "narrative" and proposing a Ricoeur-inspired account of how stories can "disclose" reality in referentially and ethically problematic ways without being straightforwardly false. [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] translate this into empirical method, showing via election-rumor case studies that misleadingness often lives in the *framing* of true evidence (an "evidence-frame" dynamic) and in collaborative "deep storytelling" across elites and audiences — continuities with [[Marwick2025-vx]]'s account of "processual redpilling" as gradual socialization into conspiratorial evidentiary regimes rather than sudden conversion.

### Taxonomy, labels, and the politics of naming

A persistent strand interrogates the vocabulary itself. [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]] anchor the "disinformation order" framing, arguing that strategic disinformation is a structural symptom of declining institutional trust rather than a collection of isolated incidents, and that "fake news" should be abandoned as an analytic category. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] empirically confirm this corrosion: across US and Italian media, "fake news" functions overwhelmingly as a rhetorical weapon for delegitimizing opponents (an SDSA — strategic delegitimization and selective amplification — logic) rather than a description of fabricated content. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] generalizes the critique to the field's master metaphors — virus, warfare, pollution — showing each captures only part of a genuinely multilevel complex system. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] extends the epistemological project into the generative-AI era, arguing that existing fact categories (evidence-based, interpretive, institutional) cannot accommodate AI's "emergent facts," while [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Mannocci2026-kc]] perform parallel conceptual labour on "coordinated inauthentic behaviour," arguing that platform-derived definitions conflate inauthenticity with harmful intent and proposing more rigorous, attribution-oriented typologies.

### Elites, parties, and the strategic supply side

A substantial cluster treats disinformation as elite political strategy rather than grassroots pathology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts parliamentary misinformation-sharing across 26 countries — only their *interaction* (radical-right populism) does — complementing [[Tai2026-qk]]'s work on institutional and ideological checks on officials' sharing behaviour and [[Rodarte2026-dk]]'s account of Brazilian parliamentarians contesting epistemic authority during the Manaus COVID crisis via distinct objectivity/authority/proximity strategies. [[Gaber2022-bk]] traces "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election as an intensification (not rupture) of PR spin, while [[Benkler2018-lw]] remains the touchstone account of an asymmetric, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem insulated from truth-correcting norms — a thesis echoed regionally in [[Copland2025-em]]'s study of Sky News Australia as a transnational propaganda node and [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s organizational history of the IRA as "outsourced trolls" embedded in Russia's media labour market. State-level strategic communication appears in [[Makeev2026-ma]] (dual domestic/foreign propaganda objectives) and [[Graham2025-gp]] (propaganda exploiting rather than evading platform trust infrastructures).

### Electoral integrity, trust, and polarization

Elections are a recurring empirical theatre. US-focused work — [[Berlinski2023-rj]], [[Eady2023-xg]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] — generally finds *limited* individual-level persuasive effects of fraud claims or foreign influence content but documents real erosion of institutional confidence, particularly among co-partisans. Brazilian election studies — [[Rossini2026-jn]], [[Rossini2026-mj]], [[Ventura2025-sw]] — extend this to the Global South, showing that belief in electoral misinformation (often circulated via WhatsApp) depresses tolerance and trust even as mainstream news use buffers it, with [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] tracing the parallel rollback of platform election-integrity policies that once scaffolded such resilience. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" and [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s finding that *discourse about* interference itself drives perceived polarization suggest that meta-level narratives about disinformation can be as consequential as the content they describe — a concern also voiced in [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[Schroeder2026-im]]'s warnings about AI-amplified manipulation of democratic deliberation.

### Measuring the problem: scale, virality, and the "not as bad as feared" camp

A methodologically rigorous lineage — [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Allen2020-nj]], [[Allcott2019-gn]], [[Guess2021-ym]], [[Mosleh2024-op]] — insists that raw exposure to fake news is small relative to total media diets and highly concentrated among older, more conservative "supersharers," challenging alarmist narratives. This is counterbalanced by work revealing *underestimated* channels: [[Yang2023-cg]] on image-based misinformation invisible to link-based measures, [[Goel2025-iq]] on true content repurposed into misleading narratives via co-sharing, [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] on indirect/embedded platform referral pathways, and [[Scalco2026-bd]]'s formal operationalization of "information voids." [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] supply the classic diffusion-dynamics and echo-chamber evidence that falsehood travels farther/faster and clusters homogeneously, while [[Osmundsen2021-et]] locates the psychological motor in partisan animus rather than ignorance — consonant with [[Pennycook2021-jq]]'s influential reasoning-deficit account and [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]]'s foundational cognitive-correction literature.

### Platforms as governors, infrastructures, and crime scenes

Platform governance research documents both genuine intervention and its limits: [[Vincent_undated-re]] and [[Holt2026-zq]] track prevalence and user-flagging dynamics on Meta/TikTok; [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] and [[Farkas2026-lr]] examine fact-checkers' fraught, rhetorically-defended partnerships with platforms amid the post-Zuckerberg retreat from third-party verification; [[Renault2025-uh]] stress-tests Community Notes for partisan bias; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Pierri2026-ib]] expose structural inequities in moderation-workforce allocation and researcher data access under the DSA; [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] shows platforms performing "discursive" rather than substantive governance of climate misinformation. At the infrastructural extreme, [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" to capture manipulation that exploits platform affordances without requiring false content at all, while [[Simeone2025-vo]] and [[Rodriguez_Farres2025-sg]] examine deplatforming's network-level ripple effects and real-time bot detection respectively.

### Radicalization, mainstreaming, and the affective turn

A cluster examines how extremist content becomes normalized rather than merely believed: [[Rothut2026-wt]] synthesizes 25 years of online-radicalization scholarship into a hybrid, multi-level model; [[Rothut2026-or]] documents "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" via Querdenken's Telegram entanglement with far-right networks; [[Nangle2026-yo]], [[Topinka2026-hb]], and [[Tuters2026-vo]] foreground affect, humour, and platform-grammar over propositional content — the "Memeplex" account of Great Reset conspiricism being a particularly strong theorization of alignment-before-narrative. [[Marwick2025-ov]] complicates the demographic stereotype of conspiracism via ConspiracyTok's young, non-white creators and "generous epistemology," while [[Righetti2025-slf9]] and [[Slack1783507648-sl94]] trace the coordinated transnationalization of anti-gender movements — directly relevant to the PROMPT project's LGBTQI+ narrative-monitoring remit, alongside [[Holland_Levin2026-qx]]'s account of #StayWoke's culture-war politicization.

### War, visual/generative disinformation, and emerging methods

The Ukraine war anchors a distinct empirical seam: [[Waight2025-al]]'s LLM-based narrative-similarity method tracks the "biolabs" story's cross-ecosystem diffusion; [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] examine sonic and deepfake-specific manipulation in Kenyan and TikTok contexts; [[Hameleers2026-mc]] finds visual disinformation's persuasive edge is issue-contingent, not universal; [[Hove2026-sl49]] and [[Choi2026-bz]] document humans' poor and carryover-affected ability to detect AI imagery and deepfakes; [[Emilio2026-ik]] theorizes the resulting "synthetic reality" stack and "Generative AI Paradox" of rational evidence-discounting; [[Crichton2026-kn]] shows generative tools being absorbed into far-right propaganda pipelines; and [[DeVerna2025-dl]] demonstrates that even capable LLMs fact-check poorly without curated retrieval — a caution echoed in [[Kasianenko2026-tn]]'s audit of search engines' uneven AI-Overview responses to conspiratorial queries.

### Countermeasures and comparative/global expansion

Intervention research spans scales: [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] field-test scalable prebunking and conversational inoculation; [[Costello2024-bg]] shows durable AI-dialogue-based debunking of entrenched conspiracy belief; [[Ramos2026-qo]] proposes narrative- and argument-checking to supplement fact-checking during crises (the Valencia floods case); [[Xue2025-bp]] interrogates fact-checking's own emotional register; and [[Spampatti2026-kx]] and [[Mahl2026-hc]] (a 47-expert Delphi study) call for coordinated, multi-level, cross-disciplinary responses modeled on climate-science governance. Finally, a de-Westernizing corrective — [[Humprecht2025-ml]], [[Humprecht2020-gd]], [[Nenno2025-xa]], [[Aharoni2026-sl6f]], [[Fletcher2026-lv]], [[Hallin2004-kg]] — insists that resilience, news-values, trust decline, and even lay definitions of "news" itself vary systematically by media-system structure, pushing the whole research programme, in Ciarlatani's spirit, toward a genuinely comparative theory of post-truth publics rather than a generalized diagnosis from WEIRD and especially American cases.
