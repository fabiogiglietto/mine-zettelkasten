# Sociocybernetics and Social Systems Theory

## The Network Society as Macro-Theoretical Ground

The oldest and most totalizing theoretical claim in this cluster is Castells' theory of the network society, present here in two cataloged instances ([[Slack1783507648-sl94]] and [[Castells2009-sle8]]), both keyed to *The Rise of the Network Society*. Castells supplies the macro-sociological scaffolding that much of the rest of this thread implicitly answers to: a global social structure organized around digital networks, a "space of flows" superseding the space of places, and communication reorganized as "mass self-communication." Crucially, Castells treats the Internet not as a tool layered atop existing social structure but as constitutive of a new mode of social organization — informationalism fused with restructured capitalism. This is the master narrative against which the more circumscribed, mid-range theoretical interventions below can be read as either specifications, corrections, or quiet abandonments.

## Second-Order Cybernetics and the Problem of Observation

Where Castells offers a structural account, [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] and [[Ptz2024-sl20]] import second-order cybernetics and Luhmannian systems theory to dissolve a different kind of problem: the tendency of internet research to locate meaning or intent in a single actor. Giglietto et al. show that "fake news" cannot be adequately theorized by reference to a creator's intent; instead, following von Foerster and Bateson, they relocate analysis to the propagator, treating truthfulness as observer-dependent and information circulation as an emergent, multilevel cascade rather than a linear transmission. Pütz et al. perform a structurally similar move for human–chatbot interaction: LLMs do not "understand," but they perform competently by parasitizing the repair work of human interlocutors — a claim built directly on Luhmann's separation of communication from psychic/cognitive states. Both papers refuse the individualist, intentionalist framing that dominates popular and even scholarly discourse (about disinformation, about AI cognition) and replace it with a systems-theoretic account of communication as an autonomous, self-organizing process irreducible to any single node's mental states. This is sociocybernetics doing its most classical work: insisting that the unit of analysis is the communicative system, not the communicator.

## From Metaphor to Mechanism: Feedback and Complexity

A second cluster takes issue with the loose metaphors that internet studies has generated to describe networked communication's pathologies, and replaces them with more precise dynamical language. [[Trilling2024-slc8]] argues that "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles" should be superseded by a typology of communicative feedback loops — reinforcing and self-regulating, coexisting with exogenous shocks in a system that is never fully closed. This directly operationalizes a cybernetic vocabulary (Ashby, thermostatic self-regulation) to explain why real reinforcing dynamics do not produce the catastrophic radicalization the bubble metaphors predict. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] performs an analogous critique one level up, at the level of misinformation research writ large: viral, warfare, and pollution analogies each capture only one layer of a four-level complex adaptive system (micro/meso/meso/macro), and interventions built on any single analogy will backfire because they ignore the recursive coupling between levels. Both papers converge on a shared sociocybernetic diagnosis — that networked communication phenomena are multilevel, recursively coupled, and resistant to single-cause or single-metaphor explanation — while explicitly working to make that diagnosis empirically tractable (DAGs and causal inference in Trilling; portfolio interventions modeled on climate policy in Frischlich).

## Systems Theory Meets Infrastructure and Agency

Two papers push social systems theory outward into design and engineering questions rather than purely explanatory ones. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] argues, well ahead of the platform-studies turn, that Internet architecture itself should be redesigned around social capital principles — trust, reach, influence, engagement — because the "prosumer" user has outgrown a purely technical, consumer-oriented network design; the paper's attempt to reconcile networking notions of centrality with social-capital notions of centrality is an early, awkward but prescient effort to make sociological concepts do infrastructural work. [[Ng2026-og]] extends this same impulse to the newest possible object: agentic AI systems. It insists that multi-agent AI populations require social theory as a *structural prior* rather than being modeled as isolated task-completers, formalizing strategic heterogeneity, network-constrained dependence, co-evolution, and distributional instability as properties inherited from social systems theory and demonstrating them empirically in a real AI social network. Read alongside Sofia, Ng shows the persistence of a single theoretical move across a fifteen-year gap: whenever a new class of networked actor (prosumers, then AI agents) emerges, the impulse is to reach for social-systems-theoretic concepts to discipline what would otherwise be treated as a purely technical or individualist problem.

## Loss, Infrastructure, and the Limits of Theory

[[Marwick2026-ss]] closes the loop by returning to lived experience rather than formal theory, but it belongs here because it operationalizes, autoethnographically, the same infrastructural stakes that Sofia and Castells treat abstractly. Marwick's account of LiveJournal-era intimacy versus contemporary platform alienation reframes "social media" explicitly as social infrastructure requiring stewardship — a claim that could be read as a humanist gloss on Sofia's socially-driven architecture, and as a lived counter-narrative to Castells' triumphalist account of mass self-communication. Taken together, these nine papers trace an arc from grand structural theory (Castells), through cybernetic and systems-theoretic accounts of observation and communication (Giglietto, Pütz), through attempts to discipline metaphor into mechanism (Trilling, Frischlich), into speculative extensions of social theory to infrastructure and artificial agents (Sofia, Ng), and finally back to the affective and infrastructural residue of what all this theorizing is ultimately about (Marwick). The persistence of this thread across four decades of theoretical vocabulary — cybernetics, systems theory, complexity science, social capital, multi-agent formalization — suggests less a single unified paradigm than a recurring conviction: that networked communication cannot be understood by aggregating individual actors, and must instead be theorized as a system with its own emergent, often counterintuitive dynamics.
