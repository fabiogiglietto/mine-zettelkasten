# Sociocybernetics & Social Systems Theory

## The systemic turn: from networks to social systems

The starting point for this line of theoretical grounding is [[Castells2009-sle8]], whose macro-sociological account of the "network society" establishes networks — rather than individuals, institutions, or even bounded societies — as the basic unit of social organization in the informational age. Castells' framework supplies the vocabulary (space of flows, timeless time, informationalism) that later, more narrowly theoretical papers implicitly presuppose: that digital technologies do not simply mediate pre-existing social structures but reconstitute the very morphology of the social. This is the macro-historical backdrop against which the more properly *sociocybernetic* and Luhmannian papers in this set operate — they take Castells' diagnosis of structural transformation and ask how, at the level of communication and interaction, such transformed structures actually operate, reproduce, and fail.

## Second-order observation: truth, understanding, and the fiction of shared meaning

A cluster of papers draws directly on second-order cybernetics and Luhmann to dismantle common-sense assumptions about meaning, truth, and understanding in digital communication. [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] uses von Foerster and Bateson to argue that "fake news" cannot be adequately theorized by reference to a creator's intent, because information is observer-dependent — a "difference that makes a difference" reconstructed anew by every propagator. This reframes misinformation not as a property of content but as an emergent, cascading property of a communication system in which each node's judgment of truthfulness recursively feeds the next. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] performs a structurally parallel move at the level of interaction: drawing on Luhmann's separation of psychic and social systems together with conversation analysis, it shows that ChatGPT's apparent conversational competence is not evidence of understanding but of successful *communication* sustained parasitically by human repair work. Both papers exemplify a shared epistemological commitment inherited from Luhmann: communication, not cognition or intention, is the proper unit of sociological analysis, and systems (whether news ecosystems or dialogic exchanges) can display coherent, observable behavior at the macro level that is irreducible to — and sometimes decoupled from — the "understanding" of any individual component.

## Feedback, self-reference, and the limits of linear causality

[[Trilling2024-slc8]] extends this cybernetic sensibility into the empirical study of platform dynamics, arguing that "echo chamber" and "filter bubble" metaphors should be replaced by a formal typology of communicative feedback loops — reinforcing and self-regulating, coexisting with exogenous perturbation in a system that is never fully closed. Its explicit debt to Ashby and to complexity/emergence traditions situates it squarely within sociocybernetics rather than conventional linear causal-inference paradigms (the paper notes that DAG-based causal models cannot even represent cycles). [[Ng2026-og]] makes a structurally similar argument for multi-agent AI systems, insisting that agentic behavior is an emergent property of interaction structure (network topology, co-evolution, distributional drift) rather than of individual agent capability — formalizing, in effect, a systems-theoretic rather than atomistic ontology for AI sociality, and empirically demonstrating self-reinforcing and destabilizing dynamics analogous to those Trilling describes for human platforms. Together these two papers show sociocybernetic thinking migrating from its classical human-communication object toward hybrid human/machine and machine/machine populations, while retaining the core insight that emergent, population-level regularities cannot be read off from the dispositions of individual units.

## Social form as an architectural and design variable

A final pairing treats "the social" itself as a variable *form* that platforms and infrastructures instantiate and can be redesigned. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] theorizes TikTok's shift from "networked publics" to "clustered publics" as a change in the underlying *social form* of online publicness — from explicit interpersonal ties to statistically inferred behavioral similarity — echoing Simmel's formal sociology and functioning as a direct, platform-specific illustration of how communication structures (in the Luhmannian sense) can be re-engineered by algorithmic mediation, with consequences for depersonalization and fragmentation that resonate with Trilling's feedback-loop concerns and Giglietto's account of opaque propagation dynamics. [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]] approaches the same problem from the infrastructural side, arguing that Internet architecture itself should be redesigned around social-capital properties (trust, reciprocity, reach) rather than purely technical metrics — an early, more prescriptive attempt to fold sociological categories into system design, anticipating Ng's later call for social theory as a "structural prior" for engineered agentic systems.

## Synthesis

Read together, these seven papers trace a coherent epistemological stance: digital social phenomena are best approached not as aggregates of individual intentions or cognitions but as self-referential, observer-dependent communication systems whose macro-level order (publics, news cascades, dialogic exchanges, AI agent ecosystems, infrastructures) emerges from, yet is irreducible to, micro-level interactions. Castells supplies the historical scale of transformation; Giglietto, Ptz, and Trilling supply the cybernetic and Luhmannian mechanics of observation, communication, and feedback; Gerbaudo and Sofia show how these dynamics are concretely instantiated and can be redesigned in platforms and architectures; and Ng extends the whole framework prospectively to a near future in which social theory must govern the design of artificial, not merely human, social systems. This body of theory functions as the epistemological substrate for the researcher's broader work on internet studies: it licenses treating platforms, algorithms, and even chatbots as communication systems to be analyzed in their own operational terms, rather than as transparent instruments of individual human agency.
