# Latin American Digital Politics

## The Brazilian Core: Platforms, Bans, and the Architecture of Trust

The anchor of this topic is a cluster of studies treating Brazil as a critical case for digital-era democratic erosion, with the Bolsonaro–Lula cleavage as the organizing axis. [[Ventura2026-yc]] opens the strongest causal claim: the 2024 Supreme Court ban on X did not depoliticize the platform but produced a "sorting ratchet," as conservative users circumvented the ban while liberal users went silent, durably shifting the surviving news environment rightward. This finding reframes platform moderation itself as a partisan event rather than a neutral public-health intervention, and it pairs productively with [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]], which widens the aperture to the regulatory battle over Brazil's "Fake News Bill" (PL 2630), arguing that platforms' lobbying campaigns against regulation constitute a privatization of public discourse specific to the structural weakness of Global South states. Read together, these two papers trace a single arc: platforms shape the terms of political contestation whether states act (bans) or fail to act (stalled regulation), and in both cases the outcome tilts toward entrenched asymmetries of power rather than restored democratic equilibrium.

Running alongside this institutional strand is a survey-based literature on how ordinary citizens' belief in electoral misinformation corrodes democratic norms. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] share a three-wave panel design around the 2022 election and converge on a common mechanism: belief in fraud-related misinformation—rather than platform or app use per se—is what degrades political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps (WhatsApp) acting as an indirect amplifier rather than a direct cause. [[Rossini2026-mj]] extends this by showing mainstream news use as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters, and by demonstrating that WhatsApp's effect on trust is conditional on network composition (strong-tie vs. stranger discussion)—a nuance that cautions against treating "messaging app use" as a monolithic risk factor.

## Inside the Encrypted and Hyperpartisan Networks

Two papers push past self-reported survey measures into the infrastructure of coordination itself. [[Kansaon2025-id]] offers the most granular evidence of organized manipulation in this set, adapting "Rapid Spread Network" methods from Twitter research to 13 million WhatsApp messages and finding that a concentrated set of coordinated accounts—bypassing WhatsApp's own forwarding limits—drove the doxxing of Supreme Court justices and the mobilization leading to the January 8, 2023 attacks. This is a direct empirical bridge between the "coordinated inauthentic behavior" literature on public platforms and the comparatively opaque, encrypted messaging ecosystem that [[Ventura2025-sw]] separately argues demands its own causal methodology (a deactivation field experiment) precisely because WhatsApp's private, closed structure makes platform audits like [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]'s Facebook ad-monitoring plugin inapplicable.

[[Marino2026-slef]] complicates the picture further by turning inward to the affective texture of a pro-Bolsonaro Facebook network across 2021–2023. Against the assumption—implicit in much of the misinformation-and-intolerance literature above—that hyperpartisan communities are stable echo chambers, Marino shows engagement patterns are highly volatile, pivoting sharply around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt, with evidence of external "brigading" by pro-Lula users contesting community boundaries. This finding sits in productive tension with [[Kansaon2025-id]]: the WhatsApp coordination data shows organized, durable mobilization infrastructure, while the Facebook affective data shows volatility and permeability, suggesting that coordination and community stability may be decoupled phenomena operating on different platforms and timescales within the same broader Bolsonarista movement.

## Platform Incentives and the Political Supply Side

[[Rodarte2026-sl16]] shifts attention from citizens and networks to elites, showing that Brazilian parliamentarians themselves internalize platform logics that penalize substantive legislative communication and reward antagonistic "lacração" content. This paper supplies a supply-side complement to the demand-side misinformation studies: if [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] show citizens being misled and polarized, Rodarte shows why elites have structural incentives to feed that polarization rather than correct it, closing a loop between platform architecture, elite strategy, and mass attitudes that this topic's other papers treat more separately.

## Geographic Expansion: Testing the Agenda Beyond Brazil

The remaining papers mark the deliberate comparative reach signaled in this topic's framing, testing whether Brazil-style coordinated-behavior and polarization dynamics travel. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] offers the sharpest contrast: Serbia's SNS-linked networks achieve mimetic, decentralized manipulation that evades the same detection frameworks built around the kind of centralized bot-farm behavior documented in Brazil, suggesting the Brazilian case sits on a spectrum of digital-authoritarian sophistication rather than representing its ceiling. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations in the Philippines as political brokerage, emphasizing the organizational and economic scaffolding behind manipulation—an angle that resonates with [[Kansaon2025-id]]'s finding that a small, concentrated set of coordinated accounts produces disproportionate effects, hinting at professionalized infrastructures underlying what might otherwise look like organic partisan activity. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] demonstrates that transnational coordination can also travel through ideological rather than national-electoral channels, with CitizenGO's anti-gender network showing centralized coordination from Spain alongside a distinctly Bolsonarist-aligned, Facebook-dominated Portuguese-language cluster—directly linking this topic's Brazilian core to a broader transnational far-right digital infrastructure. Finally, [[Wack2026-bt]] extends the agenda to AI-generated disinformation in Kenya, showing that deepfake impact is itself socially negotiated through partisan commentary rather than fixed at the point of fabrication—a reminder that as manipulation techniques evolve (from coordinated text networks to synthetic media), the Global South comparative lens developed around Brazil remains essential for understanding how resource constraints and moderation gaps shape vulnerability.

## Synthesis

Together these papers trace an arc from documenting coordinated and affective dynamics within Brazil's hyperpartisan networks, to testing the causal consequences of platform and app interventions on belief and trust, to asking how elites and regulators respond to (or are captured by) these dynamics, and finally to asking whether the resulting theoretical toolkit—coordinated inauthentic behavior, echo chambers, platform bans as partisan events—generalizes across Serbia, the Philippines, transnational movements, and Kenya. The throughline is a shared skepticism toward static models of polarization: bans ratchet rather than reset ([[Ventura2026-yc]]), communities are volatile rather than fixed ([[Marino2026-slef]]), and manipulation evolves faster than detection frameworks built on earlier, cruder autocratic templates ([[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]]). The agenda's next task, implicit across this set, is reconciling supply-side elite incentives, encrypted-platform infrastructure, and citizen-level belief formation into a single account of how digital polarization is produced and sustained in the Global South.
