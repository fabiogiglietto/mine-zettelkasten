# Italian Electoral Communication

## From social media hype to measured skepticism

The earliest work filed here already sets the programme's sceptical, empirical tone. [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] tests the then-fashionable claim that social media metrics can forecast elections, using Facebook 'Likes' from the 2011 Italian administrative elections, and finds only a weak, non-significant relationship between online popularity and vote share. This deflationary finding — that social media visibility is not a transparent proxy for electoral support — becomes a recurring premise for later work, which instead treats platform data as evidence of *campaign dynamics and media ecology* rather than as a predictive signal. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] extends this scepticism into the hybrid-media paradigm, showing that Twitter activity around the 2013 election talk shows was dominated by a narrow set of highly active users commenting on and reacting to televised content rather than setting the agenda. Politicians and broadcasters largely failed to activate this "second screen" audience, reinforcing a picture of social media as entangled with, rather than autonomous from, legacy broadcast logics — a necessary corrective before the programme turns to platform-native dynamics in later elections.

## Partisanship, insularity, and political parallelism

The 2018 election becomes the pivot around which the group's signature concept of *political parallelism in networked form* takes shape. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] operationalises insularity as a structural property of partisan news ecosystems on Twitter and links it to distinct engagement patterns on Facebook: insular, populist-aligned sources (especially those favoured by the Five Star Movement and the League) generate more amplifying shares, while cross-partisan sources attract more contestatory comments. Read against Hallin and Mancini's "polarized pluralist" model, this paper positions Italy as a strategic non-US case for studying how populist parties exploit fragmented, hyperpartisan media ecologies — a theme that recurs, more darkly, in the coordination studies that follow, and that is later picked up discursively by [[Mosca2026-yh]], which reframes "fake news" accusations themselves as a rhetorical weapon of negative campaigning, strategically deployed by political actors and selectively amplified by partisan media rather than simply symptomatic of misinformation.

## Detecting coordination: mapping hyperpartisan networks

The methodological core of the programme is a sustained effort to move disinformation research away from content- or actor-based detection toward *behavioural* detection of coordination. [[Giglietto2019-0bd8b768]] first operationalises Facebook's loosely defined notion of "coordinated inauthentic behavior," identifying networks around the 2018 general election and 2019 European election that amplified far-right and anti-immigration content via blacklisted sources. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] refines this into the "coordinated link sharing behavior" algorithm, showing that coordinated networks are markedly more likely to spread problematic domains, and distinguishing openly political from deceptively "non-political" coordination strategies. [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] then operationalises this as a *living* workflow rather than a static list, applied to the 2022 snap election: starting from known coordinated accounts, it iteratively surfaces new ones, uncovering a sprawling M5S-aligned hyperpartisan network, a religious-page clickbait operation, and a covert religious-proselytism network using Messenger bots — illustrating how campaign-adjacent coordination can be ideological, commercial, or religious in motivation, often blurring behind a thin screen of "non-political" branding. Together these three papers trace an arc from defining coordination, to measuring its link-sharing signature, to building infrastructure for monitoring it as it evolves — a direct methodological lineage addressing exactly the kind of campaign-specific case study (such as the "Seaside Campaign") that anchors this topic.

## Big data, platform policy, and the infrastructure of visibility

Running alongside the coordination studies is a strand concerned with how platform architecture and governance shape what can even be observed. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] frames three structural shifts — the blurring of organisational boundaries via networked collective action, micro-targeted advertising, and amplification-based manipulation — as jointly undermining both political transparency and established methods of studying it, using the Five Star Movement's "digital party" model and the Cambridge Analytica case as touchstones. This concern with platform opacity becomes directly empirical in [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]], which exploits the DSA-mandated Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by over 70%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and — crucially — allowed extremist accounts to offset per-post losses by posting far more frequently, ultimately out-reaching mainstream politicians in aggregate visibility. [[Philipp2026-tl]] zooms out to survey the broader regulatory landscape this paper exploits, cataloguing how DSA-mandated APIs and data portals unevenly reopen (and restrict) the kind of platform data that underpins nearly every coordination and parallelism study in this collection — turning data access itself into an object of study rather than merely an enabling condition.

## Discourse, rhetoric, and the limits of automated interpretation

A newer strand interrogates the *content* of Italian political discourse and the capacity of computational tools to interpret it. [[Paci2025-ag]] uses the IMPAQTS corpus of Italian political speech to show that even strong LLMs (GPT-4o-mini included) substantially underperform human experts at interpreting implicatures and presuppositions — the deliberately ambiguous, plausibly-deniable language that characterises much adversarial political rhetoric. This finding has direct methodological stakes for the programme's own increasing reliance on LLMs: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news from the 2018 and 2022 elections, finding that general-purpose LLM embeddings (text-embedding-3-large) outperform Italian-specific BERT models, while [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds and validates a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline — fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, and LLM-generated narrative labels — for the same 2018/2022 Facebook datasets, confronting the problem that validating LLM-integrated pipelines requires expert rather than crowdsourced annotation, precisely because LLMs often exceed low-skilled human performance even as [[Paci2025-ag]] shows they fall short of expert pragmatic competence on implicit content. These three papers jointly stake out the current methodological frontier of the group's empirical work on Italian elections: using LLMs as scalable analytic infrastructure while remaining acutely aware, via discourse-pragmatic evaluation, of what such models still cannot reliably do.

## A comparative coda

Though focused on Sweden rather than Italy, [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] offers an instructive point of comparison: its cross-platform analysis of the Sweden Democrats' 2024 campaign shows a populist party deliberately tailoring securitising visual rhetoric to each platform's affordances and audience — textual argument on Facebook and X, memetic virality on TikTok. This resonates with the Italian programme's recurring finding that political actors (from M5S's "anti-elitist cyber party" strategy to coordinated hyperpartisan networks) exploit platform-specific logics and plausible deniability to maximise reach while evading straightforward attribution, suggesting that the dynamics mapped so closely for Italy are instances of a broader, cross-national pattern in platformised electoral communication.
