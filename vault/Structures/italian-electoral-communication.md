# Italian Electoral Communication

## The Italian Arc: From Skepticism to Systematic Mapping

The Italian strand of the MINE programme begins with a cautionary note. [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]], examining the 2011 administrative elections, finds that Facebook "Likes" do not reliably predict vote share — an early corrective to social-media-as-oracle claims that sets a sober, empirical tone for everything that follows. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] complicates the picture further by showing, through the 2013 general election, that Twitter activity around political talk shows remained tightly coupled to television's agenda-setting logic: participation was narrow, elite-driven, and dominated by opinion and interaction requests rather than genuine agenda contribution. Together these two papers establish a baseline claim that recurs throughout the corpus — that raw metrics of online attention are not simple proxies for either electoral strength or democratic participation, and that Italian political communication must be read as a *hybrid* system in which old and new media logics interpenetrate.

## Partisan Attention, Insularity, and the Populist Advantage (2018–2019)

The programme's empirical centre of gravity is the 2018 general election and 2019 European election, anchored by [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]]. This paper operationalises "partisan attention" and "insularity" by combining Twitter-based audience signals with Facebook engagement data across 84,815 news stories, finding that sources favoured by the Five Star Movement and the League are markedly more insular than those favoured by mainstream parties — and that M5S in particular has mastered a dual strategy of amplifying favourable coverage via shares while mobilising supporters to contest unfavourable coverage via comments. This establishes political parallelism not as a static property of outlets but as a *strategic capacity* that varies by party organisation and digital infrastructure investment, echoing Hallin and Mancini's polarized-pluralist model but updating it for a platformed, populist-inflected media system.

[[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] extends this logic from attention economies to manipulation, introducing "coordinated link sharing behaviour" as a way to detect networks of pages, groups, and profiles sharing identical URLs in rapid, repeated succession across the 2018 and 2019 cycles. The key methodological move — shifting from content- or actor-based detection to *behaviour*-based detection — becomes foundational for the programme's subsequent work on coordination, and the empirical finding that coordinated networks are disproportionately linked to problematic and previously-flagged disinformation sources ties partisan parallelism directly to the information-disorder literature.

## Coordination as an Evolving Methodological Frontier

[[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] pushes the coordination paradigm into the 2022 snap election, addressing a structural weakness of list-based detection: malicious actors adapt and lists go stale. The workflow it proposes — iteratively surfacing new coordinated accounts from a seed list via near-duplicate detection — uncovers not only a hyperpartisan M5S network but also commercially and religiously motivated clickbait and proselytism operations, demonstrating that coordination detection generalises well beyond narrowly "political" actors. Read alongside [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] (on the 2019 UK election) and [[Oprea2025-lf]] (on the 2024 Romanian EP campaign), this body of work situates the Italian findings within a broader comparative claim: coordination is not binary but exists on a continuum, is largely orthogonal to automation, and manifests differently depending on national context — from the UK's small, highly coordinated activist fringe groups to Romania's hyperactive sharer accounts amplifying mainstream party pages. The Italian case contributes the crucial insight that monitoring must be dynamic and near-real-time, since static lists systematically underestimate the phenomenon's reach.

## Scaling Up: LLMs, Embeddings, and Validation Challenges

A newer methodological cluster asks how to analyse Italian electoral discourse at scale using large language models. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo model for clustering political news shared on Facebook before the 2018 and 2022 elections, finding the general-purpose LLM embedding consistently superior — a finding that complicates assumptions about the necessity of language-specific fine-tuned models. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] builds directly on this result, describing a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (fine-tuned classification, embedding-based clustering, LLM-generated cluster labels) applied to the same 2018/2022 Facebook corpus, and grapples with the validation problem this creates: since LLMs often outperform crowdworkers, expert human coders become indispensable, and cluster granularity must be assessed with multi-level rubrics. These two papers mark a methodological pivot within the Italian programme — from hand-built coordination algorithms toward generative-AI-assisted content analysis — while remaining anchored to the same election cycles and underlying datasets that ground the programme's substantive claims.

## Platform Governance, Visibility, and the Politics of Access

[[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] turns from content to *infrastructure*, using the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by up to 72%, beginning roughly ten months before Meta's announced global rollout and only partially reversing after January 2025 — with extremist accounts anomalously compensating through increased posting volume rather than reduced reach. This paper reframes algorithmic visibility itself as a terrain of political communication and ties the Italian case to the broader transparency debates catalysed by the EU Digital Services Act. It resonates with [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]], both of which map the shifting landscape of regulated data access (APIs, DSA-mandated portals, the "price, proficiency, permission" problem) that increasingly determines *what* election research on platforms like Facebook, X, and TikTok is even possible — and with [[Schulte2026-df]], which documents analogous infrastructural bottlenecks in multi-platform analysis of the 2025 German election. Read together, these papers suggest that the Italian programme's empirical findings about coordination, insularity, and visibility are inseparable from the evolving — and often opaque — governance regimes that structure researcher access to platform data.

## Comparative and Rhetorical Extensions

Several papers extend the Italian electoral-communication agenda outward, either geographically or theoretically. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] examines cross-platform visual securitisation in the Sweden Democrats' 2024 EU campaign, [[Lukito2026-il]] traces cross-platformization strategies of US right-leaning media between Twitter and Truth Social, and [[Larsson2026-nl]] offers a ten-year longitudinal account of sentiment and sharing among Norwegian parties — each providing a comparative backdrop against which the Italian findings on partisan parallelism, platform-specific strategy, and the amplification dynamics of partisan communities can be read as part of a wider European and transatlantic pattern rather than a national idiosyncrasy. Finally, [[Mosca2026-yh]] pivots from empirical measurement to rhetorical theory, proposing a Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification model that reframes "fake news" accusations in Italian newspapers as weapons of negative campaigning rather than simple misinformation claims — a conceptual move that complements the coordination and insularity literature by foregrounding how accusations of inauthenticity are themselves strategically deployed and selectively amplified within the same partisan media ecosystems the programme otherwise studies empirically.

Across this arc, the throughline is a shift from asking *whether* social media metrics matter (2011–2013) to *how* partisan communities strategically shape attention and coordination (2018–2022), and finally to *who controls the infrastructure* that makes such questions answerable at all (2023–2026) — with the Italian case repeatedly serving as both an empirical anchor and a methodological testbed for the broader comparative and infrastructural debates now reshaping election research under the Digital Services Act.
