# Italian Electoral Communication

## From Talk Shows to Timelines: Mapping the Italian Case

The line of inquiry that anchors this topic begins not with Facebook but with television. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] establishes the hybrid-media lens — audiences, journalists and politicians co-present across TV and Twitter during the 2013 Italian election — that later work in this program never abandons: media systems are treated as interdependent ecologies rather than as platforms in isolation. That framing resurfaces directly in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], which pairs Twitter-based insularity scores with Facebook engagement data on the 2018 campaign to show that populist-aligned news sources (M5S above all) are both more insular *and* more strategically amplified — shared when flattering, contested when critical. The paper's central move, showing that a party's success can be read off patterns of sharing versus commenting, sets the empirical vocabulary (insularity, amplification, contestation) that recurs throughout the MINE program.

The next methodological leap is [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], which reframes disinformation research around *coordinated link sharing behavior* rather than content veracity or actor blacklists, tracing networks across the 2018 and 2019 Italian elections and showing they are disproportionately implicated in problematic-domain circulation. This becomes the program's signature method, refined in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] into a live, iterative workflow for detecting and updating coordinated-account lists, applied to the 2022 snap election and yielding three qualitatively distinct case studies — a hyperpartisan M5S network, a clickbait operation laundered through religious pages, and a proselytising bot network — that demonstrate the method's genre-agnostic reach beyond narrowly "political" actors.

## Instrumentation: From Coordination Detection to LLM Pipelines

A second cluster turns the same Italian election corpora (2018, 2022) into a testbed for computational method. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks embedding models for clustering Italian political news, finding that general-purpose LLM embeddings outperform Italian-specific BERT variants — a finding operationalized in [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], which builds and validates a full "LLMs-in-the-loop" pipeline (classification, embedding, cluster labeling) for the same election data and confronts the harder problem of validating outputs that no longer reduce to fine-tuned single-task models. [[Paci2025-ag]] extends this scrutiny of LLM competence to a subtler register — implicature and presupposition in Italian political speech — showing that even strong models like GPT-4o-mini fall well short of expert interpretation, a sobering counterweight to the optimism of the embedding results and a reminder that automating the *reading* of political discourse remains harder than automating its *sorting*. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]], though set in the German rather than Italian context, belongs to this same instrumentation arc: it validates multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) against specialized computer-vision tools for visual political communication, extending the program's "which automated method actually works" question from text to images.

## From Coordination to Curation: Platform Power as the Object of Study

A third strand pivots from detecting manipulation *within* platforms to interrogating the platforms' own governance of visibility. [[Marino2023-9137f448]] applies the coordinated-network method to Italian Covid-19 discourse, identifying an "Intellectual Dark Web" cluster whose reach depends on legacy-media remediation — showing that hybridity, not insularity alone, drives amplification. This sets up the program's more recent and more consequential turn: [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses breakpoint detection on 2.5 million posts to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy cut Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72%, was rolled out roughly ten months before Meta's own announced timeline, and disproportionately spared extremist accounts, who compensated with posting volume — a direct empirical rebuttal of platform transparency claims. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] generalizes this insight beyond Italy, using Meta's URL dataset to show that partisan alignment penalizes and journalistic quality rewards reach independently of sharing, and that these effects move in lockstep with known governance interventions (2020 "break the glass"), reframing Facebook as an active curator rather than a neutral conduit for the same populist strategic-sharing dynamics documented back in [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]].

## Comparative and Infrastructural Extensions

The remaining papers extend the MINE toolkit and questions outward, both geographically and institutionally, testing whether Italian findings travel. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] apply cognate auditing and coordination-detection logics to Brazilian and German elections respectively; [[Oprea2025-lf]] documents hyperactive-user amplification in the 2024 Romanian EP campaign using methods congruent with the coordinated-sharing paradigm; [[Iris2026-pg]] scales the "who gets covered" question from party-level Italian analysis to a five-country comparison showing systematic media overexposure of the Radical Right ahead of the 2024 EP elections; and [[Schulte2026-df]] confronts, for the 2025 German election, the same infrastructural bottlenecks — platform access, multimodal noise, cross-platform incomparability — that increasingly constrain this entire research program. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]], [[Bouchaud2026-np]], and [[Philipp2026-tl]] step back to the regulatory and design layer, tracing how platform election policies, Community Notes moderation, and DSA-mandated data access shape what can even be observed — directly relevant to the access constraints [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] wrestle with empirically. Finally, [[Mosca2026-yh]] returns to the Italian press specifically, but flips the lens: rather than tracking coordinated networks that spread problematic content, it theorizes how "fake news" accusations themselves are weaponized rhetorically and selectively amplified by newspapers — a fitting bookend that shows disinformation discourse itself has become, in the Italian case, as strategically contested as the coordinated networks the program set out fifteen years of work to detect.
