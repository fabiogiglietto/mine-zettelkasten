# Elections & Social Media Campaigns

## Elections & Social Media Campaigns

This is the founding empirical terrain of MINE: a body of work that treats national elections as natural laboratories for observing how political actors, platforms, and publics co-produce (and contest) the information environment. Read together, the 48 papers filed here trace a widening arc — from early single-country descriptive mappings of Italian coordination and partisan news circulation, through methodologically ambitious cross-national and cross-platform comparisons, to a maturing second-order literature on data access, platform governance, and the very possibility of doing this research at all.

### The Italian core and the coordination paradigm

The programme's empirical spine runs through a sequence of Italian election studies. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the baseline diagnosis for 2018: populist parties (M5S, League) cultivate insular news ecologies on Twitter, and insularity predicts distinct Facebook engagement signatures (shares vs. comments) that amplify or contest coverage differently by party. [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] then pivots the research agenda decisively from content- and actor-based disinformation detection toward *behavior*: "coordinated link sharing behavior" is operationalized and shown to correlate strongly with problematic domains across both the 2018 and 2019 elections. This behavioral turn is institutionalized methodologically in [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], which builds a continuously-updating detection workflow for the 2022 snap election, surfacing not just political hyperpartisan networks but adjacent click-economy and religious-proselytism operations — a reminder that coordinated inauthenticity is not always ideological. The Italian corpus also anchors methodological experimentation: [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] validate LLM-based embedding and classification pipelines against the same 2018/2022 Facebook link corpora, showing how computational infrastructure itself becomes an object of study. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] close the loop by turning to platform governance directly — using the Meta Content Library to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy silently reshaped Italian MPs' reach (with extremist accounts compensating via volume), and that Facebook's share-to-view amplification is itself actively, unevenly curated rather than a neutral pass-through. Earlier still, [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] provides the historical baseline for this whole trajectory, showing that even in 2013 the "hybrid" TV–Twitter space was narrow, TV-driven, and dominated by opinion rather than agenda-building — establishing that fragmentation and elite control long precede the platforms now under scrutiny.

### Brazil: crisis narration, WhatsApp, and eroding trust

A second major cluster follows Brazil's contentious 2022 election and its build-up. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during the Manaus COVID crisis via three distinct narrative postures (derivative reporting, direct authority claims, proximity brokerage) — elites as media producers, not sources. [[Kansaon2025-id]] and [[Ventura2025-sw]] push the empirical lens into WhatsApp, the platform largely invisible to Twitter/Facebook-centric methods: coordinated groups organized doxxing and real-world protest mobilization (including the January 8 attacks), while a deactivation field experiment isolates WhatsApp's distinct causal role in shaping political knowledge and misinformation exposure — a rare move from correlational to causal identification in a closed, encrypted ecosystem. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] then connect these information-diet variables to downstream democratic attitudes: belief in electoral fraud claims predicts declining political tolerance and declining trust in results, with messaging-app news use working indirectly through misinformation belief, and mainstream news functioning as a stabilizing counterweight even among Bolsonaro voters. Together these papers reframe Brazilian misinformation not as noise to be corrected but as a strategic elite practice embedded in illiberal backsliding — a claim that resonates with [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country finding that misinformation-sharing is specifically a radical-right-populist phenomenon, not a general property of populism or right-wing ideology alone.

### Germany, Scandinavia, and cross-national comparative campaigns

A parallel comparative strand extends the coordination/visibility paradigm beyond Italy and Brazil. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] track the 2021 and 2025 German federal elections, with the latter explicitly reframing multi-platform electoral analysis as an "infrastructure problem" — X and TikTok yield structurally different, weakly-correlated modal signals (text/voice/face), and party platform choice itself is strategic. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] complements this with a visual-communication study of the 2021 German Instagram campaign, validating GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision tools and finding format-specific "concentrated visibility" strategies for front-runners. Scandinavian election studies — [[Larsson2026-ro]] on a decade of Norwegian Progress Party Facebook sentiment, and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] on Facebook emotional reactions across Norway/Sweden/Denmark — converge on a shared finding: negative and anger-eliciting content, especially from populist actors, drives sharing/virality even in comparatively low-polarization multiparty systems, while "Love" reactions behave oppositely. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] extends this into the 2024 EU elections, showing the Swedish Democrats deliberately tailoring securitizing visual rhetoric per platform (argumentative on Facebook/X, memetic and youth-targeted on TikTok) — and [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national media-visibility bias favoring the Radical Right across five countries' 2024 EU election coverage, independent of actual electoral strength. [[Oprea2025-lf]] rounds out this cluster with a Romanian case of hyperactive inauthentic Facebook sharing across all major 2024 EU-election parties, and [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links aggregate far-right electoral performance to voters' disinformation concerns across EU member states.

### The US axis: platform-scale exposure and its limits

A distinct cluster interrogates the *causal* reach and effects of coordinated and deceptive activity in US elections, largely through unprecedented platform-partnered access. [[Appel2026-qr]] and its companion [[noauthor_undated-bm]] analyze Meta's 49 identified deceptive networks around 2020, showing reach is extremely concentrated, driven mainly by reshares from unaffiliated accounts, and that observed correlations with downstream beliefs vanish under adjustment — a sobering methodological corrective to inflated causal narratives. [[Allcott2025-jb]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] extend this Meta-collaboration tradition: randomized ad removal produced no detectable attitudinal effects, while diffusion-tree analysis of a billion reshares documents how misinformation's virality is structurally distinctive but still a minority phenomenon. Against this comparatively reassuring picture stands [[Kim2026-wg]], whose direct-exposure tracking of 2016 voter-suppression ads finds sharp geo-racial targeting and real (if concentrated) turnout suppression — a reminder that average treatment effects can mask consequential targeted harms invisible to platform-aggregate designs. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] offers a formal counterpoint from network science: coordinated accounts in UK 2019 retweet cascades achieve influence far below optimal or even random-matched benchmarks, suggesting CIB's causal weight is often overstated. [[Simeone2025-vo]] complicates this skepticism by showing that targeted deplatforming of a handful of accounts *did* structurally collapse an election-denial community's authority network and mobilization capacity — coordination and its removal can matter, just not always in the ways assumed. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] pushes elite-network analysis further into a "logic of connective faction," showing how CRT's uptake by Republican elites was mediated by measurable ties to hyperpartisan media ecosystems that reward ideological extremity. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] contributes an agenda-building lens, showing social bots meaningfully shape campaign (if not press) sentiment agendas in the 2022 Ohio midterms. Finally, [[Gerard2025-br]] offers a structural innovation — narrative-cluster-based (rather than interaction-based) network embedding — to trace how a tiny set of "bridge users" seed cross-platform (Truth Social→X) narrative migration in the 2024 election, reframing influence as discursive alignment rather than direct interaction.

### Sensemaking, rumor, and the limits of "misinformation" as a category

Running orthogonal to the network-detection literature is a CSCW-inflected strand that questions whether "false content" is even the right unit of analysis. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] both analyze US election-integrity rumors (Arizona 2022, and 2020/2022 more broadly) as collective sensemaking processes in which accurate evidence is reframed by comment-level "framing actions," or sustained across cycles as evolving "deep stories" where *how* something is told matters as much as its truth-value. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] extends this reflexive turn experimentally: media narratives *about* interference themselves generate perceived polarization and animosity, regardless of whether interference campaigns are effective — suggesting alarmist discourse can be self-fulfilling. [[Bouchaud2026-np]] adds a structural diagnosis of X's Community Notes, arguing its bridging-based design systematically undermoderates precisely the polarizing content most relevant to electoral integrity. [[Gaw2025-ru]] reframes influence operations altogether as political brokerage in the 2022 Philippine elections, insisting the phenomenon is sustained by political-economic infrastructure and intermediary labor rather than isolated bad actors or bad content — a conceptual bridge between the network-coordination and elite-strategy literatures.

### Platform governance, transparency, and the crisis of access

The final and increasingly dominant thread turns the analytic lens onto the research infrastructure itself. [[Tai2026-qk]] shows elite misinformation-sharing is conditioned by institutional and ideological checks, setting up a governance question that [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] answers historically: platform election policies have moved through protective, civic-integrity-expansive, and now rollback phases, largely reactive to US election cycles and increasingly marked by "platform illiberalism." [[McNally2025-dn]] demonstrates empirically that Facebook's News Feed algorithm is not a black box but a detectable, differentially-applied sociotechnical system, particularly for hard news — directly motivating the DSA-based audit agenda pursued by [[Jurg2025-ur]] (YouTube's inconsistent authoritative-source labeling) and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] (volunteer-based ad auditing in Brazil 2018, the low-tech ancestor of these DSA-era audits). [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] survey the resulting data-access landscape directly, cataloguing DSA-mandated APIs and 72 access tools respectively, and diagnosing a "price, proficiency, permission" problem that structurally privileges WEIRD, well-resourced researchers and leaves consumption-side data scarce. Gaisbauer2025-br (filed here as a methodological critique of outlet-level, single-country news-sharing measures) and [[Brady2026-ln]] point toward the field's next frontier: the latter's Bluesky field experiment shows engagement-based ranking algorithms amplify moralized/toxic content and distort perceived social norms — while a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing platform enjoyment, suggesting prosocial algorithm design as a plausible intervention rather than a purely diagnostic endeavor. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] serves as an early theoretical anchor for this entire trajectory, naming the blurring of organizational boundaries, micro-targeted advertising, and attention-amplification as the three vectors that this whole topic cluster has since spent a decade empirically unpacking.

### The arc

Taken as a whole, the topic traces a discernible intellectual trajectory: from establishing that coordination and insularity are empirically detectable (Italy, 2018–2020), to documenting their consequences for trust, tolerance, and democratic legitimacy (Brazil, US, cross-national), to interrogating whether these effects are as causally consequential as assumed (the skeptical Meta-collaboration and network-science papers), to reflexively confronting the shrinking, unevenly-distributed infrastructure through which any of this can still be studied at all. The throughline connecting Rodarte's parliamentarians, Giglietto's coordinated Facebook pages, and Lukito's data-access taxonomy is a single, recurring question: who controls visibility, and how would we know?
