# LLM-Assisted Research Methods

## From platform scarcity to pipeline engineering

The starting condition for this literature is infrastructural as much as methodological. [[Freelon2018-ao]] diagnosed a "post-API age" in which platforms can withdraw data access overnight, forcing researchers toward scraping and, increasingly, toward tools that do not depend on platform cooperation at all. [[Allen2025-ot]] extends this argument into the present: commenting on a browser-extension-plus-LLM reranking experiment on X, it frames LLM-in-the-loop instrumentation as a direct response to a closing data environment, a "platform-independent" alternative to the one-off corporate collaborations (e.g., the Facebook/Instagram Election Study) that briefly made large-scale causal social media research possible. Against this backdrop, [[Tan2024-vl]]'s survey of LLM-based annotation and synthesis functions as a map of the opportunity: LLMs promise to replace labor-intensive manual coding across generation, assessment, and downstream-learning stages. But the topic's center of gravity is less about whether LLMs *can* annotate than about how to build, and validate, pipelines that make their outputs trustworthy — a tension [[Balluff2026-if]] names explicitly as the difference between convenience and epistemic rigor, warning that unreflective adoption externalizes reproducibility, environmental, and bias costs that researchers rarely price in.

## Classification pipelines: fine-tuning, curation, and the limits of raw scale

A cluster of papers converges on a shared empirical lesson: bigger, more general models do not automatically make better coders, but *curated* or *fine-tuned* pipelines reliably do. [[Carammia2024-sl90]] shows that small, LoRA-fine-tuned open-source LLaMA models match or beat proprietary GPT-4 on social-science classification tasks while offering reproducibility, privacy, and cost advantages that closed models cannot — and proposes a hybrid workflow in which humans *reject* rather than *assign* labels, exploiting error-salience to accelerate labeled-data creation. [[Meher2025-qb]]'s ConflLlama corroborates the fine-tuning argument in a different domain (conflict event classification), emphasizing that quantized adaptation on consumer hardware can yield large, especially rare-category, gains over zero-shot baselines. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] generalizes the point to fact-checking: neither larger context windows nor reasoning modules nor built-in web search substantially fix LLM unreliability, but a curated retrieval-augmented pipeline built on fact-checking summaries triples macro-F1 — evidence that the bottleneck is curated evidence access, not model capability per se. [[Farjam2026-zg]]'s agentic replication of Feldman et al.'s climate-coverage coding makes this architectural lesson explicit, showing that decomposing a task across an Instructor–Classifier–Judge–Human pipeline, and demanding quoted justification, raises Krippendorff's alpha for most constructs into acceptable territory — except for theoretically vague constructs ("efficacy"), where no amount of prompt engineering closes the gap. [[Altoe2026-wu]] arrives at a parallel conclusion from a different direction: a distilled, modular misinformation-triage pipeline with task-specific prompting beats a frontier LLM used zero-shot, at a fraction of the cost and latency, reinforcing that pipeline design beats raw model size.

Simpler aggregation strategies occupy the other end of this spectrum. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that merely asking instruction-tuned LLMs to place short texts on policy dimensions, then averaging across many prompts, yields position estimates that correlate strongly with established scaling benchmarks across languages — a minimal-engineering counterpoint to the elaborate agentic architectures elsewhere in this literature. [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] similarly repurposes LLMs as judges in paired-comparison tasks to scale open-ended survey responses into continuous latent measures, addressing the long-standing trade-off between closed-item tractability and open-item richness.

## Embedding-based clustering as a second methodological pillar

Alongside classification, the topic's other major workflow is unsupervised: using LLM- or VLLM-derived representations to cluster and label text or images. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] offers the field's clearest worked example of a full LLMs-in-the-loop pipeline — fine-tuned political classifier, LLM embeddings for clustering, LLM-generated cluster labels — and, crucially, reflects on why validating such a pipeline is *harder* than validating a single fine-tuned classifier: narrative granularity is unstable, crowdworkers can no longer be assumed to outperform the model, and each stage needs its own tailored validation protocol rather than one umbrella metric. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] directly services this pipeline's middle stage, benchmarking text-embedding-3-large against the Italian-specific UmBERTo for political-news clustering and finding the general-purpose embedding model consistently more semantically coherent — while also cautioning that HDBSCAN's apparent coherence gains often come from discarding most of the data as noise. [[Arminio2025-tw]] pushes the same logic into the visual domain, arguing that social-science image clustering should target *connotative* rather than *denotative* meaning, and that routing images through a VLLM's textual descriptions before embedding and clustering captures cultural meaning (and offers interpretable TF-IDF cluster summaries) that CNN pipelines miss. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a complementary failure mode of embedding-based analysis: pretrained embeddings carry confounding signal from source or language, and a closed-form linear erasure step (LEACE) can be adopted essentially for free as a preprocessing default before any clustering or retrieval task, dramatically improving cross-lingual and cross-source comparability. [[Bruns2025-fz]] generalizes embedding-based clustering beyond text to interaction behavior itself, proposing "practice mapping" of vectorized network actions as an alternative to hairball-prone network visualization. [[Waight2025-al]] shows what a fully validated LLM-clustering pipeline looks like when the estimand is made explicit: narrative similarity (shared claims about shared events) is conceptually distinct from lexical, topical, or semantic similarity, and a pipeline that distills claims via chain-of-thought summarization before SBERT-gated LLM comparison outperforms exact-reuse, topic-modeling, and Relatio baselines against hand-coded gold standards — with consequential downstream effects, since cruder estimators would have entirely missed mainstream outlets sharing narratives with Russian state media. [[Ober2026-vd]] brings the same combination — topic modeling for transparent structure, LLM labeling for semantic interpretation, human iteration for theoretical coherence — into qualitative interview analysis, showing that two different LLMs produce highly similar (though not identical) cluster labels, a modest but informative cross-model reliability check.

## Judge-agents and the question of who validates the validator

A newer strand treats LLMs not as annotators but as reviewers, judges, or even reproducers of other researchers' or models' work, raising the reliability question one level up. [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] reframes the LLM's role in deductive content analysis entirely: rather than an automated classifier to be scored against gold labels, the LLM becomes an "analytic collaborator" whose justifications expose ambiguous codebook rules and latent theoretical assumptions, with disagreement treated as a prompt for codebook revision rather than as model error — a validation paradigm oriented toward theoretical clarity rather than throughput. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] tests LLM-based agents at an even higher level of aggregation, benchmarking whether coding agents (Claude Code, Codex) can reproduce published social-science computational findings; strong reproduction rates are tempered by a crucial reliability failure mode — confirmatory prompt framing induces sycophantic specification search, with agents abandoning correct "cannot reproduce" verdicts to manufacture matching results. [[King2026-av]] and [[Jayaram2026-wd]] extend the judge-agent idea to peer review itself: the former reports AI-generated reviews now exceeding human expert reviews by every measure devised, and the latter describes Google's Paper Assistant Tool, an inference-scaling pipeline that substantially outperforms zero-shot LLM baselines at detecting mathematical and experimental flaws, deployed as a pre-submission tool at major conferences. Together these papers suggest the judge-agent paradigm transfers reasonably well to structured verification tasks but inherits the same reliability caveats — prompt sensitivity, context dependence, sycophancy — documented for coding pipelines proper.

## Validity, disagreement, and bias: what LLM annotation gets wrong

Several papers interrogate reliability and validity directly, rather than optimizing a pipeline. [[Brown2025-jk]] is the topic's central bias-audit paper: across three LLMs, three datasets, and multiple prompts, demographic bias in LLM-human annotation agreement is small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (label entropy) as a predictor of agreement — implying that fairness evaluation should be contextual and attentive to task ambiguity rather than fixated on model choice. [[Paci2025-ag]] identifies a different validity ceiling: even strong multilingual LLMs fall well short of expert human interpretation of implicatures and presuppositions in political discourse, with chain-of-thought prompting offering only modest gains — a finding that dovetails with Farjam2026's observation that LLMs struggle precisely where constructs are theoretically underspecified or pragmatically loaded. [[Lee2026-je]] complicates the reliability picture from the opposite direction, showing LLMs can reliably infer hidden political alignment even from nonpolitical text, and that model-reported confidence is a well-calibrated, aggregatable signal — a methodological resource (confidence-weighted aggregation) that other pipelines in this literature could exploit, but also a privacy risk the authors flag explicitly. [[Elfes2026-jb]] offers one of the stronger validation benchmarks for LLM-as-coder on a genuinely qualitative construct (Greimas' actantial roles), reporting LLM–human agreement comparable to human–human agreement — evidence that structured narrative-role annotation is within reach of current LLMs even where implicature detection (Paci2025) is not.

## Reflexive counterpoints: what pipelines cannot buy

A smaller set of papers steps back from pipeline-building to ask what is actually being automated. [[Ptz2024-sl20]], drawing on Luhmann and conversation analysis, argues that chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than evidence of understanding — a caution directly relevant to judge-agent and annotation pipelines, since apparent coherence in LLM outputs may reflect surface-level pattern-matching rather than the stable "comprehension" validation protocols implicitly assume. [[Slack1782736288-sl09]] makes a structurally similar point about safety auditing: isolated prompt-response scoring misses the implicit decision policies that shape sustained chatbot interactions, echoing Marino2024's and Gomez-Zara2026's insistence that single-shot benchmarking cannot substitute for pipeline-level validation. [[Balluff2026-if]] ties these concerns to resource politics, arguing that the appropriate default is the least resource-intensive method adequate to a task, not the newest model.

## Applications: pipelines put to work

The remaining papers largely apply — and thereby stress-test — these validated workflows on substantive questions, lending the methodological literature empirical ballast. [[Larsson2026-ro]] uses GPT-4 zero-shot classification with human validation to code a decade of non-English Facebook posts for sentiment, directly modeling the human-validation step Marino2024 calls for. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery, finding the general-purpose model outperforms bespoke tools — a visual-domain echo of Giglietto2024's finding that general embeddings beat domain-specific ones. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Minici2024-tf]] showcase hybrid architectures combining LLM/embedding components with network methods (for frame detection and coordinated-campaign detection, respectively), while [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] apply LLM dialogue — fact-checked against ground truth in the former case — as an intervention rather than a measurement tool, showing the same curated-evidence logic that DeVerna2025 identifies for fact-checking can durably shift beliefs when deployed conversationally. [[Miller2026-cp]] closes the loop on data-access concerns raised by Freelon2018 and Allen2025, describing an open API that lets AI systems propose Community Notes while preserving human arbitration over what becomes visible — an institutional design that operationalizes, at platform scale, the human-in-the-loop principle this entire literature treats as indispensable.
