# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The arc of inquiry

This cluster traces a single methodological wager: that the political effects of digital media — advertising, algorithmic curation, elite messaging, and now AI-generated persuasion — can be recovered from large-scale behavioral traces and quasi-experimental designs rather than survey self-report alone. The papers move from foundational measurement tools (ideology scaling from network and sharing data) through increasingly ambitious causal designs (feed-algorithm switches, ad removals, platform deactivations) to the newest frontier: LLMs as both measurement instruments and active persuasive agents whose effects must themselves be causally audited.

## Measuring ideology and slant at scale

The methodological bedrock is laid by [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]], which show that Twitter following and retweeting behavior encodes a recoverable, validated ideological dimension for elites and ordinary users alike, and that the degree of "echo chamber" behavior is topic- and time-contingent rather than fixed. This homophily-based logic is extended in several directions: [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes it from following to *sharing* behavior, jointly scaling media outlets, politicians, and the public on one axis and showing that electoral competitiveness predicts elite polarization in what gets shared; [[Lai2024-to]] pushes the same correspondence-analysis logic from actors to *content*, inferring the ideology of individual YouTube videos from cross-subreddit linking patterns. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] inverts the question, showing that a platform's own recommender embeddings *inadvertently* encode a near-perfect linear direction for Left-Right ideology, raising profiling and GDPR concerns and offering a projection-based fix. The newest wave swaps network inference for language models: [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that prompting-and-averaging with instruction-tuned LLMs reproduces established ideological scaling benchmarks, while [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical talk, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary — a capability that is methodologically powerful but, as the paper itself stresses, also a privacy threat. Together these papers chart a methodological progression from elite roll-call analogues, to mass-public network signals, to content-level and now LLM-inferred ideology — each step widening what can be scaled but also what can be surveilled.

## Algorithms, feeds, and the causal effects of exposure

A second cluster asks whether algorithmic curation itself shapes political attitudes, using randomized or quasi-randomized interventions. The 2020 Meta-partnered studies — [[Guess2023-ur]] (chronological vs. algorithmic feed) and [[Guess2023-ai]] (reshare removal) and [[Allcott2025-jb]] (political ad removal) — converge on a striking null: despite large changes in the on-platform content mix, none detect meaningful shifts in polarization, knowledge, or behavior. This null result becomes a foil for later work. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] revisits the same design on X post-Musk acquisition and finds a real, asymmetric conservative shift, explained by a persistence mechanism — algorithmic exposure changes *who users follow*, which outlasts the algorithm itself — that helps reconcile the earlier Meta nulls with newer positive findings. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] dissects the mechanism further, showing that apparent right-leaning amplification on Twitter/X is largely explained by rewarding "agitating" content and proximity to Elon Musk rather than ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lose visibility. [[Brown2026-br]] applies a seed-and-traversal audit design to YouTube and similarly separates algorithmic "rabbit holes" (strong) from "echo chambers" (weak, mostly user-driven) and "radicalization pathways" (absent), while still finding a platform-wide conservative tilt. [[Brady2026-ln]] extends this experimental tradition normatively, testing whether redesigned ("diversified extremity") algorithms can counteract engagement-based amplification of moralized, toxic content and the resulting misperception of social norms — reframing the polarization question around norm distortion rather than direct attitude change. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] takes a complementary structural angle, showing that simply hiding political elites' posts (rather than content moderation broadly) causally reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture.

## Misinformation, suppression, and epidemic consequences

A parallel strand asks whether exposure to specific harmful content causally changes real-world outcomes. [[Eady2023-xg]] finds that exposure to Russian IRA accounts in 2016 was heavily concentrated among already-committed partisans and dwarfed by domestic sources, with no detectable attitudinal effect — a minimal-effects result that [[Kim2026-wg]] directly complicates by showing that *average* treatment effects obscure severe, geo-racially targeted turnout suppression among specific subpopulations exposed to voter-suppression ads, including IRA-linked material. [[Allen2024-av]] applies a similar exposure-times-persuasion logic to COVID vaccine content, finding that unflagged but "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content did far more aggregate harm than flagged misinformation, because reach outweighed per-exposure potency. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] closes the loop between online speech and offline consequence most literally, building an epidemiological (SIRVA) model to estimate that antivaccine tweets caused measurable excess cases and deaths. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] challenges the field's standard "direct referrer" measurement convention itself, showing that counterfactual, embedded-pathway estimates of platforms' misinformation contribution are many times larger than direct-link measures — a methodological corrective that resonates with [[Guess2023-ai]]'s and [[Guess2023-ur]]'s caution about measuring exposure effects narrowly. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] shift focus to elite and crowd-sourced accountability, finding persistent partisan asymmetries in misinformation flagging (Republicans flagged more even under bridging-based Community Notes) and institutional/ideological checks on elected officials' own misinformation sharing. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends the causal-deactivation paradigm beyond public feeds to WhatsApp in Brazil, arguing that private messaging platforms demand separate causal scrutiny from feed-based social media.

## Media capture and covert advertiser influence

A distinct set of papers turns from user-facing feeds to the political economy of news production itself, asking how advertising dollars and elite pressure cover covertly for narrative control. [[Balluff2026-bv]] offers the clearest causal-computational case study: difference-in-differences analysis of Austrian news coverage shows how government advertising revenue bought increased visibility (though not pure favorability) for a chancellor, with "pay positive to go negative" dynamics displacing bias onto his rivals — demonstrating that media capture mechanisms operate even in established democracies, not just hybrid regimes. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] examines the Meta side of the reach equation, showing that Facebook's share-to-view amplification is neither linear nor neutral: partisan content is systematically penalized and high-quality journalism rewarded, with these penalties and rewards fluctuating in lockstep with known platform governance interventions — direct evidence against a "neutral conduit" framing of the platform. [[Rogers2026-zz]] historicizes this terrain, tracking how Meta's redefinition of "fake news" into "borderline content" has depoliticized moderation rhetoric even as the borderline content that does perform well remains overwhelmingly right-wing, and documents the shrinking data-access regime that constrains this very kind of audit. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] frame the underlying structural problem: ad delivery algorithms opaquely shape reach and cost in political campaigns, and the blurred boundaries of networked political organizations make transparency tools like the Facebook Ad Library only partially adequate to track micro-targeting and coordinated amplification.

## LLMs as persuaders — and the limits of their transparency

The most rapidly developing subfield treats conversational AI not as a measurement tool but as a persuasive actor whose causal power must itself be audited. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes what makes LLMs persuasive — post-training and prompting strategies matter more than scale, and information density is the central mechanism — while documenting a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the comparison, showing AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the same fact-density mechanism implicated, and with effects extending to real monetary donations. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this with multi-country field experiments, finding AI persuasion exceeds traditional political ads and works more on out-partisans than co-partisans, while flagging a partisan asymmetry in the accuracy of AI claims favoring right-leaning candidates. Two papers complicate the optimistic persuasion story by foregrounding trust and disclosure: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that perceived political bias in the messenger — not the message — attenuates persuasion by up to 28%, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing AI identity (as mandated by the EU AI Act) barely matters, whereas disclosing *persuasive intent* roughly halves effectiveness — jointly suggesting that the "politically contingent" and "intent-sensitive" nature of AI persuasion, not mere automation, is the key regulatory lever.

## Computational infrastructure: tools, discourse ecosystems, and their limits

A final set of papers builds or stress-tests the computational tools underlying this entire research program, and surveys the discursive ecosystems in which political media circulates. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] extend content analysis into visual and multimodal registers, showing GPT-4o's strength in political image analysis and arguing that framing analysis must move beyond text-only, fixed-category approaches. [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates that efficient LLM fine-tuning (QLoRA) can make domain-specific political-event classification accessible on consumer hardware, while [[Paci2025-ag]] offers an important cautionary counterpoint: current LLMs still struggle badly with the implicit, pragmatic content — implicature, presupposition — that is central to manipulative political discourse, showing clear limits to automation-based text analysis. Beyond tool-building, several papers map discourse ecosystems and their stakeholders: [[Bailard2024-pj]] links online collective-action framing to offline Proud Boys violence via Granger causality; [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] shows social bots function as agenda-builders against organizational campaign messaging; [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits YouTube's promotion of "authoritative" sources and opaque removal practices during EU elections; [[Richter2026-bt]] traces the shifting stakeholder composition of AI discourse on Twitter across a decade, contrasting German institutionalization with American industry/influencer dominance; and [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] step back to examine AI hype itself as a structuring journalistic and geopolitical narrative, in the US/German and Global North/South contexts respectively. Gaisbauer2025-df (methodological) and [[Much2026-gu]] (substantive, on gendered podcast ecosystems and the 2024 youth gender gap) round out the picture, each pushing the research program's boundaries — toward multi-level measurement of news circulation and toward previously under-studied "gendered media spaces" as a distinct, non-ideological channel of political persuasion.

Read together, the throughline is a steady broadening of both the objects measured (from legislators' roll calls to users' feeds to AI chatbots' own outputs) and the causal ambition of the designs used to study them — alongside a recurring tension between what platforms and models reveal about themselves (through audits, embeddings, and disclosure experiments) and what remains structurally opaque to the researchers trying to hold them accountable.
