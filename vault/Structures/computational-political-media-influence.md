# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The causal turn in platform effects research

A defining feature of this literature is its shift from correlational or observational claims about "social media effects" toward designs that isolate causal mechanisms—field experiments, deactivation studies, and quasi-experimental audits. [[Allcott2025-jb]] exemplifies the null-result strand of this tradition: randomly removing political ads from Facebook/Instagram feeds for six weeks before the 2020 US election produced no detectable shifts in knowledge, polarization, or perceptions, though the study was underpowered for turnout and vote choice. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends deactivation logic to WhatsApp in Brazil, pushing the field beyond feed-based platforms into private messaging. By contrast, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds *positive* evidence: switching X users from chronological to algorithmic feeds shifted attitudes rightward, with effects persisting asymmetrically because algorithmic exposure induces users to follow conservative accounts they keep following even after the algorithm is switched off—a mechanism that helps reconcile the largely null Meta-collaborative findings with algorithmic-amplification claims. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] and [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] similarly use platform-independent client-side interventions to isolate specific mechanisms—elite visibility and behavioral/interactional confounds, respectively—showing that apparent partisan asymmetries in algorithmic amplification often dissolve once agitation, elite proximity, and verification status are controlled for. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a meta-commentary on this turn, arguing that as API access closes, "platform-independent" experimental tools (browser extensions, custom clients) are becoming essential infrastructure for causal inference, occupying a productive middle ground between lab experiments and rare platform collaborations like the Facebook and Instagram Election Study (FIES) that underlies [[noauthor_undated-bm]] and [[Allcott2025-jb]].

Several papers extend causal designs to specific harms. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] links geolocated antivaccine tweets to county-level vaccination and mortality outcomes via a compartmental epidemic model, while [[Kim2026-wg]] demonstrates that 2016 US voter-suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and that heterogeneous treatment effects—concentrated among targeted minority populations—were obscured by prior average-treatment-effect designs relying on indirect exposure. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] challenges the field's dominant "direct referrer" measurement paradigm itself, showing via counterfactual simulation that platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is 1.5–15x larger than direct-referral studies suggest, with sharply platform-specific and partisan-asymmetric pathways.

## Media capture and covert advertiser influence

A second cluster investigates how money and political power covertly shape news coverage. [[Balluff2026-bv]] provides a rare quasi-experimental test of media capture in a consolidated Western democracy: using difference-in-differences on Austrian news corpora, it shows that government advertising to the tabloid OE24 coincided with a doubling of Sebastian Kurz's visibility and increasingly negative coverage of his rivals—a "pay positive to go negative" signature of capture that evades direct transactional evidence. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national visibility bias favoring the Radical Right across five European countries' 2024 election coverage, independent of outlet orientation or popularity, suggesting capture-like dynamics need not require direct payment to operate at scale. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] complements this with an account of "connective faction," showing how hyper-partisan media reward ideologically extreme Republican elites (via the CRT issue) with disproportionate attention and engagement, creating feedback loops that radicalize intra-party politics. Together these papers reframe media bias not as static ideological slant but as a dynamic, incentive-driven relationship between elites, advertisers, and outlets—continuous with [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s finding that misinformation-sharing by parliamentarians across 26 countries is driven specifically by the *interaction* of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone, and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]]'s demonstration that Facebook's own reach algorithms actively penalize partisan content and reward journalistic quality in ways that fluctuate with political crises—evidence that platforms, too, are active curators rather than neutral conduits.

Ad transparency and irregularity detection form an adjacent strand: [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] deploys volunteer browser-plugin auditing to surface irregular electoral ads in Brazil, while [[Votta2025-xz]] examines how delivery algorithms (not just advertiser targeting) shape the reach and cost of political ads on Meta and Google, raising transparency concerns that echo the covert-influence themes of the media capture literature.

## Detecting coordinated and inauthentic influence

A parallel body of work develops methods for detecting covert or coordinated manipulation of the information environment at scale. [[noauthor_undated-bm]] offers the most comprehensive platform-level accounting, using Meta's exposure data to show that a handful of "deceptive networks" (both politically and financially motivated) reached tens of millions of US users, disproportionately amplified by ordinary resharers rather than the networks themselves—while cautioning that naive associations between exposure and downstream political outcomes evaporate under adjustment. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE/t-CANE framework reconceives cross-platform influence structurally, identifying small sets of "bridge users" who disproportionately seed narrative migration between Truth Social and X, extending brokerage theory to discourse networks. [[Smith2025-kc]] identifies a related but distinct mechanism—"attention brokerage"—whereby amplification by high-degree accounts causally accelerates network-tie formation, offering a causal microfoundation for triad transitivity. [[Bailard2024-pj]] applies Granger causality to Proud Boys' Telegram discourse, showing that framing dynamics (not explicit calls to action) predict offline violence in a reciprocal online–offline cycle. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extends agenda-building theory to non-human actors, finding social bots function as agenda-builders against campaign messaging, particularly through negative sentiment. [[Renault2025-uh]] uses X's Community Notes as a bias-resistant instrument to show Republican posts are flagged as misleading 2.3x more often than Democratic posts, challenging claims that fact-checking asymmetries reflect partisan bias in evaluators rather than differential misinformation-sharing. [[Beacken2026-zb]] and [[Waight2026-ts]] push this detection agenda into the generative-AI era: the former comparatively analyzes GenAI propaganda across six weakened democracies, arguing effects are structurally contingent rather than uniform, while the latter shows state media control is already embedded in LLM training data, causing measurable pro-regime output shifts correlated with a country's press-freedom levels—a novel "laundering" pathway for institutional influence.

## Latent ideology estimation and multimodal signal extraction

A methodological throughline concerns recovering latent ideological signal from behavioral or content traces at scale. [[Eady2025-vm]] establishes a joint measurement model estimating ideology for media outlets, politicians, and ordinary users from URL-sharing behavior alone, finding that electorally uncompetitive politicians share more polarized content—linking measurement innovation directly to a normative claim about gerrymandering and polarization. [[Lai2024-to]] extends this genealogy (legislators → elites → mass public) to video content, inferring YouTube video ideology from Reddit cross-posting patterns. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a simpler LLM-prompting-and-averaging approach to positioning political texts, validated across languages and text types, while [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] and [[Lee2026-je]] investigate ideology as something platforms and models *infer* about users, sometimes inadvertently: X's recommender system encodes a near-perfect linear left-right direction in its embeddings independent of demographics, and LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical text, exploiting culturally politicized lexical cues. These findings raise privacy and profiling concerns that echo [[Waight2026-ts]] and connect to [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Arora2025-tx]], which extend computational content analysis into visual and multimodal domains—showing multimodal LLMs can outperform specialized computer-vision pipelines for analyzing campaign imagery, and that framing itself is inherently multimodal. [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] round out the methods conversation: the former demonstrates that efficiently fine-tuned open-source LLMs dramatically improve conflict-event classification accessibility, while the latter uses longitudinal stakeholder typologies to trace how AI discourse itself was shaped by diverging industry/institutional actors in the US and Germany—a reminder that the tools used to study political communication are themselves objects of political-communication analysis.

## The generative-AI persuasion frontier

The newest and fastest-growing cluster investigates conversational AI as a persuasive political actor in its own right, extending advertising-effects questions into human-AI dialogue. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish the empirical baseline: post-training and prompting strategies (not scale or personalization) drive persuasiveness, with information density as the core mechanism, and frontier AI systems now out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, translating into real donation behavior. [[Lin2025-xp]] confirms these effects generalize across four national election contexts, finding AI persuades primarily through facts rather than manipulation, though right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] complicates this picture by showing persuasion is not politically neutral in its reception: perceived out-party bias in the AI itself sharply attenuates persuasive success via increased user pushback rather than disengagement. [[Wack2026-bt]] extends the AI-persuasion frontier to deepfakes, showing via a Kenyan field experiment that a deepfake's impact is not fixed but socially negotiated through surrounding audience commentary—a "collective sensemaking" model that bridges AI-generated content research with the media-effects tradition. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] closes the loop by showing that even *discourse about* election interference—independent of whether interference is real or effective—can itself produce polarizing effects, suggesting that alarmist coverage of the very phenomena this topic catalogs may compound the democratic harms it aims to document. Finally, [[Voelkel2026-lc]] provides a sobering counterpoint from adjacent persuasion research: even the most-cited climate-messaging strategies, when tested at megastudy scale, produce only small attitudinal effects and no behavioral change—a reminder that not all computationally-scaled persuasion claims survive rigorous replication, and that the levers driving AI persuasion (fact density, conversational interactivity) may be qualitatively different from those governing traditional message-based campaigns.
