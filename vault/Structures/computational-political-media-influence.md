# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The methodological backbone: scaling ideology from behavioral traces

A substantial thread in this literature is foundational and methodological: how do we measure ideology at scale from digital traces rather than self-report or roll-call votes? [[Barbera2015-je]] establishes the canonical approach—Bayesian ideal-point estimation from Twitter "following" networks, treating follows as costly homophilic signals. [[Barbera2015-fw]] extends this to show that the resulting echo-chamber effect is topic- and time-contingent rather than a fixed property of online communication. This measurement tradition cascades forward: [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes from follower networks to shared URLs, jointly placing media outlets, politicians, and the mass public on a common ideological scale and showing that polarized politicians disproportionately drive the shared information ecosystem, with implications for how electoral competitiveness shapes polarized sharing. [[Lai2024-to]] pushes the paradigm into a new content domain, inferring the ideology of YouTube videos from cross-subreddit sharing patterns via correspondence analysis. More recently, LLMs have become ideology-scaling instruments themselves: [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that simply asking and averaging LLM judgments can replicate established ideological scaling benchmarks across languages and text types, while [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse—raising the stakes of ideology inference from a research tool to a potential surveillance and microtargeting risk. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] closes the loop by showing that commercial recommender systems (X's "Who to Follow") inadvertently *learn* a linear ideological direction in their embeddings without being designed to, empirically vindicating the linear representation hypothesis these measurement papers presuppose.

## Covert influence, media capture, and propaganda infrastructures

A second cluster investigates how political actors and states covertly shape media content and coverage rather than merely target audiences with ads. [[Balluff2026-bv]] offers a rigorous quasi-experimental (difference-in-differences) detection of "journalism for sale" in Austria, showing that government advertising revenue is associated with increased visibility—though not favorability—for the paying politician and increased negativity toward his rivals, a "pay positive to go negative" signature of soft capture in an established democracy. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complements this with a relational, entity-network approach to alternative media, finding that alternative outlets don't diverge from legacy media in *which* actors they cover so much as in *how* they structurally organize coverage and reference the mainstream. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Knupfer2025-vt]] trace propaganda infrastructures at larger scale: Sky News Australia's transformation into a node of transnational right-wing "network propaganda," and the "logic of connective faction," whereby intra-party ideological subgroups build symbiotic ties to hyper-partisan media ecosystems that reward and radicalize them. [[Waight2026-ts]] extends covert influence into the AI supply chain itself, showing that state-coordinated media is memorized by LLM training corpora and causally shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence—effectively laundering authoritarian media control into ostensibly neutral AI. Together these papers reframe "media capture" as a family of detectable statistical signatures—visibility shifts, network topology changes, training-data contamination—rather than a single transactional scandal.

## Targeted advertising and its causal effects on behavior

The classic ad-effects literature is represented by a set of large field experiments and quasi-experiments testing whether exposure to political advertising changes real-world outcomes. [[Kim2026-wg]] uses direct ad-tracking data to show that Russian IRA—linked voter-suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and plausibly reduced turnout substantially among their intended targets, even though average treatment effects looked negligible—a methodological lesson echoed by [[Eady2023-xg]], which finds Russian influence-campaign exposure on Twitter was heavily concentrated among already-Republican users and shows no detectable relationship to attitude or vote change, situating itself against alarmist interference narratives. [[Allcott2025-jb]] similarly finds no detectable effects of removing political ads from Facebook/Instagram feeds before the 2020 election, while noting most ads targeted co-partisans rather than persuadable voters. [[Votta2025-xz]] shifts focus from ad content to the algorithmic machinery of delivery, arguing that ad-serving algorithms shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigns and regulators alike. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] examines ad content and platform-tailoring directly, showing how a populist party's securitizing visual campaign is adapted differently across Facebook, X, and TikTok to target younger audiences with memetic disinformation. [[Much2026-gu]] extends the advertising/targeting logic to a novel medium—entertainment podcasts as "gendered media spaces"—arguing Trump's campaign exploited gender-based rather than ideology-based audience sorting to reach young men.

## Algorithmic curation as a causal lever on attitudes

A closely related cluster asks whether feed-ranking and recommendation algorithms—rather than advertising per se—causally shape political attitudes, extending the Meta-collaboration tradition. [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Guess2023-ai]] find that chronological-vs-algorithmic feeds and reshare removal on Facebook/Instagram substantially alter content exposure but produce null effects on polarization and most attitudes, establishing an influential null-effects baseline. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] complicates this baseline: switching X users onto the algorithmic feed shifts attitudes conservative, but switching off does not reverse the effect, because the algorithm changes *who users follow*, a persistence mechanism that helps reconcile the Meta nulls with X's apparent effects. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] dissects the mechanism further, showing that apparent right-wing algorithmic amplification on post-Musk Twitter is largely explained by rewards for agitating content and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se. [[Brown2026-br]] applies a similar supply/demand disentangling logic to YouTube, finding mild echo-chamber effects driven by user choice, strong content "rabbit holes," but no radicalization pathway—alongside a platform-wide mild conservative tilt. [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] push into intervention design: a "diversified extremity" Bluesky algorithm reduces exposure to moralized/toxic content and corrects social-norm misperception, while blocking political elites on X reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing—both demonstrating that targeted algorithmic redesign, rather than blunt ad or feed removal, can shift downstream epistemic outcomes. [[Allen2025-ot]] offers a reflective bridge across this cluster, arguing that platform-independent experimental tools (browser extensions, custom clients) are now essential given closing API access, and that apparent discrepancies between studies likely reflect differences in intervention level and platform moderation regime rather than contradictory truths.

## Misinformation exposure, virality, and causal attribution of harm

Several papers quantify the real-world causal impact of problematic content, often correcting for exposure-concentration effects that trouble naive virality narratives. [[Allen2024-av]] shows that unflagged, mainstream-sourced "vaccine-skeptical" content had 46 times greater aggregate persuasive impact than flagged misinformation, because reach dwarfs per-exposure persuasiveness—a lesson about where moderation should focus. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] finds untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram concentrated among few users with no measurable attitudinal effect, while [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] takes the opposite tack, building an epidemiological causal model (SIRVA) to estimate that antivaccine tweets caused thousands of vaccine refusals and measurable excess deaths. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] reframes the entire causal-attribution question by introducing an "embedded referrer paradigm," showing that direct-exposure methods radically underestimate platforms' true counterfactual contribution to misinformation exposure—a methodological corrective relevant to all the exposure-effect papers in this group. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] interrogates a related assumption—that coordinated inauthentic accounts drive cascade virality—finding their observed influence is close to random placement, far below strategic optimum, challenging prevailing narratives about coordinated manipulation's causal weight.

## Persuasion by conversational AI: a new causal frontier

The most rapidly developing cluster concerns LLMs as direct political persuaders, extending classic ad-effects questions into dialogic AI. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically maps the "levers" of AI persuasion, identifying information density as the central mechanism and documenting a persuasion-accuracy trade-off; [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing frontier AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, again implicating information throughput. [[Lin2025-xp]] demonstrates these effects translate into real voting-intention shifts across four elections, with a troubling asymmetry whereby right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate. [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows such persuasion generalizes across structurally distinct issues (climate, vaccines, inequality) and works best on skeptics via adaptive dialogue strategies. Two papers interrogate the boundary conditions and governance of this persuasive power: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that perceived partisan bias in the AI messenger substantially attenuates persuasion, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing mere AI identity (as under the EU AI Act) does little, whereas disclosing persuasive *intent* cuts effectiveness roughly in half—arguing that meaningful transparency regulation must target purpose, not just machine-identity.

## Discourse, framing, and the wider media-AI environment

A final set of papers situates these empirical findings within broader discursive and infrastructural context. [[Dodds2026-df]], [[Richter2026-bt]], and [[Mota2026-sl59]] examine the meta-level discourse about AI itself—as systemic hype infrastructure, as a stakeholder contest between institutionalized and influencer-driven actors, and as geopolitically asymmetric media framing (risk-oriented in the Global North, opportunity-oriented in the Global South). [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] push methodological innovation in framing and news-sharing measurement (multi-modal frame extraction; multi-level "political cartography"), while [[Gerard2025-br]] models cross-platform narrative diffusion structurally, identifying small sets of "bridge users" who seed migrating narratives between Truth Social and X. [[UnknownUnknown-db]] stands somewhat apart, surveying Claude users on AI's economic effects, but it shares the volume's commitment to combining self-report with large-scale behavioral/usage data to adjudicate contested claims about technology's societal impact—mirroring the broader arc of this topic: moving from correlational and anecdotal claims about media influence toward large-scale, causally identified, and mechanistically explained accounts of how digital platforms, advertising, and now generative AI shape political belief and behavior.
