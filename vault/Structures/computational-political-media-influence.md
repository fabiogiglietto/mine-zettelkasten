# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The arc: exposure, capture, and signal

These three papers form a chain of computational inquiry into how political actors and media systems use digital advertising and content ecosystems to shape public discourse. They differ sharply in method and even in what they treat as the fundamental object of study, but together they cover three moments in the causal pathway from paid or produced political communication to political outcomes: whether exposure to targeted ads changes minds ([[Allcott2025-jb]]), whether advertising revenue can buy favorable coverage from ostensibly independent news outlets ([[Balluff2026-bv]]), and how the ideological content of video media can be measured at scale once it circulates ([[Lai2024-to]]).

## Advertising's persuasive power versus its transactional power

[[Allcott2025-jb]] and [[Balluff2026-bv]] can be read as two studies of "political advertising" that turn out to concern almost entirely different phenomena. Allcott et al. use a large-scale randomized field experiment—removing political ads from users' Facebook and Instagram feeds for six weeks before the 2020 US election—to isolate the *persuasive* effect of ad exposure on ordinary voters. The headline finding is a null: no detectable movement in knowledge, polarization, perceptions, or (within power limits) turnout and vote choice, consistent with an ad market that was, as they show, overwhelmingly oriented toward mobilizing co-partisans and soliciting donations rather than persuading undecided voters. Balluff et al., by contrast, examine advertising not as a stimulus aimed at voters but as a *currency* exchanged between politicians and media outlets—government advertising revenue allegedly traded for favorable coverage in the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre." Their difference-in-differences and synthetic-DiD analysis of over 200,000 news articles finds a large, robust effect, not on favorability toward the paying politician, but on his visibility and on the negativity directed at his rivals—a "pay positive to go negative" signature of media capture. Read side by side, the two papers suggest that the causal traceability of political advertising effects depends heavily on which actor is being incentivized: individual voters exposed to fleeting ad impressions may be remarkably resistant to measurable influence, while institutional actors (editors, outlets) responding to sustained financial relationships show large, systematic behavioral shifts. This is as much a methodological contrast as a substantive one: Allcott et al. achieve internal validity through randomization but face the well-known problem of underpowered detection of rare political behaviors (turnout, vote switching), whereas Balluff et al. cannot randomize a covert bribery scheme and instead rely on parallel-trends assumptions and synthetic control weighting to approximate a counterfactual media system.

## Measuring the medium: from behavior to ideology

[[Lai2024-to]] operates at a different level of the same broad problem space. Rather than testing the causal effect of advertising or estimating capture, it develops infrastructure for *measuring* the ideological content of political media itself—here, YouTube videos—using patterns of Reddit sharing to place videos on a latent left-right dimension via correspondence analysis. This connects to the other two papers in a foundational sense: both the capture study and the ad-effects study presuppose that we can characterize the ideological or evaluative content of political media (coverage of Kurz becoming "more negative," ads being seen by "co-partisans"), but neither directly measures latent ideological slant at the level of individual content items. Lai et al.'s approach, extending latent ideology estimation from legislators and elite social media accounts down to individual pieces of video content, offers a template for exactly the kind of content-level ideology signal that studies of media capture or ad targeting might eventually incorporate—for instance, to ask whether captured outlets shift not just visibility and tone but underlying ideological framing, or whether politically targeted ads cluster around identifiably partisan video ecosystems.

## A shared methodological commitment

What unites all three papers, despite their different objects (voter attitudes, editorial behavior, content ideology), is a commitment to using large-scale, automatically extracted digital trace data—ad delivery logs, hundreds of thousands of news articles, cross-platform sharing graphs—paired with either experimental randomization or quasi-experimental causal designs to make claims that observational, small-N media studies could not support. Collectively they illustrate both the promise and the limits of this computational turn: [[Allcott2025-jb]] shows that even massive, well-randomized designs can be underpowered for the outcomes that matter most (vote choice); [[Balluff2026-bv]] shows that computational content analysis combined with careful quasi-experimental design can surface covert institutional corruption that would otherwise be undetectable; and [[Lai2024-to]] shows how the same broad toolkit of latent-variable and network-based inference, developed originally for legislators and elites, can be redeployed to scale ideological measurement down to the level of individual media artifacts—a capability future work on advertising effects and media capture will likely need to draw on.
