# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The causal turn: what platform experiments can and cannot show

The empirical center of gravity in this literature is a series of large, often collaborative field experiments asking whether specific platform mechanics—feeds, ads, reshares, algorithmic ranking—causally move political attitudes and behavior. [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Guess2023-ai]], both drawn from the Facebook/Instagram 2020 Election Study, find that switching users to reverse-chronological feeds or stripping reshares from their timelines substantially changes what people see (less political news, more or less untrustworthy content) without producing detectable shifts in polarization or knowledge. [[Allcott2025-jb]], from the same collaboration, finds a parallel null for removing political ads outright, while noting that most 2020 ad spending targeted co-partisans rather than persuadable voters—an important scope condition for interpreting the nulls. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] complicates this picture: on X, switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed *does* shift attitudes rightward, and the effect persists even after the algorithm is switched back off, because exposure changes who users choose to follow. The paper explicitly reconciles this with the earlier Meta nulls by locating the causal mechanism at the level of *follow-graph formation* rather than *feed ranking per se*—a distinction [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] sharpens further by showing that apparent right-leaning amplification on post-acquisition Twitter is largely an artifact of who posts "agitating" content and who Elon Musk personally engages with, not partisan lean itself. [[Bail2018-fk]] anticipates this asymmetry-and-backfire logic from the opposite direction, showing that even benign cross-partisan exposure interventions can polarize rather than moderate, particularly among Republicans. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] and [[Brown2026-br]] extend the causal-audit toolkit to two other levers—elite visibility and recommendation traversal—finding that suppressing political elites (not just political content broadly) improves factual knowledge and reduces misinformation susceptibility, while YouTube's recommender produces "rabbit holes" but not demonstrable ideological radicalization once user choice is properly disentangled from algorithmic supply. [[Allen2025-ot]] steps back from these results to frame the broader methodological arc: as platform API access closes, "platform-independent" designs—browser extensions, custom clients—are becoming the field's necessary adaptation, a throughline connecting several of these later studies.

## Measuring ideology at scale: from legislators to embeddings to video

A parallel and older strand asks not whether platforms move ideology but how to *measure* it computationally in the first place. [[Barbera2015-je]] is the foundational move here, treating Twitter follow decisions as costly signals from which ideal points for elites and ordinary users alike can be jointly scaled, validated against roll-call and survey benchmarks, and used to document elite-level Twitter echo chambers. [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes this logic from following to *sharing*, using URLs as a platform-agnostic currency to jointly place media outlets, politicians, and the mass public on one scale, and uncovering a substantively important result—ideologically extreme legislators dominate the shared news ecosystem, especially those from electorally uncompetitive districts. [[Lai2024-to]] pushes latent ideology estimation into a new object entirely: individual YouTube videos, scaled via cross-subreddit sharing patterns rather than user or elite behavior, extending the "ideology as latent variable" tradition from actors to content itself. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] closes the loop by showing that ideology need not even be deliberately modeled to emerge: X's opaque 256-dimensional recommender embedding encodes a near-perfect linear direction for Left-Right position, discovered post hoc through data-donation reverse engineering, raising the same profiling concerns as Barberá's follow-graph method but now embedded involuntarily inside a commercial black box. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] offers a lighter-weight complement: rather than inferring ideology from behavioral graphs, it prompts instruction-tuned LLMs directly on text and averages responses, correlating well with the very benchmarks this cluster of papers spent a decade establishing—suggesting the field's ideology-scaling toolkit is now bifurcating into behavioral-graph methods and generative-model-based methods that may need reconciling.

## Covert influence: buying coverage, buying suppression, buying reach

A second core thread turns from *measuring* ideology to *detecting manipulation*—cases where advertising money or targeting logic covertly shapes political outcomes. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is the clearest media-capture case: using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text, it shows that government-advertising arrangements between Sebastian Kurz and OE24 coincided with a doubling of his visibility and a "pay positive to go negative" pattern in coverage of his rivals—demonstrating that computational text analysis can expose media capture even when the underlying bribery is secret. [[Kim2026-wg]] documents a structurally similar but starker phenomenon on the demand side of political advertising: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in the 2016 US election, where average-treatment-effect designs (of the kind that produced earlier null findings on Russian interference) obscure large, targeted turnout effects concentrated exactly among the populations the ads were aimed at. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] address the auditing infrastructure this kind of detection requires: volunteer browser-plugin data donation to surface irregular Brazilian electoral ads, and analysis of how delivery algorithms themselves—not just advertiser targeting choices—determine who actually sees an ad and at what price, adding an algorithmic layer of opacity on top of advertiser intent. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] provides the conceptual scaffolding tying this thread together, arguing that micro-targeted advertising, blurred organizational boundaries, and strategic amplification interact to make transactional and coverage-based manipulation especially hard to regulate or even observe—precisely the gap the other four papers in this section attempt to fill empirically.

## Misinformation, coordination, and downstream harms

Adjacent to advertising and capture is a body of work asking how much online content—true or false—actually diffuses and matters. [[Allen2024-av]] delivers this section's central methodological lesson: separating *exposure* from *persuasive impact*, it shows that flagged misinformation was more persuasive per view but reached almost nobody, while unflagged, mainstream-sourced "vaccine-skeptical" content had 46 times the aggregate effect—an argument that reframes fact-checking-centric policy as targeting the wrong quantity. [[Yang2023-cg]] makes an analogous methodological correction for images rather than text, finding that link-based misinformation metrics have drastically undercounted the problem because roughly a fifth of political images on Facebook are misleading, overwhelmingly on the political right. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes the causal chain furthest downstream, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to an epidemic model (SIRVA) to estimate actual vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths attributable to Twitter content. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] revisits the exposure-measurement problem at the level of platforms rather than content, arguing that the field's "direct referrer" convention undercounts each platform's true contribution to misinformation exposure by 1.5–15x once indirect and delayed pathways are modeled—complicating simple platform-ban proposals since removing a platform would also disproportionately cut high-quality news. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] closes this thread with a skeptical note: formally modeling influence in retweet cascades, it finds that real coordinated-inauthentic accounts in the 2019 UK election achieved far less influence than optimal or even random placement would predict, suggesting CIB may be less central to diffusion than assumed—an important counterweight to the exposure-maximalist findings elsewhere in the section.

## Visual, affective, and cross-platform signals beyond the link

A smaller cluster extends computational method beyond text-link analysis into images, sentiment, and cross-platform campaign style. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] both push visual political communication forward computationally—showing that multimodal LLMs can outperform specialized vision pipelines for candidate visibility on Instagram, and that framing itself is inherently multimodal and extractable across text/image/cross-modal channels. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Xue2025-bp]] turn to affect and engagement dynamics: negative sentiment drives shares and comments while positive content drives likes in a decade of Progress Party Facebook posts, and emotionality itself becomes a strategic lever even within fact-checking content nominally committed to neutrality. [[Darius2026-xl]] scales this affective-style argument to a full EU election, showing that populist radical-right (and, more surprisingly, far-left populist) parties receive disproportionate audience support—especially on TikTok—driven by anti-elite and emotional communicative style rather than posting frequency, echoing the algorithmic-amplification arguments from the causal-experiments section. [[Much2026-gu]] extends the "engagement is driven by identity/affect rather than argument" logic into a genuinely new domain: non-political entertainment podcasts as "gendered media spaces" where incidental exposure sorts audiences ideologically without any explicit political content, offering a plausible (if only partial) account of Trump's 2024 gains among young men.

## The generative-AI frontier: from ideological inference to synthetic persuasion

The newest and fastest-growing thread asks what happens when the object of study is not human-authored ads or posts but LLM-generated persuasion and inference. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text via subtle lexical and cultural cues, extending the ideology-inference project of Barberá-style scaling into arbitrary conversational text and raising acute micro-targeting privacy concerns. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish the persuasion-mechanics baseline for conversational AI: post-training and prompting strategies (especially information density) drive persuasiveness more than scale or personalization, and frontier systems now out-persuade even elite human debaters and professional canvassers—while trading accuracy for persuasive power. [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates and extends this in real electoral contexts across four countries, finding AI dialogue persuasion exceeding traditional ad effects and, notably, that right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less factually accurate than left-leaning advocacy. Three papers then interrogate the boundary conditions of this persuasive power: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows perceived partisan bias in the model attenuates persuasion by up to 28%; [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing *identity* (per the EU AI Act) does little, but disclosing *intent* halves persuasive effect—reframing AI transparency regulation around purpose rather than source; and [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows persuasion generalizes across issues and is strongest precisely among skeptics, complicating the motivated-reasoning account these interventions were expected to run into. [[Waight2026-ts]] supplies a structural, upstream counterpart to all of this: state media control leaks into LLM training data itself, so that models produce more pro-regime outputs in languages tied to low-media-freedom states—suggesting that ideological signaling in AI outputs can originate not only from persuasive fine-tuning but from captured training corpora, closing a loop back to this topic's media-capture concerns.

## Method, infrastructure, and reflexive critique

A final set of papers supplies methodological plumbing or steps back to question the field's framing. [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Paci2025-ag]] probe the limits of LLMs as annotators for political-text tasks—efficient fine-tuning dramatically improves conflict-event classification, but even strong models fail badly at recovering the implicit, manipulative pragmatics of political speech, a sobering counterpoint to the optimism of the ideology-scaling and persuasion literatures above. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] turn the computational-social-science lens on the discourse *about* AI itself, tracing how journalistic sourcing patterns and platform-specific stakeholder composition manufacture "AI hype" as an infrastructural force, while [[Vertesi2026-lv]] argues most such critiques—including, implicitly, much accountability-oriented work in this very corpus—function as "decoys" distracting from the underlying political economy of capital and infrastructure that AI's ideological and persuasive capacities ultimately serve. Read together with the empirical clusters above, these papers suggest the corpus's own trajectory: from auditing platforms and ads for covert influence, to formally scaling ideology in text, image, and embedding space, to confronting a generative-AI moment in which the tools of ideological signaling and the tools of ideological measurement are rapidly becoming the same technology.
