# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## Overview

This structure note traces a body of computational scholarship that treats political advertising, media capture, and algorithmic curation not as separate phenomena but as facets of a single question: how digital infrastructures—ad markets, feed algorithms, LLM chatbots, and covert financial arrangements—shape what political information people see and how persuadable they are by it. The papers filed here span causal field experiments, quasi-experimental audits, and observational/text-as-data designs, unified by a commitment to inferring effects at scale rather than relying on self-report or small lab samples.

## Causal effects of algorithmic feeds

A cluster of large field experiments interrogates whether ranking algorithms causally move political attitudes, extending and complicating the null results of the 2020 Meta collaboration. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that switching US users onto X's "For You" algorithm shifted attitudes rightward and that this effect was asymmetric and durable — because the algorithm changed which accounts people *followed*, not just what they were shown, a mechanism that helps reconcile the largely null findings of [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s Facebook/Instagram ad-removal experiment. [[Brady2026-ln]] extends this logic with a bespoke "diversified extremity" algorithm on Bluesky, showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social *norms* — reframing polarization as a social-learning problem rather than pure attitude change. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates simple "algorithm favors the right" narratives by showing that apparent conservative advantage in Musk-era Twitter is largely explained by agitation and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while [[Brown2026-br]] similarly decomposes YouTube "radicalization" claims into weak echo-chamber effects (user-driven), strong rabbit-hole effects (algorithm-driven), and no evidence of ideological escalation. Together these papers model a shift from asking "does the algorithm have an effect" to asking *through which mechanism* — following behavior, norm perception, or content sequencing — effects propagate.

Two papers turn from feed ranking to the recommender's internal representations. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] reconstructs X's embedding space and shows political ideology is linearly encoded even though the system was never designed to model it, raising GDPR-relevant profiling concerns and proposing a projection-based fix. [[McNally2025-dn]] takes a black-box auditing approach to Facebook's News Feed, using time-series causality methods to show hard news specifically is targeted by ranking updates — an argument for treating algorithms as detectable sociotechnical systems rather than unknowable black boxes, in dialogue with the DSA's audit provisions.

## Methodological reckoning: the limits of platform-dependent science

[[Munger2025-cz]] provides the meta-scientific spine for this section, arguing that the Meta2020 partnership — despite its rigor — reveals structural limits of platform research: findings decay too fast (poor "temporal validity"), core constructs like "echo chamber" are inconsistently operationalized ("poetic validity"), and consensus science trends toward null, industry-convenient results. This diagnosis motivates a wave of *platform-independent* experimental tooling. [[Allen2025-ot]] frames browser-extension-plus-LLM reranking as a productive middle path between lab control and platform cooperation, and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]]'s custom X client (Twilly) operationalizes this directly, showing that suppressing elite visibility reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing without requiring platform buy-in — reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture rather than individual disposition. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] extends the methodological critique to measurement itself, showing that the field's dominant "direct referrer" paradigm for attributing misinformation exposure to platforms undercounts effects by 1.5–15x once indirect and delayed pathways are modeled via counterfactual simulation — with the twist that platform removal would also disproportionately cut high-quality news, complicating simple ban-the-platform policy proposals.

## Covert influence, media capture, and coordinated behavior

Several papers investigate hidden or non-obvious channels of political influence over information supply. [[Balluff2026-bv]] documents the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre," using difference-in-differences on two decades of news text to show government advertising functioned as a covert bribery channel, producing a "pay positive to go negative" bias pattern against a chancellor's rivals — evidence that media capture mechanisms long studied in hybrid regimes also operate in consolidated democracies. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national visibility bias favoring the Radical Right across the 2024 European Parliament elections, independent of outlet ideology, complicating populist narratives of media marginalization. [[Kim2026-wg]] shows that 2016 IRA-linked voter-suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and associated with real turnout depression concentrated in targeted subgroups — arguing that average treatment effects obscure the real stakes of microtargeted campaigns, a critique of the ATE-focused designs used elsewhere in this literature (e.g., [[Allcott2025-jb]]). [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] extend the advertising-transparency thread to Brazilian Facebook ads and to ad-delivery-algorithm cost/reach dynamics respectively, both flagging opacity as the central obstacle to accountability.

Coordinated-behavior papers push back against inflated narratives of manipulation. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formally models influence-maximization in retweet cascades and finds real coordinated accounts in the 2019 UK election achieved influence close to random placement, far below strategic optimum — a skeptical counterpoint to alarmist CIB narratives. [[Kim2026-br]] complicates this by showing, in South Korean Naver comments, that suspected state-linked trolls succeed less through promoting foreign narratives than through "Condemning Korea" rhetoric that exploits organic moral-outrage engagement dynamics. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE framework reframes cross-platform influence as structural rather than agentic, identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" who structurally seed narrative migration between Truth Social and X. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] closes the causal loop from online speech to offline harm, using a compartmental epidemic model to estimate that antivaccine tweets caused measurable excess COVID cases and deaths — a rare demonstration linking platform content directly to physical-world outcomes.

## LLM persuasion, disclosure, and the accuracy trade-off

A dense cluster examines conversational AI as a new persuasion technology. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes what makes LLMs persuasive — post-training and prompting matter more than scale or personalization, with information density as the operative mechanism — while documenting a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing frontier AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traceable to fact throughput rather than rapport, and generalizing to real donation behavior. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates across four national elections that AI persuades primarily through evidence rather than manipulation, though right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate — echoing the asymmetry flagged in [[Hackenburg2025-dj]]. Two papers investigate boundary conditions: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows perceived partisan bias in the messenger cuts persuasion by ~25%, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing mere AI identity (as under the EU AI Act) barely dents persuasion, while disclosing *persuasive intent* halves it — jointly arguing that transparency regulation must target intent, not just machine-source labeling. [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Lee2026-je]] extend the LLM-as-political-actor frame in different directions: the former shows state media control is laundered into LLM training data and outputs across 37 countries, and the latter shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical text, raising privacy and microtargeting concerns that connect back to the ad-targeting literature above.

## Measuring ideology and framing at scale

A methods-oriented cluster develops tools for scaling ideology and framing across new content types. [[Eady2025-vm]] unifies ideology estimation for media, politicians, and the public through shared URL-sharing behavior, finding that electorally safe politicians share more polarized content — linking measurement innovation to a substantive claim about gerrymandering and polarization. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation to YouTube videos via Reddit cross-posting patterns, and [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes prompting-and-averaging LLMs as a general-purpose scaling method validated across languages and text types. [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Arminio2025-tw]] push beyond text into multi-modal and visual framing analysis, arguing that image and cross-modal cues carry editorial signal invisible to text-only pipelines. [[Balluff2026-ev]] offers a relational counterpoint, showing alternative and legacy German media reference largely the same entities on Nord Stream 2 but organize them into differently structured networks — suggesting ideological difference operates through relational structure rather than topical selection.

## Contextualizing forces: platforms, discourse, and gendered/geopolitical framing

Rounding out the topic are papers that situate these computational findings within broader discursive and institutional context. [[Munger2025-cz]], [[Dodds2026-df]], and Richter2026-df-adjacent [[Richter2026-bt]] each interrogate the meta-level forces (platform incentives, journalistic hype cycles, stakeholder composition) that shape which AI/political-media narratives circulate and who controls them — [[Richter2026-bt]] tracing a 2012–2021 divergence between German institutionalization and US industry/influencer dominance of AI discourse, with implications for later regulatory trajectories. [[Mota2026-sl59]] finds a parallel North-South asymmetry in sentiment toward AI itself. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] anticipates much of this agenda, laying out three foundational disruptions — blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeted advertising, and attention-based amplification — that the more recent computational papers in this collection empirically operationalize. Finally, [[Much2026-gu]] identifies "gendered media spaces" (entertainment podcasts) as a novel, non-ideological channel of political persuasion that helps explain the 2024 youth gender gap, illustrating how the ad/media-effects toolkit developed for explicitly political content is being extended to ostensibly apolitical media ecosystems.
