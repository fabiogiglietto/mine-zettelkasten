# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The causal turn: does the platform itself move attitudes?

A dominant thread running through this collection is the shift from correlational claims about social media and polarization toward genuinely causal, quasi-experimental designs. The Meta-collaborative studies anchor this arc: [[Guess2023-ur]] and [[Guess2023-ai]] independently manipulate feed ranking and resharing during the 2020 US election and find, despite large changes to on-platform content mix and engagement, essentially null effects on polarization, knowledge, and behavior. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this null-result pattern to political advertising itself, showing that removing ads from Facebook/Instagram feeds for six weeks changed nothing measurable, while also documenting that most ads were targeted at co-partisans rather than persuadable voters—undercutting the microtargeting-threat narrative that motivates much of this literature. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] rounds out the Meta trilogy by showing untrustworthy-source exposure is low on average, highly concentrated, and attitudinally inert.

This null-result consensus is precisely what [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Brady2026-ln]] complicate. Gauthier's X field experiment finds that switching users onto the algorithmic "For You" feed does shift attitudes rightward—but crucially, switching it back off does *not* reverse the effect, because the algorithm's real power lies in reshaping *who users choose to follow*, a persistence mechanism that helps reconcile the Meta nulls with X's positive findings. Brady's Bluesky registered report likewise finds that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social *norms* around political dialogue (rather than directly polarizing individual attitudes)—suggesting the mechanism by which algorithms matter may be indirect and normative rather than blunt persuasion. [[Allen2025-ot]]'s commentary on a sibling study explicitly theorizes these divergences, arguing that intervention level (post-content vs. feed-affordance), platform, and moderation regime explain why some designs find effects and others don't, and that "platform-independent" browser-extension methods (echoed methodologically in [[Jaidka2026-sl34]]'s elite-blocking experiment) are increasingly necessary as API access closes. Jaidka's study itself provides one of the cleanest positive causal results in the set: selectively hiding elite-authored and elite-referencing content reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing without moving affective polarization—reframing misinformation as a problem of *exposure architecture* centered on elites rather than individual susceptibility.

Older contact-based interventions sit in instructive tension with this newer work: [[Bail2018-fk]]'s bot-exposure experiment found that confronting partisans with opposing views backfires (especially for Republicans), while [[Barbera2015-fw]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]] established the earlier baseline that ideological segregation online is topic- and channel-dependent rather than uniform—social media and search both increase measured segregation *and* incidental cross-cutting exposure, and most consumption remains direct, mainstream-outlet browsing. Together these papers trace an arc from early filter-bubble skepticism to sophisticated designs isolating *which* algorithmic lever (ranking, resharing, following-graph shaping, elite visibility) actually carries causal weight, and under what moderation regime.

## Advertising, suppression, and the limits of average effects

A second cluster examines political advertising and targeted influence operations directly. [[Kim2026-wg]] shows that 2016 voter-suppression ads exhibited systematic geo-racial targeting and that average-treatment-effect designs mask dramatic heterogeneous turnout effects concentrated among the targeted minority populations—an important methodological corrective to studies (like [[Eady2023-xg]] on IRA exposure) that find null average effects of foreign influence content. Eady's careful linkage of survey panels to actual Twitter timelines shows IRA exposure was extraordinarily concentrated among already-committed partisans and dwarfed by domestic political content, reinforcing a broader finding across this topic: concentration among a tiny subset of highly engaged users, also documented for fake news exposure in [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and for antivax content in [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] and [[Allen2024-av]]. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] extend the advertising-transparency question to delivery algorithms and crowdsourced auditing respectively, both underscoring how opaque targeting and delivery mechanics—not just message content—shape political ad reach, a concern [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] had already flagged as a structural challenge for the field: attention-based amplification strategies evade the "money trail" that regulators use to police paid advertising.

## Media capture and covert influence on coverage

Where the advertising literature asks whether ads influence *voters*, a parallel strand asks whether advertising revenue influences *journalists*. [[Balluff2026-bv]] provides the paper's most direct causal evidence of media capture in a consolidated democracy: using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text, it shows that after the alleged 2016 onset of the Kurz–OE24 advertising-for-coverage arrangement, Kurz's visibility surged while his competitors' coverage turned more negative—a "pay positive to go negative" signature of covert capture that evades direct observation of the underlying transaction. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] demonstrates a structurally related but distinct phenomenon: Facebook's own viewership-amplification of shared content is not neutral but fluctuates with platform governance interventions (e.g., 2020 "break the glass" measures), rewarding journalistic quality and penalizing partisan audiences in ways that vary over time—evidence that platforms, not just advertisers, actively curate visibility. [[Iris2026-pg]] extends the visibility-bias question cross-nationally, finding the Radical Right receives disproportionate news-attention relative to electoral strength across several European countries, intensifying in the final pre-election weeks—a possible normalization mechanism operating independently of any single outlet's capture. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]] probe adjacent mechanisms of agenda distortion: bots functioning as algorithmically amplified agenda-builders against campaign messaging, and the quote-tweet structure of Twitter making visible how accurate evidence gets reframed into misleading election-fraud narratives through audience "call and response." [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" ties these visibility dynamics to intra-party dynamics, showing how Republican elites who adopt hyper-partisan-coded issues (CRT) are rewarded with outsized engagement and partisan-media attention, radicalizing the party from within.

## Estimating latent ideology at scale

A methodological throughline concerns how to measure ideology from behavioral traces without ground-truth labels. [[Barbera2015-je]] is foundational here, establishing that Twitter following networks encode valid, scalable ideal-point estimates for both elites and ordinary users validated against roll-call and survey benchmarks. [[Eady2025-vm]] updates this tradition by jointly scaling media outlets, politicians, and the public from URL-sharing behavior rather than following, finding that electorally safe politicians share more polarized news—linking measurement innovation directly to a representation-and-polarization argument. [[Lai2024-to]] extends the ideal-point paradigm to a new unit of analysis, using Reddit cross-posting to estimate latent ideology for individual YouTube videos, while [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows that a commercial recommender system (X's "Who to Follow") has *itself* inadvertently learned a near-linear ideology representation in its embedding space—turning ideology estimation from a research tool into an object of algorithmic audit and privacy concern. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] push estimation into the LLM era: prompting-and-averaging with instruction-tuned models recovers reliable left-right placements across languages and text types, while multi-modal framing analysis extends automated measurement beyond text to the visual cues that shape editorial choices—both illustrating how generative models are becoming the new workhorses of ideology and framing measurement, alongside domain-specific fine-tuning approaches like [[Meher2025-qb]] for conflict-event classification. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] explicitly critiques this measurement tradition for oversimplifying ideology as unidimensional and outlet-level, arguing for multi-level "political cartography," a critique that [[Green2025-ap]]'s "curation bubbles" paper substantiates empirically: domain-level partisan audience scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, especially for moderate outlets, meaning standard measures of echo chambers may misdiagnose where real ideological sorting occurs.

## Misinformation: exposure, concentration, and consequence

Building on the concentration findings above, several papers push toward disentangling *exposure* from *persuasive effect*—the two conditions jointly necessary for misinformation to matter. [[Allen2024-av]] is exemplary: it shows fact-checked misinformation is more persuasive per view but reaches so few people that unflagged, mainstream "vaccine-skeptical" content has 46 times greater aggregate impact on hesitancy, a finding with direct implications for veracity-focused moderation policy. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] complements this with an epidemiological causal model linking geolocated antivax tweets to actual vaccination, case, and death counts. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] documents the skewed, winner-take-most diffusion structure of misinformation reshares on Facebook, while [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] challenges the field's dominant "direct referrer" measurement convention, showing that counting only immediate platform-to-site transitions underestimates platforms' true influence on misinformation exposure by 1.5–15x, with sharply partisan-asymmetric effects across Twitter, YouTube, and Reddit. [[Tai2026-qk]] shifts the lens to elite behavior, examining how institutional and ideological position condition elected officials' own propensity to share misinformation—connecting back to the elite-centered exposure architecture that Jaidka's experiment manipulates directly.

## The generative-AI turn: persuasion, bias, and inference

The newest and fastest-growing cluster asks what happens when LLMs themselves become political communicators, auditors, or objects of political bias. On persuasion, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes the "levers" of conversational AI persuasion, finding information density—not personalization or scale—drives effects, alongside a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off; [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing frontier AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers via the same fact-density mechanism, with effects generalizing to real donations. [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates large AI persuasion effects across four elections while documenting a politically asymmetric accuracy gap (right-leaning AI advocacy less accurate), and two papers probe the boundary conditions of this persuasive power: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows perceived out-party bias in the model sharply attenuates persuasion, while [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing an AI's *persuasive intent* (not merely its AI identity, as EU AI Act Article 50 requires) cuts persuasion roughly in half—jointly suggesting that regulatory transparency needs to target purpose, not just provenance. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy, while not AI-specific, supplies an important empirical backdrop here: even the most-cited persuasion strategies move attitudes only marginally and rarely touch costly behavior, a sobering baseline against which AI's persuasive gains look especially large.

A second AI strand concerns inference and bias rather than persuasion. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical text with high accuracy, raising privacy and microtargeting concerns; [[Waight2026-ts]] documents that state media control is already laundered into LLM outputs via training data, producing systematically more pro-regime responses in languages tied to low-media-freedom states; and [[Paci2025-ag]] offers a countervailing finding—current LLMs are actually poor at interpreting implicit, manipulative political discourse (implicatures, presuppositions), suggesting real limits to their use as either propagandists or fact-checkers of subtle political rhetoric. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Ng2026-og]] step back to theorize these developments: Dodds frames "AI hype" itself as a structuring force in journalism's coverage of AI, while Ng argues that multi-agent AI systems need social-theoretic structural priors, illustrated with evidence of strategic heterogeneity and distributional instability in an AI social network—gesturing toward a near future in which the objects of this literature's analysis (bots, LLM agents) are themselves the producers of political content and influence operations.

## Platform-specific amplification and the populist/radical-right advantage

A final cluster documents how platform affordances differentially favor radical and populist actors. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Brown2026-br]] use rigorous audit designs to separate algorithmic from user-driven effects on Twitter/X and YouTube respectively, finding that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage is largely explained by agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk (not ideology per se) on X, while YouTube shows strong content "rabbit holes" and a mild platform-wide conservative tilt but no radicalization pathway. [[Darius2026-xl]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] document the cross-platform payoff of populist communicative style directly: populist radical-right (and far-left) parties receive disproportionate audience support, especially on TikTok, driven by anti-elite and emotional framing rather than mere posting volume, while the Sweden Democrats tailor memetic, affectively charged securitization content specifically to TikTok's younger audience. [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Lukito2026-il]] add longitudinal and cross-platform texture—negative sentiment reliably drives shares/comments for Norway's Progress Party, and right-leaning US outlets curate distinct story packages for Twitter versus Truth Social—while [[Bailard2024-pj]] and [[Much2026-gu]] push beyond rhetoric to mobilization and persuasion effects in gendered and extremist media spaces, with Bailard linking Proud Boys' online framing cycles to offline violence and Much showing how "gendered media spaces" like entertainment podcasts incidentally sorted young male listeners toward Trump in 2024. Together, this cluster suggests that whatever null results emerge from controlled feed-ranking experiments on mainstream platforms, platform-specific affordances and algorithmic attention economies continue to structurally advantage extreme, emotionally charged, and anti-elite political communication.
