# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## Line of Argument: Measuring, Detecting, and Causally Testing Media Influence

The papers filed under this topic collectively trace an arc from *measurement* (how do we scale ideology and detect covert influence at scale?) through *causal identification* (do platform features and advertising actually move attitudes and behavior?) to *normative reckoning* (what do these findings mean for governance, given the limits of the research paradigm itself). Rather than treating "computational political communication" as one method, the corpus shows a field triangulating the same substantive questions—does exposure to political content on digital platforms change minds, behavior, or the information environment itself—through survey experiments, network-based inference, LLM-based text/video scaling, and quasi-experimental designs exploiting scandals or natural policy changes.

### Scaling ideology from behavior, not self-report

A foundational strand extends ideal-point estimation from legislators to arbitrary online actors. [[Barbera2015-je]] establishes the core method—Bayesian Spatial Following models that treat Twitter follows as costly ideological signals—validated against NOMINATE and expert surveys, while [[Barbera2015-fw]] uses this scaling to show that "echo chamber" dynamics are topic- and time-contingent rather than a fixed property of social media, with a notable liberal-conservative asymmetry in cross-cutting engagement. [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes the approach to shared URLs, jointly placing media outlets, politicians, and mass publics on one behavioral scale, and uses this to show that electorally safe politicians share more polarized content—linking ideology measurement to representation and gerrymandering debates. [[Lai2024-to]] pushes latent-ideology estimation into a new content type, scaling YouTube videos via Reddit sharing patterns, while [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes an entirely different measurement technology—prompting LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses—showing convergence with traditional benchmarks across languages. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] closes the loop between measurement and platform behavior, reverse-engineering X's recommender embeddings to show that ideological position is inadvertently encoded and used in "Who to Follow" recommendations, raising the stakes of these measurement exercises: what starts as an academic scaling tool exists internally inside the platform.

### Auditing algorithmic curation: echo chambers, amplification, and asymmetry

A second cluster interrogates whether platform algorithms—as opposed to user choice—drive polarization and skewed exposure. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] finds that ideological segregation in Facebook news exposure is far higher than prior web-tracking studies suggested and asymmetric, with a homogeneously conservative corner of the ecosystem harboring most fact-checked misinformation. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube using a novel audit design, finding mild echo chambers (mostly user-driven), strong "rabbit hole" dynamics, no radicalization pathway, but a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] similarly decomposes apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification on post-Musk Twitter into behavioral confounds (agitation, proximity to Musk, verification status) rather than ideology per se, while [[Gauthier2026-iq]] provides causal field-experimental evidence that X's algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward and asymmetrically "locks in" this shift via induced following behavior—reconciling earlier null results from Meta's 2020 studies. [[Bail2018-fk]] offers an early, cautionary counterpoint: engineered cross-partisan exposure backfires, especially for Republicans, undermining naive contact-theory hopes for algorithmic remedies. [[Brady2026-ln]] responds directly to this literature by redesigning the algorithm itself, showing engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms (not directly attitudes), while a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing platform enjoyment—reframing intervention design around social-norm misperception rather than persuasion per se.

### The Meta2020 collaboration and its methodological aftermath

A tightly linked set of papers stems from or reflects on the Facebook/Instagram Election Study. [[Guess2023-ai]] and [[Guess2023-ur]] show that removing reshares or switching to chronological feeds substantially reshapes on-platform content exposure yet produces no detectable shifts in polarization, knowledge, or offline behavior. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this null-effects pattern to political advertising itself, finding six weeks of ad removal changed nothing detectable (with the important caveat of low power for turnout/vote choice). [[Munger2025-cz]] steps back to offer a meta-scientific critique of this entire research program, arguing that its narrow, underpowered, conceptually inconsistent estimates reveal the structural inadequacy of reactive, platform-dependent social science, and calls for proactive regulatory disclosure requirements instead. [[Allen2025-ot]] engages this same crisis of platform access from the opposite direction, championing "platform-independent" browser-extension experiments (building on Piccardi et al.) as a viable alternative when API access closes—a methodological pivot borne out empirically by [[Jaidka2026-sl34]], whose custom X client demonstrates that blocking political elites (rather than reshares or algorithm type) causally reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing, isolating an "elite exposure" channel invisible to the Meta-style interventions.

### Detecting covert influence: media capture, coordinated behavior, and advertising irregularities

Where the previous clusters ask whether documented exposure changes attitudes, another group asks whether *hidden* influence operations can be detected computationally in the first place. [[Balluff2026-bv]] uses difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text to uncover a "pay positive to go negative" signature of media capture in the Kurz/OE24 advertising scandal, demonstrating that covert bribery-for-coverage arrangements leave a detectable statistical fingerprint even without access to the underlying transactions. [[Balluff2026-ev]] extends the entity-network toolkit to ask whether alternative media diverge from legacy outlets substantively (largely not) or structurally (yes, via network cohesion and self-referencing patterns). [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national visibility bias favoring Radical Right actors in European election coverage using LLM-assisted entity extraction—a bias that exists independent of any single outlet's intent, echoing the "who gets covered" question from a comparative angle. On the advertising side proper, [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] examine the technical apparatus of digital political ads—crowdsourced auditing of irregular Facebook ads in Brazil, and the role of platform delivery algorithms in shaping reach/cost—both gesturing toward how algorithmic and platform opacity complicates transparency efforts flagged conceptually by [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], whose essay frames micro-targeting, blurred organizational boundaries, and attention-amplification strategies as three intertwined challenges besetting both political actors and the scholars who study them. [[Kim2026-wg]] provides a causal-inference bridge between these covert-influence and effects literatures, showing that 2016 IRA-linked voter suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and associated with real turnout declines concentrated exactly among targeted groups—demonstrating why average treatment effects can mask consequential microtargeted harms. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] offers a skeptical counterweight from network theory, finding that real coordinated inauthentic accounts in UK election cascades achieve influence far below optimal or even randomly-expected benchmarks, suggesting CIB may be less pivotal than assumed. [[Kim2026-br]] extends covert-influence detection to two decades of Korean troll comments, finding that moral condemnation—not overt foreign praise—is trolls' most effective and most-rewarded rhetorical strategy.

### Misinformation exposure, epidemiological consequence, and multi-platform attribution

A related empirical strand asks not just whether misinformation exists but whether it is *consequential* at scale. [[Allen2024-av]] delivers a striking reframing: fact-check-flagged misinformation is more persuasive per view but reaches so few people that unflagged, "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content has a 46-fold larger aggregate effect on hesitancy—a finding with direct implications for veracity-focused moderation policy. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] complements this with a mechanistic epidemic model linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to county-level vaccination declines and downstream case/death counts, providing a rare causal chain from platform speech to public-health outcome. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] challenges the field's dominant "direct referrer" measurement convention, showing via counterfactual simulation that platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is 1.5–15x larger than direct-referral studies suggest, while also cautioning that platform removal would disproportionately cut high-quality news—complicating ban-oriented policy proposals.

### Video, image, and multimodal content as objects of ideological/framing analysis

Several papers push computational methods beyond text into visual and video domains. [[Arminio2025-tw]] argues CNN-based image clustering misses connotative, culturally embedded meaning and shows VLLM-generated textual descriptions substantially improve semantic (and interpretable) clustering of climate-related Instagram images. [[Arora2025-tx]] extends framing analysis to multimodal news content, showing that visual and cross-modal cues carry editorial signal invisible to text-only frame detection. [[Much2026-gu]] identifies podcasts as a novel "gendered media space" in which audiences sort on masculinity/femininity rather than ideology—an incidental-exposure mechanism plausibly contributing to (but not fully explaining) Trump's 2024 gains among young men, extending the topic's concern with covert or indirect political influence into non-explicitly-political entertainment media.

### LLMs as both instruments and objects of political-communication research

The final and most rapidly growing cluster treats large language models simultaneously as *measurement tools* and as *political actors in their own right*. Beyond [[Le-Mens2025-qz]]'s scaling application and [[Meher2025-qb]]'s domain-adapted event classifier (ConflLlama), [[Paci2025-ag]] stress-tests LLMs' pragmatic competence on implicit political discourse, finding persistent gaps versus expert human interpretation. A tightly argued sequence on AI persuasion—[[Hackenburg2025-dj]], [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]], and [[Kotz2026-lk]]—establishes that conversational AI is powerfully persuasive (out-persuading even elite human debaters and professional canvassers), that this persuasiveness is driven primarily by information/fact density rather than personalization or scale, that it trades off against factual accuracy, that perceived political bias of the model attenuates its effectiveness, and that it generalizes across contested domains especially among initial skeptics. Bookending this cluster, [[Waight2026-ts]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] widen the lens to political economy: state media control measurably "launders" into LLM training data and outputs, while stakeholder composition shaping public AI discourse itself diverges sharply by country—together suggesting that the very models now shown to be potent persuaders are themselves objects of the media-capture and elite-influence dynamics this topic otherwise studies in legacy and social media.
