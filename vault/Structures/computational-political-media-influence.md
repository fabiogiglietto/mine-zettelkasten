# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The arc of inquiry

This body of work treats political advertising, media capture, and ideological signaling as problems that can be measured at scale, causally identified, and — increasingly — audited by outsiders rather than taken on faith from platform disclosures. The line of argument runs from foundational diagnoses of why digital political communication resists traditional measurement ([[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]), through direct causal tests of advertising and algorithmic exposure, to a newer wave of work asking whether covert influence has simply migrated — from paid placements to captured newsrooms, to opaque recommender embeddings, and now to the training data and conversational style of large language models.

## What political ads actually do

The most direct empirical thread asks whether political advertising and its algorithmic delivery causally move outcomes that matter — turnout, vaccination, attitudes. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s large randomized ad-removal experiment on Facebook and Instagram before the 2020 US election found essentially null effects on knowledge, polarization, and perceptions, with most presidential ads targeted at co-partisans rather than persuadable voters — a finding that sits in tension with [[Kim2026-wg]]'s reconstruction of 2016 voter-suppression advertising, which shows that average-treatment-effect designs like Allcott's can mask sharply heterogeneous harms concentrated on geo-racially targeted subpopulations. [[Votta2025-xz]] complicates the picture further by showing that delivery algorithms, not just advertiser targeting, shape who sees ads and at what price, echoing [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]]'s early warning that transparency tools like the Ad Library cannot see inside the black box of algorithmic delivery. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] extends the auditing imperative geographically, showing that volunteer browser-plugin crowdsourcing can surface irregular electoral advertising in Brazil where platform disclosure is absent. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes causal ad-effects research beyond attitudes into epidemiological outcomes, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to measurable increases in COVID-19 cases and deaths through a compartmental model — a reminder that "advertising" effects research and organic-content effects research increasingly share method and stakes.

## Covert influence and the capture of coverage

A second thread investigates advertiser and state influence that operates *through* editorial content rather than through disclosed paid messages. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is the paradigm case: using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text, it detects a "pay positive to go negative" signature of media capture — a chancellor's visibility rises sharply after alleged advertising-for-coverage deals, even though his favorability does not, while his rivals are hit with more negative coverage. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complements this by showing that alternative media's distinctiveness lies not in *which* actors they cover but in *how* they relationally organize them — a subtler signature of ideological differentiation than raw entity selection. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a parallel but more diffuse distortion: disproportionate, intensifying media visibility for the Radical Right across five European countries regardless of outlet orientation, suggesting structural rather than partisan drivers of coverage bias. [[Kim2026-br]] and [[noauthor_undated-bm]] extend the covert-influence frame to coordinated inauthentic actors: the former finds that Korean state-linked trolls succeed less through pro-foreign messaging than through domestically-targeted moral condemnation that organically wins engagement, while the latter's large Meta-collaborative audit shows that "deceptive networks" reach tens of millions largely through unaffiliated resharers, and that their measurable effects on beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure confounders are controlled. [[Waight2026-ts]] pushes covert influence into a new substrate, showing that state-coordinated media survives into LLM training corpora and measurably shifts model outputs — effectively a form of media capture laundered through the AI supply chain rather than the ad market.

## Algorithmic curation as the new advertising surface

Where explicit advertising leaves a paper trail, algorithmic curation does not — and a cluster of papers treats the feed itself as the object of causal and forensic inquiry. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] provides the field's sharpest causal evidence that switching X's algorithmic feed on (but not off) shifts political attitudes rightward, and traces the asymmetry to persistent changes in *who users follow* — a mechanism that reframes recommender effects as compounding rather than reversible. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] dissect the machinery behind such effects: the former shows apparent right-leaning amplification on early Musk-era Twitter is largely explained by agitation and proximity to Musk rather than ideology per se, while the latter demonstrates that X's recommender embedding inadvertently but strongly encodes users' Left-Right position, raising direct tension with data-protection law. [[McNally2025-dn]] extends the auditing logic to Facebook's News Feed, showing that lagged engagement anomalies can reveal "black box" ranking interventions after the fact, supporting calls for DSA-style systemic audits. [[Brown2026-br]] and [[Brady2026-ln]] use randomized, platform-adjacent designs (YouTube traversal audits; a custom Bluesky feed-ranking experiment) to separate algorithmic supply from user demand, respectively finding a persistent conservative algorithmic tilt on YouTube net of user choice, and showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized/toxic content and social-norm misperception in ways a "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] and [[Allen2025-ot]] represent the maturing methodology of platform-independent experimentation — client-side interventions (blocking elite visibility; LLM-based content reranking) that let researchers estimate causal exposure effects without platform cooperation, a response directly motivated by [[Munger2025-cz]]'s meta-scientific indictment of the Meta2020 partnership as underpowered, conceptually incoherent, and structurally unable to keep pace with platform change. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] shows why this matters: direct-referrer measures of platform-to-misinformation pathways understate true exposure by 1.5–15x once indirect and counterfactual pathways are modeled, meaning most of the causal literature reviewed here likely underestimates platform effects. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] round out this thread with network-structural approaches to coordination and diffusion — the former finding real coordinated accounts far less influential than optimally-placed ones, the latter finding that a tiny set of narrative "bridge users" seed most cross-platform migration — while [[Smith2025-kc]]'s "attention broker" mechanism offers a causal, triadic account of how amplification by high-degree accounts (echoing Musk's outsized centrality in [[Efstratiou2025-gs]]) reshapes following networks more generally.

## Measuring ideology in media content

A parallel methodological thread asks how to *scale* ideology from behavioral or textual traces once causal designs are in place. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation to video content, inferring YouTube ideology from cross-subreddit sharing patterns, while [[Eady2025-vm]] unifies the measurement of media, elite, and mass-public ideology on one behavioral scale via shared URLs, finding that competitive-district politicians share less polarized news than their safe-seat counterparts — a structural link between electoral competition and the polarization of the shared information environment. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Lee2026-je]] push ideology scaling into the LLM era: the former shows that simply asking and averaging LLM judgments recovers reliable ideological placements across languages and text types, while the latter demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized cues — a capability with direct implications for the microtargeting concerns raised throughout this literature. [[Arminio2025-tw]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] extend measurement beyond text to visual and multimodal framing, arguing that connotative meaning and cross-modal cues (not just object recognition or lexical frames) carry the ideological signal that editorial choices encode. [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates the underlying infrastructure that makes much of this scaling feasible — efficient domain-adapted fine-tuning of open LLMs — using conflict-event classification as a proof of concept transferable to political-text tasks. [[Much2026-gu]] applies this measurement toolkit substantively, showing that podcast audiences sort on gendered rather than ideological signals, and that Trump's 2024 campaign exploited this "gendered media space" to reach persuadable young men who conventional ideological targeting would miss.

## Conversational AI as the next advertising channel

The final arc treats LLM-driven persuasion itself as an emerging advertising modality, subject to the same causal and disclosure scrutiny as legacy political ads. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish that conversational AI's persuasive power rests on information density rather than personalization or scale, and that it can outperform even expert human persuaders — while identifying a persuasion-accuracy trade-off directly relevant to ad-quality regulation. [[Lin2025-xp]] shows these effects generalize across national elections and exceed traditional ad benchmarks, with a partisan asymmetry in factual accuracy that mirrors known asymmetries in the broader information environment. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then interrogate the disclosure regimes meant to govern this new channel: perceived out-party bias suppresses persuasion by roughly a quarter, while disclosing *identity* alone (as under the EU AI Act) does little, and only disclosing *persuasive intent* — the logic underlying EU political-advertising law — meaningfully blunts influence. Together they suggest that the regulatory lessons learned from covert media capture and undisclosed micro-targeting apply directly to conversational AI.

## Infrastructure and discourse around the field itself

A smaller set of papers turns the computational lens on the media and discourse ecosystem that surrounds AI and political communication research itself, functioning as connective tissue rather than direct empirical tests of advertising effects. [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] examine how AI hype is manufactured and by whom — the former arguing hype is a structuring journalistic infrastructure, the latter tracing a US drift toward industry/influencer-dominated AI discourse against Germany's institutionalization. [[Mota2026-sl59]] shows this discourse itself splits geopolitically, with Global North coverage skewing risk-oriented and Global South coverage skewing developmental. [[UnknownUnknown-db]], while focused on labor-market perceptions of AI rather than political media per se, nonetheless demonstrates the same LLM-based inference-from-open-text methodology that underlies much of the ideology-scaling and covert-influence detection work above, illustrating how the field's measurement toolkit travels well beyond its original political-communication home.
