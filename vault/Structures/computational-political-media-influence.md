# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The Computational Turn in Studying Political Media and Advertising

The papers filed under this topic share a methodological commitment: rather than theorize about media power or persuasion in the abstract, they instrumentalize large-scale behavioral, textual, and visual data—ad libraries, browser plugins, panel surveys linked to platform logs, LLM-based classifiers—to detect and quantify effects that are otherwise invisible to observers, including regulators and even the platforms themselves. The resulting literature traces an arc from early concern about discrete "bad content" (fake news, foreign influence, misinformation) toward a more structural diagnosis of how platforms, advertisers, and political actors covertly shape visibility and belief at scale.

### Null Results and the Limits of Platform-Level Effects

A striking throughline is the accumulation of null or minimal findings from the most methodologically rigorous studies. [[Allcott2017-yz]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]] established early that fake news exposure in 2016 was real but concentrated and modest relative to its presumed electoral impact. [[Eady2023-xg]] extended this logic to the Russian IRA campaign, showing exposure concentrated among already-partisan users and bearing no detectable relationship to attitude change. The Meta-collaborative studies—[[Guess2023-ai]] on reshares, [[Guess2023-ur]] on algorithmic feed ranking, and [[Allcott2025-jb]] on political ad removal—replicate this pattern with experimental rather than observational designs: large shifts in content exposure, engagement, and even news knowledge, but no detectable movement in polarization or downstream political attitudes. [[Allen2025-ot]] situates this null-result cluster explicitly, using it as a foil to argue for new "platform-independent" experimental methods, a concern echoed institutionally by [[Lukito2026-nb]], which catalogs the shrinking and uneven landscape of researcher data access post-API.

Yet this null-results consensus is increasingly contested. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] reports a 2023 X field experiment finding that algorithmic feeds do shift attitudes rightward—reconciling the discrepancy with Meta's findings by showing that algorithmic effects operate asymmetrically and persist through induced *following* behavior rather than transient content exposure. [[Brady2026-ln]]'s Bluesky field experiment similarly finds that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms, though not directly attitudes—suggesting the pathway to polarization is normative misperception rather than belief change per se. Together these newer studies suggest that null effects from earlier work may reflect platform- or mechanism-specific idiosyncrasies (Meta's stricter 2020 moderation regime, short observation windows) rather than a general irrelevance of algorithmic curation.

### Re-measuring Exposure and Impact

A parallel strand refines *how* exposure and impact should be measured, arguing prior null results stem from mismeasurement rather than absence of effect. [[Allen2024-av]] decomposes "impact" into exposure × persuasiveness, finding that unflagged, mainstream vaccine-skeptical content vastly outweighs flagged misinformation in aggregate harm—a finding that reframes moderation policy away from veracity-focused interventions. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] makes an analogous methodological move for misinformation referrals, showing that counting only direct platform-to-site visits radically underestimates platforms' causal role once indirect and persistent pathways are modeled. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] pushes exposure-to-outcome causal modeling further still, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to epidemic dynamics via a compartmental SIRVA model to estimate downstream cases and deaths. [[Kim2026-wg]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] apply similar exposure-based logic to voter suppression advertising, with the former demonstrating that average treatment effects obscure severe heterogeneous harms concentrated among geo-racially targeted subpopulations—a methodological lesson that average-effect designs (including some of the Meta megastudies above) may be the wrong lens for detecting targeted harms.

### Covert Influence: Media Capture, Ad Delivery, and Algorithmic Profiling

A second cluster investigates *covert* channels of influence that evade transparency regimes entirely. [[Balluff2026-bv]] uses difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text to detect the behavioral signature of a government-advertising-for-coverage bribery scheme, demonstrating that computational textual analysis can reveal media capture even without access to the underlying illicit transactions. [[Votta2025-xz]] examines how ad delivery algorithms on Meta and Google shape political ad reach and cost in ways opaque to advertisers and regulators. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] uncovers a more diffuse mechanism: X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' ideological position, raising distinct legal and privacy questions about passive algorithmic profiling. [[Waight2026-ts]] extends covert influence to the training-data level, showing that state-coordinated media is memorized by LLMs and causally shapes their political valence—effectively "laundering" propaganda into ostensibly neutral AI outputs. Each paper demonstrates a different point in the pipeline (editorial, algorithmic, representational, infrastructural) where political influence can be embedded without explicit disclosure.

### Visibility, Framing, and the Radical Right

Several papers turn to media *visibility* and framing as objects of large-scale computational measurement, often converging on concerns about disproportionate amplification of radical and populist actors. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents cross-national overrepresentation of Radical Right parties in European election news relative to their polling and electoral strength. [[Darius2026-xl]] finds a parallel pattern in social media *audience support* (not just coverage) for populist radical right and far-left parties, with TikTok as the most asymmetric platform. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]]'s sock-puppet audit corroborates this at the algorithmic-exposure level, finding non-partisan German TikTok users disproportionately shown pro-AfD content, attributable to supply-side volume and virality rather than overt platform bias. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] offers a mechanism for how such asymmetric attention consolidates politically: a "logic of connective faction" in which ideologically extreme elites gain disproportionate engagement and media amplification by forging ties to hyper-partisan digital ecosystems, illustrated through the sudden elevation of Critical Race Theory as a Republican talking point. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] extends this line into the generative-AI era, examining far-right use of AI-generated multimedia in 2024 EU campaigning, while [[Arora2025-tx]] and [[Hu2026-sl26]] develop the underlying multimodal methods (text/visual/audio frame extraction) needed to study such content at scale, and [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs against specialized computer vision for analyzing candidate visibility strategies on Instagram.

### Ideological Scaling and Measurement Infrastructure

Underlying much of this work is a measurement tradition dedicated to recovering latent ideology from behavioral traces. [[Barbera2015-je]] established the foundational approach—Bayesian ideal-point estimation from Twitter following networks—later extended by [[Eady2025-vm]] to a joint measurement model spanning media outlets, politicians, and the public via news-sharing behavior, and by [[Lai2024-to]] to individual YouTube videos via cross-platform Reddit sharing patterns. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] offers an LLM-native alternative, simply prompting instruction-tuned models to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses, achieving strong correlations with these established benchmarks. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates a related but more unsettling capability: LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized lexical cues—a capability that converts the ideology-scaling literature into a privacy and microtargeting concern. [[Much2026-gu]] extends ideological measurement into a novel domain, showing how "gendered media spaces" (entertainment podcasts) function as an incidental ideological-sorting mechanism that the 2024 Trump campaign deliberately exploited.

### AI Persuasion and the New Frontier of Influence

A final, rapidly growing cluster asks whether conversational AI constitutes a qualitatively new persuasive force in political communication. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes the "levers" of LLM persuasion, identifying information density as the central mechanism and revealing a persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traceable to information throughput rather than rapport. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates these mechanisms across four real elections, while also documenting a partisan asymmetry in factual accuracy (right-leaning AI advocacy is less accurate). Two papers complicate the picture: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that perceived political bias in the messenger sharply attenuates persuasion, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing persuasive *intent* (not mere AI identity) cuts persuasive power roughly in half—together implying that meaningful transparency regulation must target intent disclosure, as in the EU's political-advertising rules, rather than mere AI-identity labeling under the AI Act. [[Voelkel2026-lc]]'s climate-messaging megastudy serves as a useful human-benchmark control, finding that even well-cited persuasive strategies produce only small attitudinal effects and no behavioral change—underscoring by contrast how large the newly measured AI effects are.

### Synthesis and Governance Implications

Read together, these papers chart a shift from asking "does social media cause political harm?" toward more precise questions: *which* mechanism (reshares, algorithmic ranking, following-induced exposure, ad delivery, training-data contamination, conversational persuasion) produces *which* effect (attitude change, norm misperception, vote suppression, ideological profiling, media capture) under *which* measurement regime (direct vs. embedded exposure, average vs. heterogeneous effects). Review and governance-oriented pieces—[[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] on the blurring of political-organizational boundaries and micro-targeting opacity, [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] on the rise and rollback of platform election policies, [[Lukito2026-nb]] on data-access inequities, and [[Gaber2022-bk]] on "strategic lying" as an evolution of spin—frame the empirical findings as evidence for a governance problem: platforms, advertisers, and now AI systems operate covert or semi-covert influence channels that existing transparency and regulatory frameworks only partially capture. The trajectory of the field, as [[Dodds2026-df]] cautions reflexively, must also resist its own susceptibility to AI hype, ensuring that claims about computational detection of influence remain anchored in the kind of causal rigor modeled by the strongest papers in this collection.
