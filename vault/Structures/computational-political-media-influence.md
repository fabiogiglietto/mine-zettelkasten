# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The algorithmic mediation of political attitudes

The empirical core of this topic is a set of field experiments and natural-experiment audits asking whether feed-ranking algorithms causally move political attitudes, and if so, how. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set the terms of the debate a decade ago by showing that on Facebook, individual click behavior suppresses cross-cutting news exposure more than the ranking algorithm does — a finding that has anchored a "user choice, not algorithm" reading of polarization ever since. Recent work complicates that reading considerably. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that switching US users onto X's "For You" algorithm shifts attitudes rightward on several salient issues, and — crucially — that the effect is asymmetric: switching the algorithm *off* does not reverse it, because the algorithm has already changed who users follow. This persistence mechanism helps reconcile the earlier Meta 2020 null results with newer positive findings, and it dovetails with [[Efstratiou2025-gs]], which shows that apparent right-leaning amplification on post-acquisition Twitter is largely explained by rewarding agitating content and proximity to Elon Musk rather than partisanship per se — the algorithm creates perverse incentives for controversy and platform-owner attention rather than enacting an ideological rule directly. [[Brown2026-br]] pushes the same disentangling logic onto YouTube, using a randomized-seed audit design to separate supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user selection; it finds only mild, choice-driven echo chambers, real "rabbit hole" path-dependence, no radicalization pathway, but a platform-wide conservative tilt affecting all users regardless of preference. Together these three papers converge on a methodological lesson: naive comparisons of algorithmic vs. chronological feeds, or watch-history audits, conflate curation with selection, and only designs that hold user identity fixed while randomizing exposure (or vice versa) can isolate the algorithm's independent contribution.

That lesson extends to platform-governance questions. [[McNally2025-dn]] shows Facebook's News Feed is not the inscrutable "black box" of popular discourse but a system whose interventions leave detectable time-series fingerprints — hard news, but not soft content, responds to ranking changes with a consistent ~three-week lag — arguing this supports systemic auditing under the EU's Digital Studies Act. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] extends this claim at far larger scale, showing that the translation of shares into views on Facebook is neither linear nor stable: partisan content is systematically penalized in reach and journalistic-quality content rewarded, with both effects fluctuating sharply around known "break the glass" interventions in 2020 — direct evidence that the platform actively curates rather than passively transmits. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] pursues a related transparency agenda from the representation side, reverse-engineering X's "Who to Follow" embedding space to show that a linear direction in a 256-dimensional vector correlates almost perfectly with users' Left-Right position, learned inadvertently rather than by design — raising GDPR-relevant questions about the line between active and passive profiling, and offering an orthogonal-projection fix that removes ideological signal while preserving relevance. Where these audits diagnose problems, [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] test interventions: a "diversified extremity" ranking algorithm on Bluesky reduces amplification of intergroup/moralized/emotional content and improves norm perception without harming enjoyment, while a client-side elite-blocking intervention on X shows that simply reducing exposure to political elites' posts (and mentions of them) lowers misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture rather than individual trait or belief. [[Allcott2025-jb]] offers a sobering counterpoint from the advertising side of the same 2020 election cycle: randomly removing political ads (not organic elite content) from feeds produced no detectable attitudinal effects, suggesting the causal leverage of feed algorithms and elite visibility may exceed that of paid persuasion per se. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] reinforces the null-effects strand, finding untrustworthy-source exposure on Facebook/Instagram concentrated among few users and unmoved attitudinally by reduced exposure — a useful counterweight to alarmist readings of the algorithmic-curation literature.

## Political advertising: targeting, delivery, and downstream effects

A second cluster asks directly about paid political advertising as a causal lever. [[Kim2026-wg]] provides the clearest evidence yet of a specific adversarial mechanism — geo-racially targeted digital voter suppression ads in the 2016 US election — showing that average-treatment-effect designs mask large, concentrated turnout effects among the targeted subpopulations, a methodological warning that echoes throughout the topic. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] documents a parallel transparency intervention in a different democracy, using volunteer browser-plugin data donation to surface potentially irregular Facebook political ads during Brazil's 2018 election, extending post-2016 platform-accountability concerns beyond the US case. [[Votta2025-xz]] turns attention from targeting to delivery, arguing that ad-delivery algorithms on Meta and Google — not just advertiser choices — shape who sees political ads and at what cost, adding an opaque intermediate layer between campaign intent and audience exposure. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] extends causal ad-effect estimation into public health, using a compartmental epidemic model fit to geolocated antivaccine tweets to derive a lower-bound estimate of vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths attributable to platform-borne persuasive content — methodologically distinct from the others but substantively continuous with the project of linking online exposure to real-world behavioral outcomes.

## Media capture and covert influence on coverage

A parallel strand shifts from platforms to newsrooms, asking how advertisers, political elites, and digital networks covertly shape editorial content. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is the paradigm case: using automated content analysis and difference-in-differences around Austria's "Inseratenaffäre," it shows that government advertising functioned as a covert bribery channel increasing a chancellor's visibility and depressing coverage of his rivals — "paying positive to go negative" — demonstrating that quasi-experimental computational methods can detect media capture even when the underlying transaction is secret. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a structural, cross-national visibility bias rather than a bribery scheme: Radical Right parties received disproportionate news attention relative to polling and electoral results across five European countries ahead of the 2024 EU elections, a pattern robust across outlets of varying orientation and intensifying toward election day. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] identifies a related but distinct mechanism operating through digital rather than advertising incentives: Republican elites who adopted the "Critical Race Theory" frame were more ideologically extreme and more tightly networked with hyper-partisan media, and were rewarded with disproportionate engagement and coverage — a "logic of connective faction" in which hyper-partisan outlets function as digital surrogates reinforcing intra-party radicalization. [[Balluff2026-ev]] complicates assumptions about alternative media's independence from the mainstream, finding that German alternative outlets reference largely the same named entities as legacy media on Nord Stream 2 but organize them into more cohesive network structures and cite mainstream media more often — alternative media differentiate relationally, not referentially. [[Goel2025-iq]] identifies a subtler capture-adjacent phenomenon: users repurposing factually accurate mainstream articles to support misinformation narratives, showing that domain-level reliability metrics miss a large share of narrative-level misinformation diffusion. [[Makeev2026-ma]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] extend the media-capture and framing lens outward to state propaganda and geopolitics: the former theorizes a dual-objective model of Russian wartime messaging split between domestic legitimacy and foreign demoralization, and the latter shows Global North outlets frame AI pessimistically as risk/weapon while Global South outlets frame it optimistically as developmental "wonder," reflecting geopolitical position rather than technological substance.

## Measuring ideology and framing at scale

Underpinning much of this work is a methodological throughline in latent ideology and framing estimation. [[Eady2025-vm]] offers the most ambitious synthesis, jointly scaling news outlets, politicians, and ordinary users from shared URLs on a common metric, and showing that ideologically extreme legislators dominate the shared news environment and that electoral competitiveness predicts sharing polarization — a structural link between representation and the online information ecosystem. [[Lai2024-to]] extends the same correspondence-analysis logic from actors to content, scaling individual YouTube videos' ideology via cross-subreddit sharing patterns, addressing a gap the video-content domain had left open relative to legislator- and outlet-level scaling. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a lighter-weight alternative — prompting instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses — validated across languages and text types, positioning LLMs as a general substitute for Wordscores/Wordfish-style scaling. [[Lee2026-je]] pushes LLM-based inference further, showing that models can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical text by exploiting subtle, culturally politicized lexical cues, which is as much a privacy-risk finding as a measurement contribution. [[Arora2025-tx]] extends measurement into the visual and multi-modal register, arguing that text-only framing analysis with fixed frame sets misses systematic differences in how outlets frame identical events once images and cross-modal cues are considered.

## Conversational AI as a persuasion technology

A distinct but closely related cluster investigates generative AI itself as a novel persuasive medium, complementing the platform-algorithm studies above by asking about one-on-one conversational influence rather than feed exposure. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] maps the "levers" of AI persuasiveness across 19 models and hundreds of issues, finding that post-training and prompting strategy matter more than scale, that information density is the central mechanism, and — most consequentially — that the same levers that increase persuasion also degrade factual accuracy. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the comparison to elite human persuaders (professional canvassers, championship debaters) and finds frontier AI systems reliably out-persuade them, with the advantage again traceable to information throughput rather than rapport, and generalizing to real monetary donations. [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates and extends these persuasive-effect findings across four live elections, showing AI dialogue persuasion exceeds typical advertisement effect sizes, works via facts rather than manipulation, and shows a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy (AI advocating right-leaning candidates makes more inaccurate claims). [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows these effects generalize beyond electoral persuasion to durable belief and policy-support shifts on climate, vaccination, and inequality, concentrated among initial skeptics — the opposite audience pattern from static-message persuasion research. Against this backdrop of AI's growing persuasive power, [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] tests the regulatory response, finding that disclosing mere AI identity (as required by the EU AI Act) does little to blunt persuasion, whereas disclosing persuasive *intent* cuts the effect roughly in half — evidence that meaningful transparency regulation must target purpose rather than provenance.

## Institutional and geopolitical shaping of AI-mediated political discourse

A final cluster situates these platform- and model-level dynamics within larger institutional and geopolitical forces. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state media control leaks into LLM training data and measurably shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence in languages tied to low-media-freedom countries, effectively "laundering" state-coordinated content into ostensibly neutral AI answers — a training-data analogue to the media-capture dynamics documented in [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Iris2026-pg]], but operating on models rather than newsrooms. [[Beacken2026-zb]] surveys how GenAI propaganda use varies across six democratically weakened states, arguing against deterministic narratives of uniform AI-driven authoritarian capability in favor of structurally contingent adoption patterns, a framing that resonates with [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s dual-audience propaganda model. [[Richter2026-bt]] traces the pre-GenAI formation of AI discourse itself, showing sharply diverging US (industry/influencer-dominated) and German (institutionalized, pluralistic) stakeholder trajectories on Twitter between 2012–2021 that plausibly conditioned each country's subsequent AI regulatory posture. [[Dodds2026-df]] closes the loop by theorizing AI hype in journalism as a structuring infrastructure — not mere discourse — that redistributes legitimacy and resources through sourcing patterns and geopolitical framing, directly complementing [[Mota2026-sl59]]'s finding of North-South divergence in AI's political valence in the press. Finally, two more narrowly methodological contributions — [[Meher2025-qb]] on efficient LLM fine-tuning for conflict-event classification and [[Smith2025-kc]] on causally identifying "attention brokerage" as a driver of triad transitivity in social networks — extend this topic's computational toolkit into adjacent domains (event coding, network formation) that are likely to feed back into future studies of political-media dynamics on these same platforms.

Read together, this body of work traces an arc from early, largely reassuring findings about algorithmic filter bubbles ([[Bakshy2015-rn]]) toward a much more unsettled present: algorithms that measurably move attitudes and persist even after being switched off ([[Gauthier2026-iq]]), advertising and media-capture mechanisms detectable only through careful quasi-experimental design ([[Kim2026-wg]], [[Balluff2026-bv]]), and a new generation of conversational AI systems whose persuasive power now rivals or exceeds that of expert humans ([[Hackenburg2026-ud]]) while embedding state and institutional influence in ways audiences cannot easily see ([[Waight2026-ts]]). The connecting thread is methodological: nearly every advance here comes from designs that separate exposure from selection, disclosure from effect, and correlation from causal mechanism — the shared computational and quasi-experimental toolkit that defines this line of inquiry.
