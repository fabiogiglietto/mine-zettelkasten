# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## Media capture, advertiser influence, and the political economy of news attention

A cluster of papers treats news coverage itself as an outcome to be causally explained by hidden financial and political relationships rather than as a neutral backdrop. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is the clearest instance: using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian political news, it shows that government advertising revenue channeled to a tabloid tracks with a sharp rise in a chancellor's visibility and a turn toward negative coverage of his rivals — media capture operating through a "pay positive to go negative" logic rather than crude praise. [[Iris2026-pg]] extends the visibility question cross-nationally, documenting that radical-right actors receive disproportionate attention relative to their electoral weight across five European countries, a structural rather than outlet-specific pattern that complicates radical-right claims of media marginalization. [[Balluff2026-ev]] pushes back against an intuitive corollary of capture theory — that alternative media diverge from legacy outlets in *which* actors they cover — showing instead that German alternative and mainstream outlets reference largely the same entities around Nord Stream 2, with divergence appearing in relational network structure and in how often alternative media cite other media, rather than in raw actor selection.

Two papers reframe who does the "corrupting." [[Tornberg2025-ir]] argues, against a generic populism-as-misinformation account, that it is specifically the interaction of populism and right-wing ideology — radical-right populism — that predicts elite sharing of low-factuality sources across 26 countries, tying misinformation to a legitimacy crisis of liberal institutions rather than to media decline writ large. [[Knupfer2025-vt]] shows a related but distinct mechanism operating inside parties: a "logic of connective faction" in which ideologically extreme Republicans who adopt talking points like Critical Race Theory are rewarded with disproportionate attention from hyper-partisan media, radicalizing intra-party dynamics through digital surrogate networks. [[Makeev2026-ma]] extends the capture logic to interstate propaganda, theorizing that authoritarian regimes run dual-track messaging — reassuring domestic audiences while trying to demoralize foreign ones — a framework that [[Waight2026-ts]] operationalizes empirically by showing that state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized by commercial LLMs and measurably shifts model outputs toward the regime's preferred valence, effectively laundering state messaging through a supposedly neutral technology. [[Mota2026-sl59]] shows a parallel geopolitical asymmetry in how AI itself is covered, with Global North outlets framing it as risk and Global South outlets framing it as opportunity. Underlying much of this work is a methodological worry, made explicit in [[Schemer2026-mh]] and Gaisbauer2025-br: conclusions about partisan media influence and polarization are highly sensitive to how researchers operationalize "slant" and "polarization" in the first place, meaning the capture and bias literatures rest on measurement choices that are rarely stress-tested.

## Algorithms as political actors: causal audits of feeds and ads

A second, tightly interlinked cluster asks whether platform algorithms themselves — not just advertisers or elites — causally reshape political attitudes and exposure. [[Bakshy2015-rn]] is the foundational null-ish result: Facebook's News Feed modestly reduces cross-cutting exposure, but user choice reduces it more, pushing back against strong filter-bubble narratives. [[Allcott2025-jb]], part of the Meta-academic 2020 election collaboration, similarly finds that removing political ads from feeds produced no detectable attitudinal effects, while showing that most presidential ads target co-partisans rather than persuadable voters. Against this backdrop of nulls, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] delivers a striking positive result: switching users from chronological to algorithmic feeds on X causally shifted attitudes rightward, and — crucially — this shift persisted even after the algorithm was switched off, because exposure had caused users to follow new accounts that kept feeding them the same content. This asymmetric, follow-graph-mediated mechanism helps reconcile the earlier Meta nulls with claims of algorithmic radicalization, a reconciliation explicitly discussed in [[Allen2025-ot]], which situates platform-independent experimental tools (such as those audited by browser extensions) as a necessary third way between lab experiments and increasingly restricted platform collaborations.

[[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] probe the algorithmic substrate more directly: the former shows that apparent right-wing amplification on post-Musk Twitter is better explained by rewarding agitating content and proximity to Musk than by political leaning per se, while the latter reconstructs X's actual embedding space and finds that a linear direction correlates almost perfectly with users' left-right position — an inadvertent, GDPR-relevant political profile that can be surgically removed without destroying recommendation relevance. [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user choice for YouTube, finding rabbit holes but not echo chambers or radicalization pathways, alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt independent of user behavior. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] treat the feed itself as a historically contingent, actively governed object: the former shows lagged, section-specific effects of documented Facebook ranking changes on News Feed engagement, and the latter shows that partisan and quality signals in Facebook's share-to-view amplification fluctuate sharply around known "break the glass" interventions, evidence against the idea of a stable, neutral pipeline from sharing to viewing. [[Brady2026-ln]] closes the loop experimentally, showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms of political dialogue, while a custom "diversified extremity" algorithm can partially repair this without sacrificing platform enjoyment — a rare demonstration that algorithmic design, not just removal, is a lever for democratic health. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] complements this by showing that simply reducing the visibility of political elites (rather than political content generally) improves factual knowledge and reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] extend the causal-ad-effects tradition to delivery mechanics and epidemiological outcomes respectively, the latter linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to a mechanistic SIRVA epidemic model to estimate downstream vaccine refusals, cases, and deaths — one of the few studies in this set to trace online speech all the way to offline mortality.

## Latent ideology at scale: scaling methods for content and users

A parallel methodological arc concerns how to measure ideology itself from behavioral traces, extending classical ideal-point estimation beyond legislators. [[Eady2025-vm]] jointly estimates ideology for media outlets, politicians, and ordinary users from shared URLs on a single common scale, showing that ideologically extreme politicians dominate the visible news-sharing environment and that electoral non-competitiveness predicts more polarized sharing — a structural link from gerrymandering to online polarization. [[Lai2024-to]] extends the same sharing-based logic to a new unit of analysis, video content, recovering the ideology of individual YouTube videos from cross-subreddit linking patterns. [[Green2025-ap]] complicates both projects by showing that source-level (domain) ideology scores systematically mask story-level heterogeneity — moderate-scoring outlets like the Wall Street Journal contain highly heterogeneous "curation bubbles" of stories with divergent partisan audiences, meaning the field's dominant measurement unit (the domain) may misrepresent the phenomenon it claims to capture. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a genuinely new measurement technology: prompting instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on ideological dimensions and averaging responses, which correlates well with established benchmarks across languages and text types — a lightweight alternative to Wordscores/Wordfish-style scaling that dovetails with [[Bouchaud2026-lr]]'s discovery that ideology is linearly encoded inside commercial recommender embeddings.

## Large language models as persuaders, profilers, and mediators

A rapidly growing subset of the corpus treats LLMs not as measurement tools but as political actors in their own right — capable of persuading, inferring hidden traits, and being manipulated. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, raising the specter of scalable political microtargeting from innocuous text. On the persuasion side, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] systematically decomposes what makes conversational AI persuasive, finding that post-training and information density matter far more than model scale or personalization — and documenting a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the claim, showing frontier AI out-persuades even elite, coached human debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage traceable to sheer information throughput rather than rapport. [[Lin2025-xp]] corroborates this across four real elections, finding AI dialogues shift vote preferences more than traditional ads while relying mainly on facts rather than manipulation, though right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate — echoing asymmetries documented elsewhere in this topic (e.g. [[Tornberg2025-ir]], [[Renault2025-uh]]). Two papers qualify the seemingly unstoppable trajectory of AI persuasion: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely telling users an LLM is biased against their party cuts its persuasive power by roughly a quarter, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing an AI's persuasive *intent* (not just its AI-ness, as mandated by the EU AI Act) cuts persuasion in half — together suggesting that perceived neutrality and purpose, not mere machine identity, are the operative levers for meaningful transparency regulation. [[Paci2025-ag]] supplies a countervailing limitation: even frontier LLMs struggle to correctly interpret implicatures and presuppositions in real Italian political speech, suggesting their persuasive fluency may outpace their pragmatic comprehension. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] rounds out the LLM-as-instrument theme by showing multimodal LLMs like GPT-4o now outperform specialized computer-vision pipelines for analyzing visual political communication, lowering the barrier to large-scale image-based studies.

## Coordinated behavior, bots, and the structure of covert influence

Several papers ask whether coordinated and automated accounts meaningfully shape political discourse, arriving at mixed answers that complicate alarmist narratives. [[Appel2026-qr]] provides the most comprehensive accounting of deceptive networks in the 2020 US election, finding reach is highly concentrated in a few networks and driven mostly by indirect resharing rather than direct posting — and that naive correlations between exposure and downstream beliefs vanish once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. [[Di-Marco2025-aa]] formalizes influence measurement for coordinated accounts in retweet cascades and finds that real-world coordinated accounts in the 2019 UK election achieve influence far below optimal or even greedy benchmarks, resembling random placement — evidence that CIB may matter less than commonly assumed, simply for want of resources and strategy. [[Smith2025-kc]] offers a complementary, causally identified micro-mechanism for how amplification does translate into structural change, showing that when high-degree "attention brokers" retweet an account, their followers causally accelerate new following ties to that account — a local engine for the triad transitivity that shapes network topology more broadly. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] finds bots function as effective second-level agenda-builders against campaigns (especially via negative sentiment) but have little traction on the press, suggesting institutional insulation matters. [[Bailard2024-pj]] and [[Kim2026-br]] extend coordinated-influence analysis to extremist mobilization and cross-national information warfare respectively: the former shows Proud Boys' online framing Granger-causes offline violence in a reciprocal online-offline cycle, while the latter documents two decades of suspected state-linked trolling in Korean news comments organized around morally condemning rhetoric rather than overt propaganda. [[Gerard2025-br]] contributes a platform-agnostic method (CANE) for reconstructing discourse networks from narrative co-participation rather than interaction data, revealing a tiny set of "bridge users" who seed most cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] and [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] document earlier, more descriptive attempts to audit irregular advertising and coordination in the 2018 Brazilian and 2021 German elections, laying groundwork later studies formalize.

## Misinformation as narrative and diffusion, not just false facts

A distinct thread argues that misinformation is poorly captured by binary true/false or domain-blocklist metrics, and instead lives in framing, narrative, and sharing dynamics. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] jointly develop this argument through qualitative-computational analysis of election-integrity rumors, showing that misleading claims typically combine accurate evidence with distorted political frames, and that meaning is collaboratively constructed by influencers, elites, and audiences across the run-up to and aftermath of the 2020 election — a "deep storytelling" process invisible at the level of any single post. [[Goel2025-iq]] operationalizes a version of this insight quantitatively, showing that mainstream news articles co-shared with fake news disproportionately contain narrative structures typical of misinformation, meaning source-level reliability scores miss a large channel of narrative repurposing. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] extends the causal arrow the other direction, examining how ordinary journalistic strategies can inadvertently seed new conspiracy beliefs. On the diffusion side, [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] characterizes the reach and virality of misinformation across a billion reshared Facebook posts, while [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] argues that the field's dominant "direct referrer" paradigm for attributing misinformation visits to platforms drastically undercounts platforms' true influence once indirect and delayed pathways are modeled counterfactually — with Twitter and YouTube showing especially large hidden effects for Republicans and Democrats respectively. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] turn to elite-level asymmetries: the former shows Republicans are flagged 2.3x more often than Democrats even under X's crowdsourced Community Notes system, undermining claims that this asymmetry reflects fact-checker bias, while the latter shows institutional role and ideology jointly condition which elected officials share misinformation. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends the causal-exposure toolkit to WhatsApp in Brazil, addressing the understudied role of encrypted messaging. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies a structural caveat relevant to this whole cluster: because a small minority of highly active users generate most visible content, both citizens and researchers risk mistaking the visible "tip of the iceberg" for genuine public opinion.

## Multimodal and format-specific political communication

Several papers shift attention from text to the visual, sonic, and platform-specific packaging of political messages. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats tailoring securitizing visual discourse platform by platform — argumentative posts on Facebook/X versus memetic, affectively charged TikTok content using decontextualized foreign footage aimed at younger voters. [[Larsson2026-ro]] documents a decade of the Norwegian Progress Party's Facebook sentiment, finding negative content reliably drives shares and comments while positive content drives likes — a granular demonstration that engagement metrics encode distinct affective logics. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesizes this literature systematically, reviewing how Facebook's Reactions feature has become a proxy for controversy and emotional response in political research, especially for populist and far-right communication. [[Arora2025-tx]] pushes framing analysis explicitly into the multimodal register, showing that text-only frame extraction misses systematic cross-outlet differences visible only when images and their interaction with text are analyzed. [[Much2026-gu]] introduces "gendered media spaces" — nonpolitical entertainment podcasts where audiences sort by masculinity/femininity rather than ideology — as a novel, incidental channel through which Trump's 2024 campaign reached young men, complementing [[Lukito2026-il]]'s finding that right-leaning outlets curate substantially different, non-duplicated content across Twitter and Truth Social depending on platform affordances.

## Infrastructure: measurement, data access, and the politics of studying platforms

Underlying all of the above is a set of papers concerned less with substantive findings than with whether the field can trust its own instruments. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that web-scraping methodology itself — not just time delay — introduces large, non-randomly distributed distortions in measures of news consumption, with paywalled and interactive content most affected. [[Lukito2026-nb]] catalogs 72 data-access tools and diagnoses a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem structuring who can do this research at all, arguing platform-permission-based access is inherently ephemeral and calling for DSA-style regulatory mandates. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] anticipates many of these concerns a half-decade earlier, arguing that big data has simultaneously transformed political communication practice (blurred organizational boundaries, microtargeting, attention amplification) and the methods needed to study it. [[Meher2025-qb]] demonstrates that efficient LLM fine-tuning (QLoRA) can make sophisticated event classification accessible on consumer hardware, addressing resource barriers directly, while [[Dodds2026-df]] and [[Richter2026-bt]] turn a critical lens on the discourse *about* AI itself — the former arguing AI hype functions as a structuring infrastructure within journalism, the latter tracing how the stakeholders driving US and German AI discourse on Twitter diverged sharply (industry/influencer-dominated versus institutionalized), a divergence with downstream consequences for how each country now regulates the very generative AI systems studied throughout this collection.
