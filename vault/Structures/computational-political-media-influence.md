# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The causal turn in political advertising research

The oldest question in this literature — does political advertising work? — has been given increasingly rigorous causal answers, and the answers increasingly diverge depending on how bluntly "effect" is measured. [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s large-scale Meta collaboration, randomizing the removal of political ads from Facebook and Instagram feeds before the 2020 US election, found essentially nothing: ads were overwhelmingly targeted at co-partisans, not persuadable voters, and their removal moved no measured attitude. [[Kim2026-wg]] shows why average-treatment-effect designs like this can miss the real story: using direct exposure measurement rather than proxies, it finds that 2016 voter-suppression ads (including IRA-linked ones) were geo-racially targeted at non-White voters in contested counties, and that turnout effects—invisible in the aggregate—were large precisely among the targeted subgroup. Read together, these two papers argue that the central methodological lesson of the platform-advertising literature is heterogeneity: null average effects and consequential targeted effects are not in tension but describe the same underlying microtargeting logic from different vantage points. [[Votta2025-xz]] pushes on an upstream mechanism behind this heterogeneity, showing that ad-delivery algorithms themselves—not just advertiser targeting choices—shape who is reached and at what cost, adding an algorithmic layer of opacity to the targeting question. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]'s crowdsourced browser-plugin audit of Facebook ads during Brazil's 2018 election and [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]]'s epidemiological linkage of geolocated antivaccine tweets to county-level vaccination, case, and death counts extend the causal-inference toolkit to new outcomes (electoral irregularity, public health) and new populations, while [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies the conceptual scaffolding for the whole cluster, arguing that networked, weak-tie political organization and attention-economy amplification make micro-targeted advertising both more consequential and harder to regulate than the "follow the money" paradigm assumes.

## Covert influence: media capture and propaganda's dual audiences

A second strand asks not whether ads persuade audiences directly, but whether advertising money and state coordination can covertly reshape the news content audiences receive. [[Balluff2026-bv]] operationalizes this with a difference-in-differences design on the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre," showing that government-advertising largesse to a tabloid was followed by a doubling of a favored politician's visibility and, tellingly, a "pay positive to go negative" pattern in which coverage of his rivals turned more hostile — a computational signature of media capture in an ostensibly liberal democracy. [[Makeev2026-ma]] theorizes a parallel but distinct covert-influence logic at the level of the state itself, arguing that regimes dependent on domestic legitimacy run dual-track propaganda campaigns, tailoring reassuring narratives at home while trying to erode adversaries' resolve abroad. [[Waight2026-ts]] closes this cluster by tracing the same mechanism into a new pipeline: it shows that state-coordinated Chinese media is memorized in LLM training corpora and causally shifts model outputs toward pro-regime valence, especially in lower-media-freedom languages — media capture laundered, unintentionally, into the outputs of ostensibly neutral AI systems, and a bridge to the LLM-focused clusters below.

## Feeds, recommenders, and the politics of algorithmic curation

The largest cluster concerns whether platform algorithms themselves inject political bias beyond what users' own choices would produce, a question that traces back to [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s foundational Facebook study distinguishing network composition, algorithmic ranking, and individual clicks as separable sources of ideological sorting. Contemporary audits revisit this decomposition with more causal leverage and higher stakes. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X shows that switching users onto the algorithmic feed shifts attitudes rightward and that the effect persists asymmetrically after switching off, because the algorithm changes who users choose to follow — a mechanism that helps reconcile this result with the null findings of the 2020 Meta studies. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complicates the "algorithm favors the right" narrative further, showing that apparent partisan bias in Twitter's post-Musk recommender is better explained by rewarding agitating content and proximity to Musk himself than by ideology per se. [[Brown2026-br]] brings a similarly disentangling logic to YouTube, formally separating echo chambers, rabbit holes, and radicalization pathways and finding strong content-driven rabbit holes but no partisan radicalization, alongside a platform-wide conservative tilt independent of user identity. Two papers extend this audit tradition methodologically: [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] uses a custom X client to show that suppressing elite-authored (and elite-referencing) content causally reduces misinformation susceptibility without altering polarization, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture; and [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] shows that the standard "direct referrer" method for blaming platforms for misinformation exposure radically understates true influence once indirect and delayed pathways are counted via counterfactual simulation. [[Allen2025-ot]] ties this whole methodological turn together explicitly, arguing that as API access closes off, platform-independent tools like these (and Piccardi et al.'s LLM-reranking browser extension) are becoming essential, positioned between lab experiments and full platform collaborations like the Facebook/Instagram Election Study. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] adds a structural discovery underlying much of this: reconstructing X's recommendation embedding space, it finds a linear direction that near-perfectly tracks users' Left-Right position, learned inadvertently and orthogonally to demographic attributes — direct evidence that recommenders can encode the very ideological signal whose downstream effects the audit papers are measuring.

## Measuring ideology at scale: from behavior to language models

A parallel methodological lineage asks how to measure ideological position itself, rather than its effects. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideal-point estimation from legislators and users to individual pieces of video content, scaling YouTube videos by which subreddits share them. [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes further, building a joint measurement model that places news outlets, politicians, and ordinary users on one ideological scale from shared URLs alone, and using it to show that polarized politicians dominate the shared-news environment and that electoral safety predicts more extreme sharing — a substantive link between representation and the polarized information ecosystem the earlier clusters study. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes a lighter-weight alternative to these homophily-based scaling methods: simply prompting instruction-tuned LLMs to place texts on a dimension and averaging responses, which correlates strongly with established benchmarks across languages and text types. [[Arora2025-tx]] pushes ideology/framing measurement beyond the left-right scalar altogether, arguing that automated framing analysis must incorporate visual and cross-modal cues, not just text, to capture how outlets across the political spectrum actually construct editorial meaning.

## The generative-AI turn: LLMs as persuaders, inferencers, and governance objects

The newest arc in this topic concerns generative AI itself as a political-media actor. A tight sequence of persuasion experiments establishes the core empirical facts: [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] finds that post-training and prompting strategies—mediated by information density—drive AI persuasiveness more than scale or personalization, and documents a persuasion-accuracy trade-off; [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] escalates the comparison, showing frontier AI out-persuades even elite competitive debaters and professional canvassers, with the advantage collapsing only when AI is throttled to human throughput, again implicating fact density as mechanism; [[Lin2025-xp]] validates these effects in real electoral contexts across four countries, finding AI persuades via facts rather than manipulation but that pro-right-leaning AI advocacy is systematically less accurate; and [[Kotz2026-lk]] shows these dialogue effects generalize across structurally distinct issues (climate, vaccines, inequality) and are strongest precisely among skeptics, the audience static messaging struggles to reach. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] then turns to governance, showing that the EU AI Act's mandated AI-identity disclosure barely dents persuasion, whereas disclosing persuasive *intent* cuts it roughly in half — reframing "meaningful transparency" for regulators around purpose rather than mere machine identity.

Adjacent to the persuasion studies sits a cluster on what LLMs can infer or fail to infer about political meaning. [[Lee2026-je]] shows LLMs can reliably infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary and raising fresh micro-targeting/privacy risks that echo the advertising-targeting concerns above. [[Paci2025-ag]] supplies a countervailing result, showing current LLMs still struggle to correctly interpret implicatures and presuppositions in real Italian political speech, suggesting a gap between models' capacity to detect *whose* alignment is present and their capacity to understand *what* manipulative political language is actually doing.

## Peripheral threads

A handful of papers sit at the topic's edges, extending its methods or concerns into adjacent territory rather than addressing advertising or media capture directly. [[Xue2025-bp]] examines emotionality as a strategic feature of fact-checking content, a media-effects question that dovetails with the persuasion-mechanism findings above. [[Smith2025-kc]] identifies a causal "attention broker" mechanism by which amplification accelerates tie formation in social networks, a structural analogue to the follow-persistence mechanism [[Gauthier2026-iq]] documents for feed algorithms. [[Richter2026-bt]] and [[Ng2026-og]] widen the lens furthest: the former traces which stakeholders (industry, government, individual influencers) have historically driven public AI discourse on Twitter, and the latter argues for embedding social theory into agentic AI design — both less about political advertising per se than about the broader sociotechnical and institutional conditions under which the platforms and models studied throughout this topic operate.
