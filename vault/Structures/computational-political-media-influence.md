# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

### The arc of inquiry

This body of work traces a methodological and substantive progression: from measuring *exposure* to political content, to estimating its *causal effects*, to uncovering the *hidden mechanisms*—algorithmic, financial, and now generative—by which political actors and platforms shape what audiences see. Early foundational pieces established that online political communication is not simply an undifferentiated "echo chamber" but a structure that varies by topic, platform, and actor ([[Barbera2015-fw]], [[Barbera2015-je]]), while [[Chadwick2013-ns]] supplied the theoretical scaffolding—the hybrid media system—for understanding how legacy and digital media now co-produce political reality. The papers filed here extend that lineage into the platform-experiment era and, most recently, into a generative-AI era in which synthetic persuasion and AI-mediated discourse raise the same old questions of influence and accountability in new technical guises.

### From exposure to causal effect: the Meta collaboration and its aftermath

A cluster of large, platform-partnered field experiments forms the empirical backbone of the causal literature. [[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], and [[Nyhan2023-gb]] jointly demonstrate that removing reshares, switching to chronological feeds, or reducing like-minded exposure substantially alters what Facebook/Instagram users see—yet produces little to no detectable shift in polarization or political knowledge. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this null-effect pattern to political advertising itself, finding that stripping ads from feeds for six weeks changed nothing measurable, largely because campaigns target co-partisans rather than persuadable voters. Read together with [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]—which show that ideological segregation and misinformation reach are asymmetric and right-skewed even though average effects are muted—this cluster complicates simple "platforms cause polarization" narratives while still documenting consequential asymmetries in what different audiences encounter. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] pushes this further by showing that the very mapping from shares to views is not stable but fluctuates with Facebook's own governance interventions, reframing the platform as an active, time-varying curator rather than a neutral conduit—a direct challenge to treating "the algorithm" as a fixed background condition in these experiments.

The null-effect consensus from the 2020 Meta studies is complicated by newer work using independent, non-platform-sanctioned methods. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X finds that switching users onto the algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes rightward—asymmetrically and persistently, because the algorithm changes who users choose to follow, not just what they are shown transiently. This "follow-persistence" mechanism offers a plausible reconciliation with the earlier null Meta findings: effects that operate through durable network restructuring may be invisible to studies that only toggle feed ranking briefly. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] deepen this algorithmic turn, respectively showing that right-leaning visibility gains on post-acquisition Twitter are better explained by agitation and proximity to Musk than by ideology per se, and that X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect left-right axis in its embedding space—raising both privacy and fairness concerns distinct from, but complementary to, the engagement-based analyses. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] and [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] extend the causal-intervention paradigm beyond Meta's walled garden: the former shows that blocking political elites (not just reshares) reduces misinformation susceptibility, reframing misinformation as an exposure-architecture problem; the latter shows that conventional "direct referrer" methods radically underestimate platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure once indirect and counterfactual pathways are modeled, with YouTube's effect on Democrats' exposure to liberal misinformation emerging as a striking example invisible to older designs.

### Misinformation, virality, and the limits of alarm

A second thread interrogates exactly how much online misinformation actually matters, and for whom. [[Guess2020-rr]] and [[Eady2023-xg]] are foundational here, showing that "fake news" and Russian IRA content reached only a narrow, already-committed slice of the public and bore no measurable relationship to vote choice—corroborated at larger scale by [[Appel2026-qr]]'s audit of 49 deceptive networks, where associations between exposure and downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled. Yet this skepticism about aggregate effects coexists with evidence of real, if concentrated, harms: [[Allen2024-av]] shows that *unflagged*, mainstream vaccine-skeptical content did far more damage to vaccination intentions than flagged misinformation, because reach dwarfs per-view persuasiveness; [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] uses epidemic modeling to estimate a causal, if modest, death toll attributable to antivaccine tweets; and [[Yang2023-cg]] shows that image-based misinformation—ignored by link-based measures—is far more prevalent and partisan-skewed than previously estimated. [[Kim2026-wg]] demonstrates that average-treatment-effect designs can themselves obscure serious harms, finding that geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 depressed turnout sharply among the specific populations they targeted even though aggregate effects looked small. Together these papers argue for disaggregated, mechanism-sensitive measurement over blanket claims about platform effects, a methodological lesson echoed by [[Tornberg2025-ir]], which shows that misinformation sharing by elites is not a generic populist or right-wing pathology but specifically a radical-right-populist one, and [[Tai2026-qk]], which examines institutional and ideological checks on elite misinformation sharing more broadly.

### Media capture, advertising, and covert influence

Where the first two threads focus on platform algorithms and virality, a distinct set of papers investigates older forms of covert influence now made newly detectable through text-at-scale methods. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is exemplary: using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text, it detects the fingerprints of the "Inseratenaffäre" advertising-for-coverage scandal—visibility gains for Kurz and harsher coverage of his rivals—even though the underlying bribery was covert, demonstrating that computational designs can expose media capture that direct evidence cannot. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents a related but more diffuse phenomenon: systematic, cross-national overrepresentation of the Radical Right in European election coverage, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks regardless of outlet orientation, suggesting structural rather than conspiratorial causes. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies an early conceptual map of these dynamics—blurred organizational boundaries, micro-targeted advertising, and attention-amplification strategies—that anticipates much of the empirical work that follows, including auditing efforts like [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]]'s crowdsourced detection of irregular Facebook ads in Brazil and [[Votta2025-xz]]'s investigation of how ad-delivery algorithms themselves shape reach and cost in political campaigns. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] and [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] extend the lens to coordinated behavior and bot-driven agenda-building around elections, with the latter showing that social bots function as a genuinely novel category of agenda-builder that most strongly sways campaigns rather than the press.

### Scaling ideology and content: measurement infrastructure

A methodological throughline across the topic is the development of scalable tools for estimating ideology and framing from digital traces. [[Barbera2015-je]] remains the ur-text, establishing network-based ideal-point estimation from Twitter follows; [[Eady2025-vm]] generalizes this to shared URLs, jointly scaling media outlets, politicians, and ordinary users on one behavioral dimension and showing that reduced electoral competition is associated with more polarized elite sharing. [[Lai2024-to]] extends latent ideology estimation to an entirely new unit of analysis—individual YouTube videos—via Reddit cross-posting patterns, while [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] shows that LLMs can be prompted and averaged to produce reliable position estimates directly from text, bypassing bespoke scaling models. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] push this infrastructure into the visual and multimodal domain, validating LLM-based image analysis for candidate visibility and extending framing analysis beyond text. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]]-adjacent work on LLM inference shows these same scaling capacities carry a darker twin: LLMs can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse, raising the microtargeting and privacy concerns that [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] had flagged years earlier as an open regulatory problem. [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] address a related measurement gap—tracking narrative (not just lexical or topical) diffusion across languages and platforms—with the latter identifying a tiny set of "bridge users" responsible for most cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X, and [[Pante2025-pq]] cautions that even well-established coordination-detection findings about inter-state influence operations need re-examination against proper control baselines.

### The generative-AI inflection point

The most recent arc concerns what happens when the content itself, not just its curation, becomes machine-generated. A cluster of experimental papers establishes that conversational AI is a remarkably potent persuader: [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] identifies information density as the central persuasive lever and documents a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off; [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] shows AI out-persuades even elite human debaters and professional canvassers, with the same fact-density mechanism implicated; [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates large persuasion effects across four live elections, while noting a partisan asymmetry in factual accuracy; and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing AI *identity* does little to blunt this persuasion, whereas disclosing *intent* cuts it roughly in half—an important corrective to transparency regulation modeled on the EU AI Act. These findings intersect with the covert-influence thread above: if AI persuasion operates largely through legitimate-seeming factual density rather than manipulation, then the media-capture-style detection methods developed for human-authored covert influence ([[Balluff2026-bv]], [[Iris2026-pg]]) may need adaptation for a world of synthetic political content, a concern that [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] begin to document empirically in far-right and democratically weakened contexts, and that [[Dodds2026-df]], [[Richter2026-bt]], and [[Mota2026-sl59]] situate within the broader discursive and geopolitical construction of "AI hype" itself.

### Platform-specific audits and open questions

Several papers resist easy synthesis but sharpen the topic's edges: [[Brown2026-br]] disentangles algorithmic from user-driven effects on YouTube, finding rabbit holes but not radicalization, alongside a subtle platform-wide conservative tilt; [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] finds a similar incidental pro-AfD skew on TikTok, attributable to supply-side virality rather than overt platform bias; [[Lukito2026-il]] documents how right-leaning outlets curate distinct content across Twitter and Truth Social; [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] and [[Karlsson2026-hd]] examine, respectively, the perverse engagement incentives facing elected officials and the self-fulfilling polarizing potential of *discourse about* election interference itself; and [[Much2026-gu]] introduces "gendered media spaces" to explain how non-political podcast content became an incidental vector for ideological sorting in the 2024 US election. Methodological pieces such as [[Stagnaro2025-pz]], [[Meher2025-qb]], and [[Wan2026-ai]] round out the topic by interrogating the sampling, model-adaptation, and individual-difference foundations on which all of this large-scale computational inference ultimately rests.

Across this arc, the field moves from documenting *that* online political media environments are segregated, capturable, and persuadable, toward specifying the precise *mechanisms*—algorithmic follow-persistence, information density, covert advertising arrangements, embedding-space profiling—through which that influence is exercised, while remaining persistently alert to the risk of mistaking correlation, selection, or aggregate nulls for the absence of consequential, if concentrated, effects.
