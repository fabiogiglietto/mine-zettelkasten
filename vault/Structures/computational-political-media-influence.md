# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The experimental turn: what does political advertising and algorithmic curation actually do?

The empirical core of this literature is a sequence of large-scale field experiments asking a deceptively simple question — does exposure to political content on a platform change what people believe, feel, or do? The answer that emerges is stubbornly heterogeneous. The Meta-collaboration studies [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Guess2023-ur]] randomized away political ads, reshares, and algorithmic ranking itself during the 2020 US election and found almost uniformly null effects on polarization, knowledge, and behavior, despite large and measurable changes in the on-platform content mix. [[Bail2018-fk]] had earlier shown that even intergroup contact-style interventions (following opposing-view bots) could backfire rather than moderate. Read together, these papers constitute a kind of null-results canon that the rest of the topic is implicitly arguing with or around.

That canon is increasingly challenged by studies that change *what* is manipulated or *how* exposure is measured. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] directly reconciles its findings with the Meta null results by showing that X's algorithmic feed shifts attitudes conservative not through static content ranking but through a persistence mechanism — it changes who users *follow*, an effect that survives even after the algorithm is switched off. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] complements this by decomposing "right-leaning accounts gain visibility" into behavioral mechanisms (agitation, proximity to Elon Musk, verification status) rather than ideology per se. [[Brown2026-br]] performs an analogous supply/demand decomposition for YouTube, finding real "rabbit hole" dynamics but attributing apparent echo chambers to user choice rather than the algorithm, while still detecting a platform-wide conservative tilt in recommendations. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] pushes the intervention further upstream, showing that suppressing elite-authored (and elite-referencing) content — rather than political content in general — causally improves belief accuracy without moving polarization, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure architecture rather than individual susceptibility. [[Allen2025-ot]] explicitly theorizes this turn toward "platform-independent" experimentation (browser extensions, custom clients) as a methodological response to closing API access, positioning it between lab control and platform-cooperation studies and using it to explain why Piccardi et al.'s reranking intervention found effects where FIES did not.

Targeted and suppressive advertising receives its own causal treatment. [[Kim2026-wg]] recovers a canonical null-effects puzzle (prior IRA studies found little impact) by measuring *direct* exposure and *heterogeneous* effects, showing that 2016 digital voter-suppression ads were geo-racially targeted and depressed turnout sharply among the specific subpopulations they targeted — an argument that average treatment effects obscure microtargeted harm. [[Eady2023-xg]] runs the companion analysis for IRA influence more broadly and finds the opposite: exposure was real but so concentrated among already-committed partisans, and so dwarfed by domestic media exposure, that no attitudinal effect is detectable — a sober corrective to interference narratives that nonetheless shares [[Kim2026-wg]]'s insistence on exposure-based measurement over indirect proxies. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] extend the ad-auditing project to delivery algorithms and crowdsourced transparency tooling respectively, documenting that platform-side mechanics (cost, reach, irregular targeting) are themselves objects worth auditing independently of advertiser intent. [[Bollenbacher2026-vz]] takes the causal-inference ambition furthest downstream, linking geolocated antivaccine tweets to a compartmental epidemic model to estimate attributable vaccinations refused, cases, and deaths — translating platform-exposure causality into public-health outcomes. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends the deactivation paradigm beyond feed-based platforms entirely, to WhatsApp in Brazil, arguing that private messaging requires its own causal toolkit.

## Covert influence and media capture

A second strand turns the causal lens away from users and onto *outlets*, asking whether advertising money can covertly buy favorable coverage. [[Balluff2026-bv]] is the keystone case study: using automated content analysis and a difference-in-differences design across 220,000+ Austrian news articles, it detects a quasi-experimental signature of media capture — rising visibility (but not favorability) for Sebastian Kurz in a tabloid allegedly bought off, coupled with more negative coverage of his rivals — demonstrating that computational methods can surface "journalism for sale" even when the underlying transaction is secret. [[Goel2025-iq]] identifies a structurally similar blind spot from the opposite direction: factually accurate mainstream articles are disproportionately co-shared with fake news to construct misleading narratives, meaning source-level reliability metrics miss a large class of narrative capture that operates through genuine journalism rather than fabrication. Both papers argue, in effect, that the unit of analysis for "misinformation" or "bias" must move below the outlet level — to article, paragraph, or narrative — to detect influence that domain blacklists and naive sentiment counts cannot see. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] extend this granular, message-level sensibility to fact-checking emotionality and multimodal framing respectively, both arguing that existing computational measures of media content (binary veracity, text-only frames) are too coarse to capture how persuasion and bias actually operate in practice. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies an early conceptual frame for much of this cluster, arguing that networked collective action, microtargeting, and attention-amplification strategies jointly erode the money-trail and organizational-boundary assumptions that older regulatory and methodological tools relied on — a diagnosis this literature has since operationalized empirically.

## Measuring ideology at scale: from legislators to videos to embeddings

A parallel methodological lineage asks not whether exposure changes attitudes but how to *measure* the ideological position of actors and content in the first place. [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] establish the foundational network-homophily approach — inferring ideal points from Twitter follow/retweet structure — and already identify the topic-dependence of polarization that later papers (e.g. [[Flaxman2016-lm]] on browsing data) would generalize across media consumption more broadly, with [[Flaxman2016-lm]] showing that social media and search modestly *increase* both segregation and opposing-view exposure simultaneously, while direct browsing to mainstream outlets remains dominant. [[Eady2025-vm]] updates this tradition by jointly scaling media, politicians, and the mass public from shared URLs alone, substantively linking electoral competitiveness to elite polarization in sharing behavior — a measurement innovation with a causal-adjacent payoff. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] explicitly critiques the field's reliance on unidimensional, outlet-level ideology scores and proposes a multi-level "cartography" spanning story, outlet, and content.

This scaling project then extends beyond text and users to new content modalities and new tools. [[Lai2024-to]] estimates latent ideology for individual YouTube videos via Reddit cross-posting patterns, explicitly generalizing ideal-point estimation from political actors to video content itself — a direct methodological ancestor of [[Brown2026-br]]'s BERT-based video-ideology measure used to audit YouTube's recommender. [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] discovers that X's black-box recommender embedding *already* encodes a near-perfect linear Left-Right direction without being designed to, empirically validating the linear representation hypothesis in a real production system and raising the prospect of deliberately scrubbing political information from recommendations. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[Lee2026-je]] mark the LLM-ification of ideology scaling: the former shows that simply asking and averaging LLM judgments reproduces established benchmark positions for political texts, while the latter shows LLMs can infer users' political alignment even from nonpolitical discourse, exploiting subtle culturally-politicized lexical cues — turning ideology estimation from a structural/network problem into a semantic inference problem, with attendant privacy risks that echo [[Bouchaud2026-lr]]'s profiling concerns.

## Misinformation's exposure-impact gap

A recurring methodological lesson across several papers is that *measuring exposure* and *measuring persuasive impact* are distinct problems that must be multiplied together, not conflated. [[Allen2024-av]] makes this the explicit organizing principle: flagged misinformation is more persuasive per view but reaches almost no one, while unflagged, "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content reaches tens of millions and drives most of the aggregate harm — a finding that directly parallels [[Goel2025-iq]]'s argument about mainstream-content repurposing and [[Eady2023-xg]]'s concentration-of-exposure logic. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] generalizes the exposure half of this equation across seven platforms, showing that "direct referrer" methods radically undercount misinformation exposure relative to a counterfactual "embedded referrer" framework, while cautioning that platform removal would also disproportionately cut high-quality news — a sobering methodological corrective for anyone using simple click-chain measures (a critique with the same spirit as [[Yang2023-cg]]'s demonstration that image-based misinformation, invisible to link-based methods, may be the platform's largest misinformation category by volume, asymmetrically concentrated on the right).

## Large language models as persuaders, profilers, and launderers

The newest and fastest-growing cluster asks what happens when the content itself is generated by LLMs rather than curated by humans or algorithms. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish the empirical ceiling of this capability: conversational AI not only persuades but, with the right post-training and prompting levers, out-persuades expert human canvassers and elite debaters, with information density/fact throughput as the operative mechanism — alongside a troubling persuasion-accuracy trade-off. [[Lin2025-xp]] replicates and extends these persuasion effects across real national elections, again attributing gains to facts and evidence rather than manipulation, while documenting a partisan asymmetry in claim accuracy that mirrors asymmetries found elsewhere in this literature (e.g., [[Yang2023-cg]]'s partisan misinformation skew). Two papers complicate the picture: [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that perceived political bias in the messenger — not the message — sharply attenuates persuasion, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing AI *identity* (per the EU AI Act) does almost nothing to blunt persuasion, whereas disclosing *persuasive intent* cuts it roughly in half, jointly arguing that transparency regimes focused on "is this AI" are aimed at the wrong lever.

Beyond persuasion, LLMs are shown to perform (and sometimes fail at) the inferential and representational tasks central to this topic. [[Lee2026-je]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] both demonstrate ideological signal leaking from ostensibly neutral systems; [[Waight2026-ts]] extends this to a geopolitical register, showing that state media control over training data causes LLMs to launder pro-regime content into seemingly neutral outputs, with effects scaling with national media unfreedom — a direct computational echo of the media-capture mechanism documented qualitatively in [[Balluff2026-bv]] and thematically in [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s account of dual-audience state propaganda. [[Paci2025-ag]] supplies a cautionary counterpoint, showing current LLMs are poor at the pragmatic task of interpreting implicit, manipulative content in real political speech — a reminder that the same systems celebrated for persuasion and inference elsewhere in this cluster have real ceilings. Methodological papers [[Meher2025-qb]] and [[Farjam2026-zg]] round out the toolkit side, demonstrating how fine-tuned and locally-hosted LLMs can replace or augment human coders for conflict-event classification and multilingual content analysis, respectively — infrastructure that much of the above substantive work depends on.

## The discursive and institutional surround

A final set of papers steps back from causal and measurement questions to ask how AI and platforms are themselves talked about, and by whom — contextualizing the technical findings above within a politics of narrative and institutional power. [[Dodds2026-df]] frames "AI hype" as a systemic, resource-redistributing infrastructure embedded in journalistic sourcing practices; [[Richter2026-bt]] traces the stakeholder composition of that discourse longitudinally, showing German institutionalization against American industry/influencer capture; [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] and [[Mota2026-sl59]] extend discourse analysis to Google's AI Overviews and Global North/South framings of AI respectively, both finding systematic asymmetries in whose interests and anxieties are rendered visible. These papers do not test causal claims in the manner of the rest of the topic, but they supply the institutional backdrop — who controls narrative legitimacy, whose content trains the models, whose advertising dollars buy coverage — against which the causal and measurement findings above should be read: media capture, algorithmic ideology-profiling, and LLM persuasion are not isolated technical phenomena but instances of a single recurring struggle over who shapes the political information environment and who can see that shaping happening.
