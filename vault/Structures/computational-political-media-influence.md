# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## Causal Effects of Political Advertising and Platform Design

At the empirical core of this collection sits a cluster of large-scale field experiments asking whether paid political advertising and algorithmic curation actually move attitudes and behavior. The Meta-collaboration studies—[[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Guess2023-ur]]—converge on a strikingly consistent null: removing political ads, reshares, or algorithmic ranking from Facebook/Instagram feeds during the 2020 US election altered what people saw (less political news, less untrustworthy content, less engagement) but barely touched polarization, knowledge, or behavior. [[Eady2023-xg]] extends this minimal-effects conclusion to foreign influence operations, showing that exposure to Russian IRA content in 2016 was dwarfed by domestic media exposure and bore no detectable relationship to attitude change. [[Appel2026-qr]] generalizes the pattern further across 49 deceptive networks in 2020, finding that naive correlations between exposure and downstream beliefs evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled—a methodological corrective to a literature prone to overclaiming from observational exposure data.

Against this backdrop of nulls, [[Gauthier2026-iq]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] complicate the minimal-effects consensus by locating conditions under which platform mechanics do matter. Gauthier's X field experiment finds that the "For You" algorithm causally shifts attitudes rightward—not by direct persuasion but by inducing users to follow conservative accounts whose influence persists after the algorithm is switched off, a sticky-exposure mechanism that helps reconcile the apparent contradiction with Meta's null findings. Kim's EScope study similarly argues that average treatment effects mask real harm: geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 depressed turnout sharply among the specific subpopulations they targeted, even though an ATE-only lens would miss this. Together these papers suggest the "ads don't work" finding is partly an artifact of looking for homogeneous, average effects in contexts where platform and advertiser power operates through targeting and persistence rather than uniform persuasion. [[Votta2025-xz]] and [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] push on adjacent infrastructural questions—how delivery algorithms shape the cost/reach of political ads, and how crowdsourced auditing can surface irregular targeting—underscoring that the opacity of ad delivery systems remains a first-order obstacle to causal inference in this space.

## Media Capture, Covert Influence, and Visibility Bias

A second thread moves from advertising-as-persuasion to advertising-as-leverage: how money and coordinated activity covertly shape what counts as news. [[Balluff2026-bv]] offers the most direct evidence of this dynamic, using difference-in-differences on two decades of Austrian news text to show that government ad revenue bought not glowing coverage of Kurz but a "pay positive to go negative" pattern—boosted visibility and more negative coverage of his rivals—illustrating how media capture can operate without ever producing crude propaganda. This sits alongside [[Iris2026-pg]]'s cross-national finding that European radical-right parties receive structurally disproportionate visibility relative to their polling and electoral strength, a bias that is outlet-agnostic and intensifies near election day, suggesting that attention economics rather than deliberate collusion can independently amplify fringe actors. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] and [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] extend the coordinated-influence lens to Serbia and Germany respectively, with the former arguing that mimetic, decentralized coordinated inauthentic behavior (as practiced by SNS-linked networks) is harder to detect than classic bot farms—an important corrective to detection-centric CIB research. [[Tornberg2025-ir]] reframes elite misinformation itself as a structural-ideological phenomenon: not populism or right-wing ideology alone but their conjunction (radical-right populism) predicts parliamentarians' sharing of low-factuality sources, tying media capture and visibility bias back to a coherent elite political-economy story about legitimacy crises and clickbait symbiosis. [[Tai2026-qk]] adds an institutional dimension, suggesting that officials' misinformation sharing is checked (or not) by their institutional role and ideological position. Collectively, these papers argue that "media bias" in the platform era increasingly means subtle, quasi-experimentally detectable capture and visibility asymmetry rather than overt propaganda.

## Measuring Latent Ideology and Framing at Scale

A methodological throughline concerns how to recover ideology and framing from behavioral traces rather than self-report. The foundational network-based approach of [[Barbera2015-je]] and [[Barbera2015-fw]] established that Twitter following/retweeting structure encodes valid, scalable ideological signal for both elites and masses—work that [[Eady2025-vm]] updates and extends by jointly estimating ideology for media, politicians, and the public from shared URLs, revealing that ideologically extreme politicians dominate the shared information environment and that electoral safety breeds polarized sharing. [[Lai2024-to]] pushes this correspondence-analysis tradition into a new domain, inferring the latent ideology of YouTube videos from cross-subreddit sharing patterns, while [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] proposes an entirely different mechanism—prompting and averaging LLM judgments—to scale political text positioning across languages and genres, in effect replacing Wordscores/Wordfish-style estimation with generative-model elicitation. [[Arora2025-tx]] complements this ideological-scaling work with multimodal framing analysis, arguing that text-only frame extraction misses systematic visual and cross-modal editorial choices that likely covary with outlet ideology, and [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] demonstrates that multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) now outperform specialized computer-vision pipelines for analyzing visual political communication on Instagram, lowering the barrier to this kind of visual-ideological research. [[Farjam2026-zg]] and [[Meher2025-qb]] round out the methods cluster as infrastructure papers, showing how locally-hosted and fine-tuned LLMs can replicate and scale human content-analysis and event-classification coding—technical groundwork that enables much of the above substantive work.

## Algorithmic Curation and Radicalization Pathways

A closely related set of papers interrogates whether recommendation algorithms themselves manufacture ideological distortion, independent of user choice. [[Brown2026-br]] offers the most rigorous disentangling to date, using an audit design on YouTube that separates supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user selection; it finds no evidence of algorithm-driven echo chambers or radicalization pathways, but strong evidence of short-horizon "rabbit holes" and a platform-wide drift toward moderately conservative content for everyone. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] reaches a structurally similar conclusion for post-acquisition Twitter/X: apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification is largely explained by behavioral features (posting agitating content, proximity to Elon Musk) rather than political leaning per se, though the algorithm's centralization around Musk himself is a distinct and consequential finding. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] extends the sock-puppet audit methodology to TikTok, finding that non-partisan German users are disproportionately and quickly exposed to pro-AfD content, attributable to a supply-side abundance of viral and multiplier-driven content rather than overt platform favoritism. Read together with [[Gauthier2026-iq]] above, this cluster suggests a consistent empirical pattern: algorithms rarely *impose* ideology from nowhere, but they reliably reward attention-grabbing, often right-coded behavioral signals, which in turn reshapes the long-run information diet through persistence and virality rather than one-shot bias.

## Misinformation Exposure, Narrative Repurposing, and Visual/Multimodal Content

Several papers challenge the dominant "fake news domain list" paradigm for measuring misinformation exposure, arguing it radically undercounts real harm. [[Allen2024-av]] shows that factually accurate but "vaccine-skeptical" mainstream content had 46 times the aggregate persuasive impact of flagged misinformation, because reach swamped per-exposure potency. [[Goel2025-iq]] complements this by showing that mainstream articles co-shared with fake news disproportionately contain misinformation-adjacent narrative structures, implying that narrative repurposing—not just false claims—is a major vector. [[Yang2023-cg]] extends the critique to modality: image-based misinformation on Facebook is pervasive and starkly partisan-asymmetric, yet largely invisible to link-based measurement. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] generalizes this undercounting critique methodologically, proposing a counterfactual "embedded referrer" framework showing that platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure is 1.5–15x larger than direct-referral designs suggest, with sharply platform- and partisan-specific patterns (Twitter for Republicans, YouTube for Democrats). [[Ventura2025-sw]] pushes the boundary further into private messaging, using a WhatsApp deactivation experiment in Brazil to argue that misinformation research focused on public feeds misses an entire parallel ecosystem. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] offers a methodological corrective in the same spirit, proposing multi-level (story/outlet/content) cartography against oversimplified unidimensional partisan-sharing measures. Together these papers argue that both the measurement unit (domain vs. narrative vs. image vs. platform) and the exposure pathway (direct vs. embedded, public vs. private) have been too narrowly specified in prior work.

## Generative AI as Persuader, Launderer, and Ideological Signal

The most recent and rapidly growing thread turns the analytic lens on LLMs themselves—as persuasive agents, as carriers of embedded political bias, and as inference tools that can read ideology out of ordinary text. On persuasion, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish that conversational AI can match or exceed even expert human persuaders (professional canvassers, elite debaters), with information density/fact-checkable claims identified as the central mechanism—a finding replicated and extended by [[Lin2025-xp]], which demonstrates large real-election persuasion effects driven by evidence-based "central route" argumentation rather than manipulation, notably more effective on out-partisans than motivated-reasoning theories would predict. This raises an accuracy concern: Hackenburg2025-dj documents a direct persuasion-accuracy trade-off, and [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that disclosing persuasive *intent* (not mere AI identity, as EU AI Act Article 50 requires) is what actually blunts persuasive power—a regulatory-design finding with direct policy stakes.

A parallel set of papers treats LLMs as latent repositories and amplifiers of political bias rather than active persuaders. [[Waight2026-ts]] shows that state-coordinated media is memorized into LLM training data and causes measurably more pro-regime outputs in languages tied to low-media-freedom countries, effectively "laundering" state influence into seemingly neutral AI answers—a direct computational-era analogue to the media-capture dynamics documented by [[Balluff2026-bv]]. [[Lee2026-je]] demonstrates that LLMs can infer users' political alignment from ostensibly nonpolitical text with high accuracy, exploiting culturally politicized vocabulary, raising the micro-targeting and privacy concerns that [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] finds are otherwise empirically modest. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Much2026-gu]] extend the inquiry to new content forms and campaign strategies: AI-generated multimedia in far-right Instagram/X campaigning during the 2024 EU elections, and the use of "gendered media spaces" like entertainment podcasts as an ideological sorting and persuasion channel that helped widen the 2024 US youth gender gap. Taken together with the measurement papers above, this cluster suggests that the central methodological challenge of the field is converging: detecting ideological signal and influence operations that no longer look like crude bots or flagged falsehoods, but are instead embedded in model weights, narrative framing, gendered content ecosystems, and algorithmically rewarded behavioral patterns.

## Collective Action, Coordination, and the Boundaries of Political Communication

A final set of papers extends computational methods to the organizational and mobilizational dimensions of political communication that underlie advertising and media-capture dynamics. [[Bailard2024-pj]] uses Granger causality on fine-tuned frame classification to show how Proud Boys' online diagnostic and motivational framing feeds into offline violence in a reciprocal cycle, modeling extremist communication as an ecosystem rather than discrete moderatable posts. [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction" shows how intra-party factions use hyper-partisan media ties to gain outsized digital visibility and radicalize mainstream discourse, echoing the attention-reward dynamics found in the visibility-bias and algorithmic-amplification clusters above. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] documents bots functioning as agenda-builders against organizational campaign messaging, while [[Kim2026-br]] uncovers two decades of state-linked troll activity in Korean news comments, finding that morally condemning rhetoric—not overt promotion—is the most engagement-effective manipulation strategy. [[Richter2026-bt]] and [[Gaber2022-bk]] close the loop by situating these dynamics historically: Richter traces how stakeholder composition in AI discourse itself diverged between the US and Germany in ways that prefigure current regulatory asymmetries, while Gaber's conceptual treatment of "strategic lying" in Brexit and the 2019 UK election frames the entire computational turn as an intensification, not a rupture, of long-standing spin and agenda-setting tactics—reminding the collection that the platforms, algorithms, and models under study are new instruments for very old games of political influence.
