# Computational Studies of Political Media and Advertising

## The Measurement Problem: Scaling Ideology from Behavior

A foundational strand of this literature asks how to recover latent ideological positions from the digital traces political actors and audiences leave behind. [[Barbera2015-je]] establishes the template: Twitter "following" decisions function as costly signals that can be scaled, Bayesian-style, into ideal points comparable across legislators, elites, and ordinary citizens, validated against roll-call votes and expert surveys. [[Eady2025-vm]] extends this logic from following to *sharing*, arguing that URLs are a more platform-agnostic behavioral currency, and crucially shows that ideology estimated this way diverges meaningfully from roll-call-based measures (e.g., "Squad" members scoring far left of their centrist NOMINATE placement) — a reminder that different behavioral signals capture different facets of political identity. [[Lai2024-to]] pushes the scaling project into a new object entirely: video content itself, using Reddit cross-posting patterns to place YouTube videos on a latent ideological axis, extending ideal-point estimation from *actors* to *artifacts*. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] represents a methodological pivot within this same goal — rather than inferring ideology from network structure, it asks LLMs directly to locate texts on policy dimensions and averages the responses, suggesting generative models can substitute for the homophily-based inference that Barberá and Eady rely on. Together these papers trace an arc from network-structural measurement toward prompting-based measurement, while consistently converging on the idea that ideology is recoverable from incidental behavioral or textual residue rather than self-report.

## Platform Algorithms and the Limits of Attitude Change

A second cluster uses field experiments to adjudicate whether algorithmic curation itself drives polarization — a question the discipline has struggled to answer definitively. The Meta2020 collaboration papers ([[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Allcott2025-jb]]) form a null-result baseline: removing reshares, replacing algorithmic feeds with chronological ones, and removing political ads all substantially altered on-platform content exposure but produced no detectable shifts in polarization, knowledge, or turnout. [[Munger2025-cz]] offers a meta-scientific reading of exactly this body of work, arguing that such null findings reflect not the absence of platform effects but the structural incapacity of academic science to keep pace with rapidly mutating platforms — a critique of "temporal validity" that reframes the entire null-result literature as inconclusive rather than reassuring.

Later work complicates the null consensus by varying context and mechanism. [[Gauthier2026-iq]] finds that X's algorithmic feed *does* shift attitudes rightward relative to chronological feeds — but only in one direction, because algorithmic exposure induces persistent *following* changes that survive deactivation, reconciling the apparent tension with Meta's earlier nulls by locating the causal pathway in network restructuring rather than transient exposure. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] dissects a related mechanism on post-acquisition Twitter, showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic amplification is substantially explained by behavioral proxies (agitating content, proximity to Musk) rather than ideology per se, revealing perverse incentives toward controversy embedded in engagement optimization. [[Brady2026-ln]] intervenes directly on this incentive structure, designing a "diversified extremity" algorithm and showing that engagement-based ranking amplifies intergroup/moralized/emotional content and distorts perceived social norms — a norm-misperception pathway to partisan animosity that is independent of direct attitude change. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] narrows the causal target further, showing that suppressing elite-authored (and elite-referential) content specifically — rather than political content broadly — reduces misinformation susceptibility and low-credibility sharing, reframing misinformation as a problem of exposure *architecture* centered on elites rather than individual traits. [[Ventura2025-sw]] extends the deactivation paradigm beyond feed-based platforms entirely, using a WhatsApp field experiment in Brazil to argue that private messaging constitutes a distinct, understudied information ecosystem.

## Foreign Influence, Suppression, and Covert Advertising

A third thread uses quasi-experimental and survey-linked designs to assess the real-world consequence of covert or adversarial political advertising. [[Eady2023-xg]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] both examine the 2016 Russian influence campaign but reach starkly different conclusions by asking different questions: Eady et al. show that exposure to IRA content was dwarfed by domestic media exposure and concentrated among already-committed Republicans, implying minimal average persuasive effect; Kim et al., focusing specifically on *voter suppression* ads and geo-racial targeting, find that average-effect designs obscure substantively large turnout depression concentrated among the narrowly targeted subpopulation — explicitly reframing the earlier null literature as an artifact of aggregation rather than evidence of harmlessness. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] extends the auditing paradigm transnationally, using volunteer browser-plugin crowdsourcing to surface irregular Facebook electoral advertising in Brazil, while [[Votta2025-xz]] interrogates a subtler mechanism — how ad-delivery algorithms themselves (not just advertiser targeting) shape reach and cost in ways opaque to campaigns and regulators.

Covert influence over *coverage*, rather than direct ad exposure, is the focus of [[Balluff2026-bv]], which uses automated content analysis and difference-in-differences to detect media capture in the Austrian "Inseratenaffäre": government advertising bought not increased favorability but a "pay positive to go negative" dynamic in which a captured outlet boosted a patron's visibility while disparaging his rivals. This sits alongside [[Rohrbach2026-rc]], which examines how ordinary journalistic framing strategies — absent any corrupt transaction — can inadvertently seed conspiracy belief formation, and [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]], which frames micro-targeting, networked organizational blurring, and attention-amplification strategies as jointly undermining traditional regulatory and methodological tools, since amplification (unlike advertising) leaves no money trail to audit. [[Arora2025-tx]] contributes a complementary measurement innovation here, showing that textual frame-detection alone misses systematic multi-modal editorial choices that differentiate outlets by political leaning, relevant to any attempt to detect subtle advertiser-driven coverage shifts.

## Gendered and Comparative Media Environments

[[Much2026-gu]] extends the covert-influence logic to non-political entertainment media, showing that podcasts function as "gendered media spaces" where incidental ideological sorting occurs through masculine/feminine coding rather than partisan selection — a mechanism the Trump campaign is shown to have exploited strategically, targeting gender expression over ideology. [[Beacken2026-zb]] broadens the comparative lens geographically, arguing against techno-deterministic accounts of generative-AI propaganda by showing that adoption patterns across six democratically weakened countries are shaped by local structural conditions rather than following a uniform trajectory — a corrective that resonates with Waight et al.'s finding (below) that institutional context, not just technology, governs AI-mediated political influence.

## The AI Turn: Persuasion, Bias, and Covert Institutional Influence

The newest and most rapidly growing cluster asks how generative AI reconfigures the dynamics established above. On pure persuasive capacity, [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]] establish that conversational AI is a uniquely powerful persuader — exceeding even elite human debaters and professional canvassers — with information density (fact-checkable claim volume) as the dominant mechanism, a finding echoed in [[Lin2025-xp]]'s multi-election field experiments and extended to real-world behavior (charitable donation) and durable attitude change. [[Kotz2026-lk]] generalizes this capacity across structurally distinct contested issues (climate, vaccination, inequality), showing AI dialogue works best precisely among skeptics — the audience traditional persuasion struggles hardest to reach.

But this persuasive power is politically contingent and ethically fraught. [[DiGiuseppe2026-pu]] shows that merely *perceiving* an LLM as partisan-biased sharply attenuates its persuasiveness, operating through increased counterarguing rather than disengagement. [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] shows that transparency mandates modeled on EU law only work if they disclose *intent*, not mere AI identity — directly informing policy debates about covert AI-mediated influence analogous to the covert advertiser-influence problem documented in [[Balluff2026-bv]]. Most strikingly, [[Waight2026-ts]] closes the loop between media capture and generative AI by showing that state-coordinated media content is memorized into LLM training data and causally shapes model outputs toward pro-regime valence — an institutional "laundering" of media control into ostensibly neutral AI outputs that recasts the entire topic's central concern (covert advertiser/institutional influence over information environments) as a problem now embedded in the infrastructure of AI itself, rather than only in news coverage or ad delivery.
