# Social Media and Elections (International Cases)

## The Arc: From Detection to Consequence

The papers in this collection trace an arc that moves from *detecting* coordinated and manipulative activity, to *understanding its reach*, to *grappling with its political and democratic consequences* — all while pushing against a backdrop of a rapidly closing data-access regime. Four thematic lines organize this arc: (1) methodological innovation in detecting coordination, (2) the Brazilian post-Bolsonaro case as a sustained laboratory, (3) comparative European and global cases, and (4) the meta-level crisis of researcher access itself.

### Detecting Coordination: From Binary Bots to Narrative Brokerage

A methodological throughline begins with [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] and [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], whose coordinated-link-sharing-behavior framework reoriented disinformation research away from content/actor veracity toward *ecological, action-based* detection — a move extended by [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]]'s workflow for continuously updating coordination lists as actors adapt. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] and [[Keller2019-nk]] push this further by showing that coordination is a continuum, not a binary, and that it is largely orthogonal to automation — the South Korean NIS astroturfing case and the UK 2019 election both reveal that human-coordinated "cyborg" campaigns leave behavioral traces (temporal clustering, principal-agent shirking) more diagnostic than bot signatures. This logic travels into closed platforms with [[Kansaon2025-id]], which adapts Twitter-style "Rapid Spread Network" detection to 13 million WhatsApp messages in Brazil, finding coordinated accounts fueling real-world mobilization (the January 8 attacks). [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] extend coordination detection into explicitly authoritarian settings (Russia/Belarus Telegram, China's COVID protests), while [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] complicates the paradigm by arguing Serbia's SNS has evolved *beyond* detectable bot-farm signatures into convincing grassroots mimicry — suggesting the detection arms race is far from settled. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s CANE framework represents the furthest methodological advance, modeling users via shared narrative participation rather than platform-specific interaction signals to find small sets of "bridge users" who seed cross-platform narrative migration between Truth Social and X.

Running parallel to detection is a reflexive strand on whether detected coordination actually *matters*. [[Eady2023-xg]] and [[Appel2026-qr]], both working with rare platform-provided exposure data, arrive at strikingly deflationary conclusions: IRA exposure in 2016 was concentrated among already-committed partisans and showed no measurable attitudinal effect, and Meta's 49 deceptive networks reached millions but their correlational links to discernment and polarization evaporate under covariate adjustment. Against this, [[Kim2026-wg]] insists that average treatment effects obscure real harm — geo-racially targeted voter-suppression ads in 2016 did measurably depress turnout among their intended targets, a methodological rebuke to null-finding studies that rely on aggregate exposure. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] adds scale to this debate, mapping the genuinely distinct diffusion dynamics of misinformation across a billion Facebook reshares.

### Brazil as Sustained Case: Coordination, Narrative, and Democratic Erosion

Brazil's post-Bolsonaro trajectory anchors much of this collection empirically. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] examines how parliamentarians contested epistemic authority with journalists during the Manaus COVID collapse, identifying three legitimation strategies (derivative reporting, authority claims, brokerage) that recur across the corpus. [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] extends this to show that platform incentives structurally reward antagonism ("lacração") over substantive congressional communication — a "democratic cost" of virality-seeking that generalizes mediatization theory beyond the Global North. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]], using the same three-wave panel, show electoral misinformation beliefs corroding both political tolerance and trust in election results, with WhatsApp news use operating as an indirect amplifier — complicating the null results found for Russian interference abroad by showing domestically-rooted misinformation has real attitudinal traction. [[Marino2026-slef]] challenges the static echo-chamber assumption by tracking volatile affective engagement in pro-Bolsonaro Facebook networks around the January 2023 coup attempt, while [[Ventura2025-sw]] uses a WhatsApp deactivation experiment to causally isolate messaging-app effects on misinformation exposure. [[Ventura2026-yc]] closes this arc with the 2024 X ban, showing that state-imposed platform bans produce durable rightward "sorting ratchets" — a finding that resonates with [[Simeone2025-vo]]'s US-based demonstration that targeted deplatforming can permanently flip a community's authority structure, suggesting democratic interventions in information ecosystems have asymmetric, lasting partisan consequences regardless of hemisphere. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] rounds out the Brazilian material with an early (2018) crowdsourced ad-auditing effort that prefigures later regulatory demands for transparency.

### Comparative and Transnational Cases: Germany, Latin America, and Beyond

Germany and the broader European arena provide comparative ballast. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] and [[Schulte2026-df]] both target German federal elections (2021 and 2025 respectively), with the latter reframing the entire enterprise as an *infrastructure* problem — parties strategically differentiate by platform (AfD on TikTok, BSW on X), and multimodal signals (text, voice, face) diverge, meaning single-platform or single-modality analysis is structurally insufficient. This infrastructural critique dovetails with [[Darius2026-xl]], [[Iris2026-pg]], and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]], which collectively establish that radical-right and populist parties enjoy disproportionate visibility and audience support across European platforms — not chiefly through higher posting volume but through emotionally resonant, anti-elitist content that platforms (especially TikTok) reward algorithmically. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country tweet analysis supplies the elite-level mechanism: misinformation sharing is driven specifically by the *interaction* of populism and right-wing ideology, not either alone, linking directly to [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]]'s account of AI-generated multimedia in far-right 2024 campaigns and [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s comparative study of generative-AI propaganda across six democratically weakened states (including Bolivia). [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] offers a Scandinavian counterpoint, showing populist parties' advantage in eliciting shareable "Angry" reactions even in less-polarized multiparty systems. Beyond Europe, [[Gaw2025-ru]]'s brokerage framework for the 2022 Philippine election and [[Wack2026-bt]]'s Kenya deepfake experiment extend the volume's geographic reach, with the latter's "collective sensemaking" finding — that audience commentary, not the synthetic artifact itself, determines a deepfake's persuasive power — a crucial corrective to techno-deterministic alarm. [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade Korean troll analysis and [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s UK survey experiment add further nuance: the former shows moral condemnation outperforms direct propaganda as an engagement strategy, while the latter demonstrates that mere *discourse about* interference — regardless of its real effectiveness — can itself deepen affective polarization, a sobering coda to the volume's recurring null-effects findings. [[Heft2021-ky]]'s hyperlink analysis of transnational right-wing media ecologies and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]]'s Navalny/pro-government coordination study broaden the authoritarian-context comparisons introduced earlier.

### The Governance and Access Crisis

A final cluster steps back from specific elections to interrogate the conditions of possibility for this research. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] periodizes platform election-policy evolution (protection → civic integrity → backlash), showing how election governance is reactive to US cycles and vulnerable to ownership-driven reversal. [[Philipp2026-tl]] and [[Lukito2026-nb]] both diagnose the structural "price, proficiency, or permission" barriers reshaping what election research is even feasible under the DSA and post-API conditions — a concern that [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] both operationalize as methodological workarounds. Together, these pieces suggest that the central finding of this entire literature may be less about any single platform's manipulation than about the fragile, politically contingent infrastructure through which manipulation, its detection, and its consequences can be observed at all.
