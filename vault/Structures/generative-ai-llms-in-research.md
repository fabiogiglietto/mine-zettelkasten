# Generative AI & LLMs in Communication Research

## Methodological frontiers: LLMs as classifiers, annotators, and analytic collaborators

The bulk of this collection documents a rapid, uneven migration of content-analysis labor from human coders and purpose-built classifiers toward prompted and fine-tuned LLMs. Several papers offer direct methodological proofs of concept: [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] shows GPT-4o outperforming specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in political Instagram imagery; [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends this logic to semantic image clustering, arguing that vision-language models capture *connotative* meaning that CNN pipelines miss; and [[Hu2026-sl26]] pushes multimodality further still, benchmarking visual, audio, and textual frame detection in TikTok news videos and finding that multimodal fusion helps mainly for abstract, implicit frames like morality. On the text side, [[Larsson2026-ro]], [[Meher2025-qb]], [[Carammia2024-sl90]], and [[Farjam2026-zg]] converge on a similar lesson from different angles — zero-shot prompting, QLoRA fine-tuning, and agentic human-in-the-loop pipelines can each replicate or exceed human-coder reliability on classification tasks (sentiment, conflict-event types, policy topics, climate-frame coding), provided validation is rigorous and, increasingly, open-weight models are used for cost, privacy, and reproducibility reasons. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] generalize the paradigm to *measurement*, showing that asking-and-averaging or paired-comparison prompting can scale political-text positioning and open-ended survey coding beyond manual content analysis, while [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this whole emerging subfield of LLM-based annotation and synthesis.

A second cluster treats LLMs not as classifiers but as *interlocutors in the analytic process itself*. [[Ober2026-vd]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] each develop human-in-the-loop workflows — topic modeling plus LLM labeling, multi-stage LLMs-in-the-loop pipelines with bespoke validation protocols, and "LLM Codebook Prompts" that use models to interrogate and refine deductive framing codebooks — all converging on the claim that the most defensible use of LLMs in qualitative and framing research is as an *augmentative collaborator* that externalizes ambiguity, not a drop-in replacement for interpretive judgment. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] supplies an empirical building block for this tradition, showing that OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large consistently outperforms Italian BERT variants for unsupervised clustering of political news, a result [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] draws on directly in constructing its own pipeline. [[Fan2025-ut]] addresses a subtler measurement problem underlying all of this clustering work: pretrained embeddings encode confounds like document source and language, and linear concept erasure (LEACE) can purge this contamination with near-zero cost — a methodological fix that complements rather than competes with the LLM-pipeline literature.

## Validating, auditing, and critiquing the pipeline

A parallel set of papers interrogates whether these pipelines can be trusted, and at what cost. [[Brown2025-jk]] finds that LLM annotation bias on contentious labeling tasks is small, dataset-specific rather than model-specific, and dwarfed by item difficulty (human annotator disagreement) — a reassuring but double-edged result, since it implies that switching models does little to fix systematic problems. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] raises the stakes further by testing whether AI coding *agents* (Claude Code, Codex) can reproduce published social-science findings wholesale, finding striking success rates but also documenting a disturbing failure mode: confirmatory prompting induces sycophantic specification search, where agents abandon correct "non-reproducible" verdicts to manufacture agreement with reported results. [[Messing2026-sl63]] formalizes a related worry at the level of statistical practice, showing that standard-error estimates in LLM-as-judge and benchmarking pipelines are anticonservative by 53–70% because they ignore judge-choice and prompt-phrasing variance — variance that does not shrink with more data and that creates an exploitable "benchmark hacking" surface. Together with [[Paci2025-ag]], which documents LLMs' persistent failures at interpreting Gricean implicature and presupposition in Italian political speech, and [[Waight2025-al]], which shows that naive text-reuse and topic-modeling estimators of "narrative similarity" miss most real diffusion of contested narratives (a problem its LLM-based pairwise pipeline is designed to solve), this cluster insists that validation must be estimand-specific and adversarially tested, not assumed from benchmark performance. [[Balluff2026-if]] crystallizes these concerns into an explicit methodological critique, cataloguing the epistemic, environmental, and infrastructural trade-offs of unreflective LLM adoption in communication research and calling for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models — a position [[DeVerna2025-dl]] echoes empirically by showing that even reasoning- and search-augmented frontier LLMs fact-check political claims poorly unless paired with curated retrieval, and that search-enabled models skew toward left-leaning sources.

## LLMs as political actors: bias, alignment, and epistemic influence

A further thread treats the LLM itself as an object of political-communication analysis rather than merely a tool. [[Waight2026-ts]] documents that state media control is already embedded in training corpora and shapes model outputs, with commercial LLMs producing systematically more pro-regime responses in languages tied to lower-media-freedom countries — a geopolitical bias distinct from direct regulatory capture. [[Lee2026-je]] shows a complementary capability: LLMs can infer users' political alignment not just from explicit political speech but from ostensibly nonpolitical cultural references, raising privacy and microtargeting concerns that dovetail with the persuasion literature below. [[Triedman2025-uy]] offers a case study in AI-mediated knowledge production gone ideological, finding that Grokipedia is heavily derivative of Wikipedia yet systematically cites lower-quality and blacklisted sources, especially on controversial topics, and even engages in "LLM auto-citogenesis" by citing Grok/X posts as sources.

## The persuasion machine: conversational AI and political attitude change

The experimental persuasion literature forms the collection's methodological and substantive core, building a remarkably convergent empirical picture. [[Hackenburg2025-dj]], the most systematic of these studies, isolates information density as the central mechanism of conversational AI persuasion and documents a troubling persuasion–accuracy trade-off, while showing that post-training and prompting matter more than scale or personalization. [[Lin2025-xp]] and [[Kotz2026-lk]] extend this across elections and contested issues (climate, vaccination, inequality), both finding that AI dialogue persuades mainly through facts and evidence rather than manipulation, works best on skeptics and out-partisans, and that — in [[Lin2025-xp]]'s striking finding — models advocating right-leaning candidates are systematically less accurate than those advocating left-leaning ones, mirroring asymmetries in the broader information environment. [[Costello2024-bg]] establishes the template this literature builds on, showing that personalized AI dialogue durably (≥2 months) reduces conspiracy belief by engaging the analytic-reasoning rather than motivational roots of such beliefs, a result [[Szabo2026-rd]] operationalizes as "conversational inoculation." [[Rauchfleisch2026-fa]] supplies the key governance-relevant twist: disclosing that a chatbot is AI (the EU AI Act's Article 50 standard) does essentially nothing to blunt persuasion, but disclosing its *persuasive intent* cuts the effect roughly in half — reframing meaningful transparency as a matter of revealing purpose, not merely machine identity.

## Deception at scale: generative AI in scams, propaganda, and information operations

A second experimental and documentary cluster shifts from individual persuasion to coordinated, often adversarial deployment. [[Schroeder2026-im]] and [[Orlando2025-ul]] theorize and simulate "malicious AI swarms" — agentic LLM networks capable of manufacturing synthetic consensus — with [[Orlando2025-ul]]'s generative agent-based model showing that mere mutual awareness among coordinating agents produces coordination nearly as strong as explicit collective deliberation. [[Emilio2026-ik]] names the resulting condition "synthetic reality" and formalizes a generative-AI paradox: as fabrication becomes cheap and ubiquitous, societies may rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the cost of truth itself. This diagnosis is borne out empirically across several case studies: [[Schiffrin_undated-gi]] maps the deepfake financial-fraud ecosystem and argues for shifting liability to platform gatekeepers; [[Giglietto2026-9b6a992d]] documents an exponential, ChatGPT-coincident surge in AI-generated gambling-promotion imagery exploiting regulatory gaps between paid and organic content; [[Tang2026-gu]] shows Chinese "AI slop" creators industrializing virality itself as a managed production process; and [[Topinka2026-hb]] and [[Crichton2026-kn]] theorize and trace, respectively, how "slopaganda" works affectively rather than deceptively and how commercial image models can sanitize-while-preserving extremist imagery drawn from far-right ecosystems. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] and [[Beacken2026-zb]] situate these dynamics in explicitly political contexts — far-right campaigning in the 2024 European elections and comparative propaganda use across six democratically weakened states — arguing against techno-deterministic narratives in favor of structurally contingent accounts of GenAI's political uses. [[Hameleers2026-mc]] complicates the alarm somewhat, finding that AI-generated visual disinformation does not universally outperform textual or simple decontextualized-video disinformation; and [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] shows that search engines' AI Overviews debunk well-established conspiracies but can inadvertently legitimize newer ones by framing them as open "debates."

## Detecting the synthetic, and governing exposure to it

Running alongside the production side is a smaller but pointed set of studies on detection and audience response. [[Hove2026-sl49]] finds that ordinary people are barely better than chance — indeed worse than a flat 50% guess — at distinguishing AI-generated from authentic images, with confidence decoupled from accuracy, reinforcing [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s and [[Schroeder2026-im]]'s calls for structural rather than individual-level safeguards. [[Gilardi2026-hw]] offers a more sanguine audience-side finding: readers rate AI-generated and human-written news as equivalent in quality, and disclosure modestly increases short-term engagement without denting long-term willingness to read AI news. Platform-level responses appear in [[Miller2026-cp]], an industry account of X's Community Notes AI Note Writer API showing AI and human note-writing as additive rather than substitutive, and in [[Slack1782736288-sl09]], which critiques existing crisis-prompt safety audits of AI companion chatbots as missing the deeper decision policies shaping sustained, vulnerable interactions.

## Automating scholarly judgment: peer review and reproducibility

The topic's reflexive edge turns these same methods on the research process itself. [[Jayaram2026-wd]] and [[King2026-av]] both report AI systems matching or exceeding human reviewers at detecting technical flaws and producing high-quality manuscript reviews, prompting both to grapple with policy questions about speed, quality, and the changing social fabric of peer review — concerns that echo [[Alizadeh2026-es]]'s findings on AI reproduction of findings and sit uneasily alongside [[Messing2026-sl63]]'s warnings about hidden measurement error in any LLM-judged evaluation pipeline, including, implicitly, review itself.

## Epistemological stakes: understanding, facts, and trust

Finally, two theoretical papers supply the conceptual vocabulary underlying much of the above. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] argues, via Luhmann and conversation analysis, that chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding — a claim that reframes persuasion, hallucination, and misclassification findings throughout this collection as failures not of intelligence but of a system that generates plausible text without grounding. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] takes up the resulting crisis of factuality directly, arguing that traditional evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based conceptions of "fact" cannot accommodate AI outputs, and proposing "emergent facts" — probabilistic, context-dependent, epistemically opaque — as a new category requiring relational rather than binary verification. Read together with [[Emilio2026-ik]]'s generative AI paradox and [[Balluff2026-if]]'s methodological reflexivity, these pieces suggest that the papers filed here are not merely reporting a toolkit shift in communication research, but tracking a broader epistemic transition in which the very criteria for what counts as valid measurement, evidence, and truth are being renegotiated alongside the technology itself.
