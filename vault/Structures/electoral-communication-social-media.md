# Elections & Social Media Communication

## From second screens to coordinated networks: the Italian core

The program's longue durée begins well before "disinformation" became a keyword. [[Giglietto2012-32b6b551]] already asked, in the skeptical empirical mode that recurs throughout the corpus, whether Facebook "Likes" could forecast the 2011 Italian administrative elections — and found they largely could not, a early caution against treating platform metrics as proxies for political reality. [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] extended the same skepticism to Twitter-as-second-screen during the 2013 campaign, showing that audience "participation" around televised talk shows was narrow, dominated by opinion and interaction requests rather than agenda-building, and still firmly subordinated to television's rhythms — an early empirical instantiation of the "hybrid media system" theorized contemporaneously by [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]], whose vocabulary of interdependence, political information cycles, and adaptive power relations underwrites nearly every later paper in this collection.

The methodological spine of the MINE program proper crystallizes around the 2018–2022 Italian election cycle. [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]] establishes the descriptive baseline — populist parties' news ecosystems are more "insular," and insularity predicts amplification (shares) over contestation (comments) — before [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] pivots the whole research agenda from content- and actor-based disinformation detection toward *coordinated link sharing behavior* as a behavioral, ecological signature of manipulation. That shift becomes the program's signature method: [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]] operationalizes it as a living workflow for the 2022 snap election, iteratively surfacing new coordinated accounts (a hyperpartisan M5S network, a religious clickbait operation, a proselytizing bot network) rather than relying on static lists. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] then documents the next methodological turn — folding LLMs into the pipeline for classification, embedding, and narrative labeling across both the 2018 and 2022 corpora — while confronting the validation problems that come with "LLMs-in-the-loop" research. The arc culminates in two papers that turn the lens onto the platform itself: [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] uses breakpoint detection to show that Meta's political-content-reduction policy throttled Italian MPs' reach months before its announced rollout and disproportionately spared extremist accounts, and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] (on US data, but methodologically continuous) demonstrates that Facebook is an "active curator" whose share-to-view amplification shifts in lockstep with governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] supplies the connective theoretical essay, naming networked organizational blur, microtargeting, and attention amplification as the three vectors big data has introduced into political communication — a framework the empirical papers above each instantiate.

## Exporting coordination detection: UK, Brazil, Romania

The coordination-detection toolkit migrates outward from Italy. [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] reframes coordination as a continuum rather than a binary for the 2019 UK election, showing that the most tightly coordinated clusters were small activist and antagonist groups, not mainstream parties, and that coordination is largely orthogonal to automation — a finding that complicates any simple "bots did it" story later echoed in [[Arceneaux2026-xk]]'s study of social bots as agenda-builders in the 2022 Ohio midterms. In Brazil, [[Kansaon2025-id]] applies a "Rapid Spread Network" adaptation of this logic to 13 million WhatsApp messages, tracing coordinated accounts from misinformation dissemination to real-world mobilization around the January 8, 2023 attacks, while [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] shows an earlier, lower-tech instantiation of the same impulse — volunteer browser-plugin auditing of irregular Facebook ads in the 2018 Brazilian election. [[Oprea2025-lf]] carries the inauthenticity lens to Romania's 2024 EU campaign, quantifying "hyperactive user" shares across party pages and indicting Meta's policy-practice gap. Together these papers establish coordinated/inauthentic behavior detection as a genuinely comparative method rather than an Italian idiosyncrasy — even as [[Appel2026-qr]]'s massive FIES analysis of 49 Meta-identified networks around the US 2020 election complicates the picture by showing that most exposure to such networks occurs *indirectly*, through reshares by ordinary unaffiliated users, and that platform/survey-linked causal estimates of their effects evaporate once pre-exposure characteristics are controlled.

## Brazil as an expanding site of inquiry

Brazil accumulates its own dense sub-literature within the program, moving from coordination (above) to the downstream political-psychological and normative consequences of a saturated misinformation environment. [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] form a matched pair drawing on the same three-wave panel: the former shows belief in electoral fraud claims predicting *declining political tolerance*, especially via messaging-app news use, while the latter shows the same misinformation beliefs eroding *trust in election results* even as mainstream news consumption acts as a stabilizing counterweight — together reframing misinformation's harm as corrosion of democratic norms rather than mere factual confusion. [[Marino2026-slef]] and [[Rodarte2026-dk]] push into the affective and discursive texture of Bolsonaro-era polarization: the former finds hyperpartisan Facebook communities to be far less stable echo chambers than assumed, with engagement dynamics reorganizing sharply around the January 2023 coup attempt; the latter shows parliamentarians on both sides contesting journalistic *epistemic authority* itself during the Manaus COVID crisis, with government critics mimicking news formats and Bolsonaro allies severing ties with the press altogether. [[Rodarte2026-sl16]] closes the loop institutionally, showing via interviews and 4.5 million posts that Brazilian legislators believe (correctly, per the engagement models) that covering legislative work is penalized while antagonism is rewarded — a "democratic cost of virality" that generalizes the program's platform-incentive argument beyond disinformation per se. [[Ventura2026-yc]] extends the Brazilian arc into platform governance proper, showing that the 2024 X ban produced a durable rightward "sorting ratchet" in the platform's political composition, a finding that resonates with [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s broader claim that platform election policy is reactive and unstable rather than principled.

## Germany: multimodal and infrastructural challenges

The German strand is comparatively compact but methodologically ambitious, tracking the shift from Facebook/Twitter-centric analysis toward visual and multi-platform methods. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] validates multimodal LLMs (GPT-4o) against specialized computer-vision tools for analyzing candidate visibility in 2021 Instagram campaign content, finding striking front-runner personalization differences between candidate and party accounts. [[Slack1783507532-sl10]] examines political advertising and coordinated behavior ahead of the same 2021 election. By 2025, [[Schulte2026-df]] reframes the entire German case as an infrastructure problem: collecting 125,000+ posts across X and TikTok reveals not a lack of findings but a lack of *reusable research infrastructure* for multi-platform observability — an argument that dovetails with the governance-and-access cluster below. [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]]'s sock-puppet audit of TikTok closes the arc by showing non-partisan German users are incidentally over-exposed to pro-AfD content, driven by supply-side volume and virality among unofficial "multiplier" accounts rather than overt platform favoritism — a finding that parallels [[Darius2026-xl]]'s cross-platform, 27-country finding that populist radical right parties enjoy disproportionate *audience support* (not just activity) across the EU, especially on TikTok.

## The 2024 European Parliament elections as a convergence point

A cluster of papers converges on the 2024 EP elections as a natural experiment in radical-right visibility across media and platforms. [[Iris2026-pg]] documents disproportionate *news* coverage of the Radical Right across five countries relative to polling and electoral benchmarks, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks — directly undercutting radical-right "censorship" narratives. [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] shows the Sweden Democrats enacting visual "securitising discourse" differently per platform, concentrating memetic, affectively charged content (often using non-Swedish footage) on TikTok to reach younger voters. [[Darius2026-xl]] and [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]] supply the quantitative backbone for why this strategy works — audience support, not mere posting volume, drives the PRR advantage, concentrated on TikTok. [[Perez-Curiel2026-ld]] extends the visual-strategy question into generative AI, examining far-right deployment of AI-generated multimedia on Instagram and X. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] closes this cluster from the demand side, linking far-right electoral performance to voters' heightened disinformation concerns — a reminder, echoed in [[Karlsson2026-hd]]'s UK survey experiment on interference narratives, that *discourse about* manipulation can itself be a polarizing force independent of manipulation's actual efficacy. [[Jurg2025-ur]] audits the supply side of platform governance during the same election, finding YouTube's "authoritative source" ranking inconsistently labeled and its removal communications opaque — a direct empirical complement to the regulatory-access literature.

## The US collaborative-research counterpoint

A substantial body of large-N, often platform-partnered US studies (2016–2024) functions in this corpus as both methodological benchmark and empirical counterweight to the program's coordination-and-visibility findings. The 2016 fake-news literature — [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], and the synthesizing [[Tsfati2020-uo]] — converges on a "concentrated minority" thesis: exposure and sharing of fake news were real but heavily concentrated among older, more conservative, highly engaged users, while most political information still flowed through mainstream outlets, with [[Tsfati2020-uo]] adding the crucial twist that mainstream *coverage* of fake news may disseminate it more widely than the fake sites themselves. The 2020 Meta-partnered studies — [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Allcott2025-jb]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] — largely replicate this null/limited-effects pattern for algorithmic feeds and political advertising, a pattern that [[Gauthier2026-iq]] directly contests: its 2023 X field experiment finds the algorithmic feed *does* shift users rightward, reconciling the earlier null results by identifying a persistence mechanism (algorithm-induced following behavior that outlasts the algorithm itself). [[Kim2026-wg]] complicates the "small effects" consensus from another angle, showing that average-effect designs obscure large, geo-racially targeted turnout suppression in 2016. [[Much2026-gu]] extends the US arc into 2024, introducing "gendered media spaces" — podcasts — as a novel, non-ideological sorting mechanism behind Trump's gains among young men. Read against the MINE program's Italian/Brazilian coordination work, this cluster suggests a methodological fault line: platform-partnered individual-exposure designs tend to find null or modest effects, while independent, network- and reach-based designs (the program's own signature) more often find structural, concentrated, platform-mediated effects — a tension [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] addresses head-on by arguing that treating governance interventions as stable background (rather than dynamic objects of study) is precisely what produces these divergent conclusions.

## Narrative, rumor, and the grammar of election misinformation

A parallel strand treats election misinformation not as discrete false claims but as collective narrative performance. [[Prochaska2025-ef]]'s "deep storytelling" framework shows how 2020–2022 US election rumors are sustained through elite-influencer-audience collaboration in which *how* a story is told matters as much as its content; [[Prochaska2026-pr]] formalizes this into a cross-cycle "grammar" of 568 rumors (Actor–Object–Action, with optional Scene and Remedy), arguing most election rumors are structurally recombinant rather than novel. [[Gaber2022-bk]] offers a UK-focused companion concept, "strategic lying," theorizing Brexit- and 2019-election-era falsehoods as an intensification of established PR tactics rather than a post-truth rupture — notably arguing that *rebuttal itself amplifies* the lie, a mechanism resonant with [[Tsfati2020-uo]]'s mainstream-amplification argument. [[Tai2026-qk]] shifts attention to elite supply, asking how institutional and ideological position condition elected officials' own misinformation sharing, while [[Lukito2026-il]] examines how right-leaning US outlets curate distinct content packages across Twitter and Truth Social during the 2022 midterms — both studies extending the program's concern with strategic communicative adaptation to platform-specific audiences and incentives.

## Data access, platform governance, and the DSA horizon

The collection's final, increasingly self-reflexive cluster turns to the infrastructural conditions of election research itself, largely catalyzed by the EU Digital Services Act. [[Philipp2026-tl]] surveys the new DSA-mandated APIs and portals and their uneven implementation; [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes the resulting landscape of 72 access tools around a "price, proficiency, or permission" framework, diagnosing a severe imbalance between production- and consumption-side data and a WEIRD-centric research infrastructure; [[de-Vreese2026-zx]] argues that current platform resistance to Article 40 should be read as evidence the regulation is finally biting rather than as regulatory failure. [[McNally2025-dn]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] supply concrete audits within this new regime — the former showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm leaves detectable, lagged fingerprints on hard-news engagement over a decade, the latter showing X's Community Notes bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing (including election-related) content by design. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s policy-genealogy of five platforms' election rules — moving from narrow protection (2018–2020) through "civic integrity" expansion (2020–2022) to recent rollback — provides the regulatory backdrop against which [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]]'s Italian reach-suppression findings and [[Schulte2026-df]]'s infrastructural diagnosis for Germany's 2025 election should both be read: not as isolated national case studies, but as data points in a single, ongoing argument the MINE program has been building since 2012 — that electoral communication on social media cannot be understood independently of the platforms' own shifting, often undisclosed governance of visibility.
