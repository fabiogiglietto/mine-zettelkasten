# Social Media Audience Engagement

## From imagined audiences to social TV

The strand's deepest roots lie in theorising what an "audience" even is once platforms mediate the relationship between speaker and public. [[Marwick2011-sl02]] supplies the conceptual scaffolding still implicit in much later work: Twitter users address a "networked audience" they cannot see, managing context collapse through imagined readers, self-censorship, and micro-celebrity branding. This framing—audiences as constructed, performed-for entities rather than pre-given demographics—anticipates a recurring worry across the collection: that "engagement" metrics (likes, reactions, shares) are traces of strategic self-presentation as much as of authentic response.

[[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] operationalises this for the classic "social TV" case, tracking millions of tweets accompanying Italian talk shows during the 2013 election. It finds that second-screen participation, though voluminous, was narrow in reach and dominated by opinion/comment rather than agenda-shaping contribution—audiences talking *about* television more than talking back *to* it. This early result already flags a tension that resurfaces throughout the topic: high volume of platform activity does not equate to meaningful influence over institutional actors, whether broadcasters, politicians, or (later) platforms themselves. [[Farci2017-2a88c00e]] extends the audience-as-performance lens to everyday sociality, showing how Italian Facebook users build "networked intimacy" through tagging, implicit sharing, and above all "liking" as a low-cost relational signal—work that quietly prefigures the entire reactions-as-affect literature by treating a single click as a meaningful communicative act embedded in an imagined community of readers.

## Reactions, emotion, and the metrication of engagement

A second cluster picks up precisely where Farci leaves off, once Facebook formalised affect into the Reactions button. [[Anwar2024-34dba628]] synthesises this now-substantial literature, showing that reactions function as "paralinguistic digital affordances" whose distribution differs systematically by content type: lifestyle content skews positive, political content skews negative and heterogeneous, and anger reliably outperforms other emotions in driving further engagement. [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] supplies a fresh comparative test of this claim across Norwegian, Swedish, and Danish elections, confirming that populist parties are particularly adept at eliciting "Angry" reactions and that anger—unlike "Love"—positively predicts sharing, while also showing national variation (Sweden's more even emotional balance, Denmark's outsized "Love" spikes) that complicates any single Scandinavian model of affective politics. [[Xue2025-bp]] pushes the emotion-engagement nexus into an unexpected genre—fact-checking—showing that even ostensibly dispassionate corrective content is often emotionally charged, and that this emotionality shapes both engagement and audience sentiment toward the fact-checked target. Together these three papers trace a throughline from taxonomy (Anwar) to comparative political application (Kalsnes) to genre-bending extension (Xue), all converging on the same finding: engagement metrics are not neutral counts but affectively loaded signals that reward negativity and confrontation.

[[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] offers a complementary, non-quantitative case of affectively charged audience engagement: the #JeNeSuisPasCharlie counter-hashtag as a site of dissent within a moment of enforced solidarity. Read alongside the reactions literature, it suggests that audience "engagement" with institutional or collective narratives is rarely monolithic—dissenting minorities construct discursive strategies analogous to the negative-reaction dynamics quantified elsewhere, even where no emotion button exists to record them.

## Citizens, institutions, and information gatekeeping

A third thread turns from political parties to institutional and civic information campaigns, asking how citizen engagement is shaped by gatekeepers upstream of the platform. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] examines how Google News Italia's aggregation choices structure which health topics reach Facebook audiences, finding that engagement is driven less by source credibility than by topical "popularity"—celebrity health stories generate outsized comment volumes compared to technical medical-science content. This resonates with the reactions literature's emotion-engagement coupling but shifts the frame from politics to public health, illustrating that the "engagement rewards negativity/emotionality" pattern generalises beyond electoral communication into everyday institutional information flows—precisely the kind of citizen-engagement-with-campaigns concern this topic aims to track over time.

## Exposure, curation, and the architecture of circulation

A more methodologically self-conscious cluster interrogates *how* content reaches audiences in the first place, pushing back against simple narratives of algorithmic manipulation. [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s landmark Facebook study remains a touchstone: friend networks are ideologically homophilous but contain substantial cross-cutting ties, and individual clicking behaviour narrows exposure to diverse content more than the News Feed algorithm does. [[Allcott2025-jb]] extends this experimental tradition into the 2020 US election, finding that removing political ads from feeds for six weeks produced no detectable attitudinal effects—a null result that, like Bakshy's, complicates alarmist accounts of platform-driven polarization. [[Green2025-ap]] and [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] both critique the methodological apparatus underlying such debates: Green shows that domain-level partisanship scores mask enormous story-level heterogeneity, meaning audiences "unbundle" and "re-bundle" content into partisan curation bubbles invisible to source-based measures, while Gaisbauer proposes a multi-level "political cartography" (story, outlet, content) to correct the same blind spot. Read together, this cluster marks a maturation of the field from broad claims about filter bubbles and echo chambers toward increasingly granular, causally rigorous, and self-critical measurement of what "exposure" and "engagement" actually capture.

## Platforms in flux: migration, creator labour, and audience-centred concepts

The most recent papers turn to how the audience-engagement paradigm itself is being reshaped by platform instability and creator economies. [[Wang2026-ub]] documents the failed migration of academic Twitter users to Mastodon after Musk's acquisition, showing that even highly motivated, well-connected communities struggle to sustain engagement outside dominant platforms—a reminder that "audience" is partly an artifact of platform lock-in and network effects rather than pure content quality. [[Volpe2026-um]] examines the underside of engagement metrics from the producer's side: Italian micro-influencers performing care and expertise around second-hand fashion not purely from conviction but as a survival strategy against algorithmic demands for constant, emotionally resonant output—an update of Marwick and boyd's micro-celebrity branding for the TikTok/Instagram era of platformised precarity. Finally, [[Wedel2026-ix]] returns to the audience-reception question posed at the outset by Marwick, but recasts it for young adults navigating YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok: rather than institutional "news," audiences decode "informativeness" through relevance, perceived expertise, and platform-specific cues, regardless of professional journalistic credentials.

Taken as a whole, the topic traces a coherent arc: from theorising the imagined, performed audience (Marwick) and its early empirical instantiation in social-TV second screens (Iannelli) and everyday intimacy practices (Farci), through the formalisation of affect into engagement metrics (Anwar, Kalsnes, Xue, Giglietto), into institutional and health-information contexts (Ducci), toward increasingly sophisticated and self-critical measurement of exposure and circulation (Bakshy, Allcott, Green, Gaisbauer), and finally into the contemporary moment where platform fragility (Wang), creator labour (Volpe), and audience-centred reception concepts (Wedel) demand that "engagement" be theorised anew for a post-Twitter, algorithmically saturated media ecology.
