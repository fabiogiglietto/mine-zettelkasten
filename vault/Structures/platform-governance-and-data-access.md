# Platform Governance & Research Data Access

## Platform Governance & Research Data Access

### From the Wild West to the walled garden: a history of contraction

The arc of this literature begins with loss. [[Freelon2018-ao]] names the "post-API age" at the moment Facebook's 2018 Pages API closure eliminated TOS-compliant data collection, and [[Bruns2019-nr]] diagnoses the same moment as an "APIcalypse" in which platforms instrumentalized the Cambridge Analytica scandal to frustrate critical scholarship rather than protect privacy. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] extend this into a fuller periodization — from laissez-faire APIs, through a brief "Golden Age" of academic access, into today's bifurcated regime of commercial paywalls and regulatory "walled gardens." [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya offer elegiac, bibliometric and historiographic accounts of Twitter's API specifically, showing a measurable decline in scholarly output once Enterprise pricing was imposed, while [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates Twitter/X's broader institutional and normative collapse. [[Yang2026-tq]] generalizes the pattern across disciplines: social-media-based empirical research tripled from 2010 to the early 2020s, then plateaued and declined from 2022–2024 as API restrictions bit, with research attention persistently misaligned with actual platform usage (Twitter/X and Facebook overstudied, YouTube and TikTok understudied). Tools once central to this ecosystem — CrowdTangle, Social Science One, Pushshift — are repeatedly invoked across these papers as cautionary case studies in the fragility of "data philanthropy" and "independence by permission."

### Article 40 and the DSA as regulatory remedy — and its limits

Against this backdrop, the Digital Services Act's Article 40 is treated across many papers as a genuine inflection point: a shift from platform discretion to a binding legal right. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], [[Pierri2026-ib]], and [[Giglietto2025-ed60bc90]] collectively frame this as a "moving target" moment — turbulence that signals the regulation is finally biting entrenched asymmetries rather than evidence of regulatory failure. Yet nearly every empirical engagement documents how far implementation falls short of the promise. [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] finds TikTok's DSA-compliant Research API riddled with unexplained inconsistencies; [[Rieder2025-ju]] audits YouTube's Data API search endpoint and finds it "forgetful by design," with severe temporal decay undermining retrospective systemic-risk research; [[Bruns2026-pn]] and [[Cullen2026-cb]] analyze the Meta Content Library and CrowdTangle's "clean room" successor respectively, showing how technical design (Jupyter-only environments, monthly data deletion, exclusion of qualitative tools) structurally disadvantages non-programmers, non-US institutions, and the Global South. [[Lukito2026-nb]] systematizes this as a "price, proficiency, or permission" problem across 72 access tools, finding consumption-side data starkly underrepresented relative to production-side data. [[Annabell2026-ev]] shows that even the foundational term "data" in Article 40 functions as a contested "floating signifier," narrowly operationalized by platforms (aggregated, anonymized, pre-structured) in ways that exclude qualitative and humanities conceptions of evidence. [[Peters2026-mo]] documents a parallel contest over "data quality," largely absent from official EU text but inserted into the Delegated Regulation through academic/NGO advocacy — with Meta notably inverting the argument to claim its own data is too poor-quality to bother sharing. [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]], [[Schulte2026-df]], and [[Philipp2026-tl]] converge on treating observability itself as research infrastructure, documenting formal EU proceedings against X, TikTok, and Meta for Article 40 breaches, and calling for standardized, reusable multi-platform pipelines rather than one-off case studies.

### The methodological consequences of thresholds and censoring

A distinct cluster interrogates how specific data-access design choices distort findings, independent of regulation. [[Allen2021-ai]] and [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] both dissect the 100-public-shares threshold in Meta's URL Shares Dataset: the former shows it inflates apparent fake-news prevalence by roughly 4x relative to a representative Nielsen panel, the latter shows it produces suspiciously uniform cross-country trends attributable to algorithmic ranking changes (likely "Meaningful Social Interactions" shifts) rather than genuine local dynamics — both papers warning that scale does not guarantee unbiased inference. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] extends this skepticism to web-scraping methodology itself, finding that the collection environment (in-situ vs. ex-situ), not time delay, is the dominant source of measurement distortion in news-exposure research, with errors non-randomly distributed across content categories. [[Zheng2026-bi]] responds constructively, building genuinely random-sample tools (TubeStats, TokStats) to counteract the "denominator problem" created by popularity-biased opportunistic sampling. Collectively these papers establish that even where access exists, the *structure* of that access — thresholds, sampling regimes, API ranking parameters — actively co-produces the empirical picture researchers can draw, a theme [[McNally2025-dn]] inverts productively by showing Facebook's News Feed changes are detectable statistically despite "black box" framing, arguing for exactly this kind of systemic algorithmic auditing under DSA Article 40(4).

### Platform policy shifts as object of study: political content reduction, fact-checking retreat, and Community Notes

A second major thread uses the DSA-era access regimes (Meta Content Library, Community Notes APIs) to study specific, consequential platform policy reversals. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] is paradigmatic: using Meta Content Library data to show that Meta's political content reduction policy suppressed Italian MPs' Facebook reach by 72%, began roughly ten months before Meta's announced rollout, and — crucially — was never disclosed in Meta's DSA transparency reporting, even as extremist accounts offset the policy by posting more frequently. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] complements this by showing Facebook's share-to-view amplification is actively and unevenly moderated, intensifying partisan penalties and journalistic-quality rewards around the 2020 election — evidence against treating platform governance as a stable background condition, as [[Munger2025-cz]] argues the Meta2020 partnership effectively did. [[Rogers2026-zz]] traces Meta's terminological evolution from "fake news" to "borderline" content and argues this depoliticizing vocabulary, paired with successive API closures, has steered research toward industry-sanctioned partnerships while obscuring that surviving borderline content is overwhelmingly right-wing. The collapse of professional fact-checking is examined from several angles: [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]] shows Zuckerberg's framing of fact-checkers as censors is empirically unfounded (removal occurs in only ~30% of verified cases), while [[Farkas2026-lr]] documents the rhetorical "apologia" fact-checkers deploy to defend platform funding dependencies. [[Renault2025-uh]] and [[Bouchaud2026-np]] turn to Community Notes as a purported alternative, finding respectively that Republican posts are flagged 2.3x more often than Democratic ones (undermining the bias rationale for replacing fact-checkers) and that the bridging algorithm structurally undermoderates polarizing content, creating electoral risks — with [[Miller2026-cp]] describing the AI Note Writer API's design response. [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] situates all of this within a broader three-phase arc of platform election policy (protection → civic integrity → rollback/"platform illiberalism"), and [[Moran2025-qn]] documents the parallel institutional collapse of the trust-and-safety profession itself.

### Industry capture of the evidence base

A reflexive, metascientific strand asks whether platform dependence compromises the research literature meant to hold platforms accountable. [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] quantifies this directly: about half of high-profile social media papers in Science/Nature/PNAS carry undisclosed industry ties, with "industrial saturation" reaching ~80% once editors and reviewers are included, and industry-tied work skewing toward user-focused topics (misinformation sharing) rather than platform-scale dynamics. [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] generalizes this into a tobacco/fossil-fuel-style agnotology framework, while [[Heiss2026-qv]] frames the DSA and stronger "research on research" norms as the dual necessary response. [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]] offers a methodological counter-case — Facebook's own ad infrastructure repurposed for independent survey recruitment of hard-to-reach populations — illustrating that platform affordances can sometimes be turned toward (rather than against) independent inquiry, though with ambiguous validity results. [[Allen2025-ot]] and [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] showcase adaptive responses to the closing data environment: platform-independent browser-extension experiments and cross-national extensions of Meta-partnered datasets (URL Shares) respectively, both explicitly framed as compensating for lost or constrained official access.

### Theorizing platform power and observability

Underpinning all of the above is a conceptual lineage running from [[Gillespie2010-sla2]]'s account of "platform" as strategically ambiguous discursive technology, through [[van-Dijck2018-up]]'s "platform society" and [[Rieder2020-sl35]]'s critique of transparency-as-panacea in favor of "observability," to [[Gillespie2022-jx]]'s typology of reduction as an invisible, unaudited form of moderation. [[Petre2019-zh]]'s concept of "platform paternalism" and [[De2026-ld]]'s "changecraft" extend this into the discursive management of platform change itself, while [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] shows how Meta's Newsroom externalizes blame for problematic content onto "inauthentic" outside actors. [[Bechmann2026-dr]] explicitly argues that DSA-era research should move beyond individual causal effects toward platform collective behavior as "democratic infrastructure," a framing echoed in [[Crosset2026-mq]]'s comparative analysis of EU/US content-moderation law as "security bureaucratisation" and in [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]]'s account of how platform lobbying (e.g., against Brazil's PL 2630) exports asymmetric power to the Global South. Looking forward, [[Tornberg2026-im]] and [[Weinbrand2026-sf]] trace an emergent transition from "platform society" to "AI society," in which generative AI (AI Overviews, LLM-based inference) threatens to reproduce — and deepen — the same data-access asymmetries this entire literature has spent a decade diagnosing, since the AI Act, unlike the DSA, currently offers no equivalent independent-researcher-access mechanism. Rounding out the picture, case studies of audited moderation failure ([[Rieder2026-pp]] on YouTube's Tate-space, [[Karo2026-dn]] on TikTok jihadist content, [[Jurg2025-ur]] on YouTube election search, [[Tonneau2025-bv]] on language disparities in moderator allocation, [[Simeone2025-vo]] and [[Ventura2026-yc]] on deplatforming/banning effects, [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] on coordinated inauthentic behavior, [[Kuznetsova2026-hw]] on climate misinformation governance, [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] on inadvertent ideological profiling, [[Ahuja2025-ku]] on dark patterns under Article 25) demonstrate empirically what the access-and-methodology papers argue in the abstract: that platform governance can only be held accountable to the extent that independent researchers are structurally enabled — through regulation, infrastructure, and disclosure — to see what platforms do.
