# News Diffusion and Platform Algorithms

## Beyond the share button: visibility as the central problem

The animating question across this collection is deceptively simple: does what people *share* determine what people *see*? [[Bakshy2015-rn]] set the terms of debate a decade ago, showing that Facebook's News Feed algorithm modestly dampens cross-cutting exposure but that individual click choices do so more — a finding often invoked to dismiss strong "filter bubble" claims. [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] revisits this question with five years of Meta's Privacy-Protected URLs Dataset and overturns the assumption of a stable, linear amplification pipeline: sharing reliably predicts views, but that relationship is systematically dampened for partisan-audience content and boosted for journalistically rigorous content, and — crucially — these penalties and rewards swing sharply in step with known governance interventions like the 2020 "break the glass" measures. This reframes the platform not as a passive conduit but as an actively, unevenly calibrated gatekeeper. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] complements this at the level of raw diffusion trees, showing misinformation's distinctive virality and reach patterns during the 2020 election, while [[Allcott2025-jb]]'s null results on political ad removal and [[McNally2025-dn]]'s longitudinal reconstruction of Guardian engagement anomalies (with detectable ~19–24 day lags after algorithmic interventions) together argue that the "black box" is more interpretable, and more consequential, than it appears. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends this US-centric research program to Germany, France, and Italy, finding that untrustworthy URL shares rise around elections even as *Views* of untrustworthy content stay comparatively stable — an early empirical hint of the very dampening mechanism that [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]] later isolates directly.

## Algorithms as active curators, not neutral conduits

A cluster of papers pursues this governance question experimentally and forensically. [[Gauthier2026-iq]]'s field experiment on X is the most direct causal demonstration: switching users onto the algorithmic feed shifts political attitudes rightward and persists even after the algorithm is switched off, because exposure reshapes *who users follow* — a mechanism that helps reconcile the earlier Meta null results with observed asymmetric effects. [[Efstratiou2025-gs]] narrows in on the Musk acquisition moment, showing that apparent right-leaning algorithmic advantage is better explained by agitating content and proximity to Musk himself than by ideology per se, while legacy-verified institutional accounts lost visibility outright. [[Brown2026-br]] and [[Brady2026-ln]] push the audit logic further by disentangling supply-side algorithmic effects from demand-side user choice: Brown finds strong "rabbit hole" effects but no independent echo-chamber or radicalization push on YouTube, whereas Brady's Bluesky field experiment demonstrates that engagement-based ranking amplifies moralized, emotional content and distorts perceived social norms — and that a redesigned "diversified extremity" algorithm can mitigate this without sacrificing enjoyment. Beneath these visible effects lies architecture: [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] shows X's recommender system inadvertently encodes a near-perfect linear representation of users' Left–Right position, entangling political profiling with ordinary friend-recommendation infrastructure, while [[Jurg2025-ur]] and [[Votta2025-xz]] turn the audit lens onto YouTube's "authoritative sources" labeling and onto the opaque cost dynamics of political ad delivery, respectively — both arguing that governance claims of neutrality collapse under empirical scrutiny.

## Partisan sorting, curation bubbles, and the limits of source-level measurement

A methodological throughline challenges the field's default unit of analysis: the outlet or domain. [[Green2025-ap]]'s "curation bubbles" argument is pivotal — domain-level partisanship scores conflate genuine moderation with story-level heterogeneity, especially for moderate-scoring sources like the Wall Street Journal, whose individual stories can serve starkly divergent partisan audiences. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends this logic to misinformation itself, showing that mainstream articles co-shared with fake news are more likely to carry misinformation-adjacent narrative structures — meaning source-reliability blocklists miss a large share of how misleading narratives actually travel. [[Gaisbauer2025-by]] proposes a multi-level "political cartography" (story, outlet, content) as a corrective to prior single-level, single-country designs, and [[Waight2025-al]] supplies a complementary technical advance: an LLM-based pipeline for measuring narrative similarity that recovers cross-source claim-sharing invisible to exact-text-reuse or topic-modeling methods, applied to the Ukraine "biolabs" case. [[Eady2025-vm]] offers a parallel measurement contribution at the level of ideology rather than narrative, jointly scaling politicians, media, and ordinary users from shared URLs and showing that electorally uncompetitive districts produce more polarized elite sharing. [[Mosleh2024-op]] and [[Nenno2025-xa]] broaden the empirical base geographically and platform-wise, the former documenting divergent engagement patterns with partisan/low-quality news across seven platforms, the latter showing that news-value signatures of "misinformation" (especially conflict) hold cross-nationally but that the WEIRD/non-WEIRD divide fails to capture important variation, with the US and Brazil as outlying, hyper-polarized cases.

## Elites, institutions, and contests over narrative authority

Several papers relocate the visibility question from algorithms to the political actors who exploit them. [[Rodarte2026-dk]] documents Brazilian parliamentarians competing for "epistemic authority" during a health crisis through derivative reporting, direct anti-press claims, and local brokerage — a taxonomy of elite strategies for supplanting journalistic gatekeeping that resonates with [[Knupfer2025-vt]]'s "logic of connective faction," in which the sudden rise of Critical Race Theory as a GOP talking point reveals how ideologically extreme legislators are rewarded with disproportionate hyper-partisan media attention and engagement, radicalizing intra-party politics from within. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Kim2026-wg]] extend the elite/institutional frame to misinformation sharing and targeted voter suppression respectively, the latter showing that geo-racially targeted 2016 suppression ads produced measurable turnout declines invisible to average-treatment-effect designs. [[Balluff2026-bv]] and [[Iris2026-pg]] examine visibility capture at the outlet level: Balluff traces Austria's "Inseratenaffäre" advertising-for-coverage scandal via difference-in-differences, while Iris documents disproportionate Radical Right visibility across the 2024 European elections, intensifying in the campaign's final weeks even in countries lacking domestic radical-right representation. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] flips the causal arrow experimentally, showing that simply hiding political elites' posts from X feeds reduces low-credibility exposure and misinformation susceptibility — direct evidence that elite-centered exposure architecture, not just individual disposition, drives belief distortion. [[Ducci2022-10cb5d70]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] round out this cluster with health-adjacent cases: Google News' gatekeeping of Italian health content shows engagement driven by topic emotionality rather than source identity, while Ghezzi documents how UK newspapers selectively cite COVID-19 scientists along partisan lines, extending elite/narrative contestation into science communication.

## Coordination and non-human amplification

A parallel strand interrogates the causal weight of bots and coordinated networks often assumed to drive visibility. [[Efstratiou2026-ij]] traces COVID-19 science communication pathways and finds a genuinely coordinated, contrarian retweet network amplifying a narrow set of credentialed anti-consensus experts — importantly, not bot-driven but human-coordinated, with news coverage following rather than preceding this Twitter activity. [[Arceneaux2026-xk]] argues for revising agenda-building theory to admit bots as a "fourth" communicator category, finding they most effectively transfer negative attribute salience to campaigns while leaving the press comparatively insulated. [[Zhao2025-ny]] examines hashtag hijacking as a related attention-capture tactic combining narrative substitution and bot amplification. Yet [[Di-Marco2025-aa]]'s graph-theoretic post-hoc audit complicates the prevailing narrative of coordinated-account potency: observed coordinated accounts in UK election cascades achieve influence far below optimal or even resource-matched benchmarks, suggesting CIB is often less pivotal than assumed. [[Smith2025-kc]] supplies a structural mechanism for how influence concentrates regardless of coordination — the "attention broker" who accelerates triad formation through attributed amplification, a causal account of how visibility begets more visibility organically.

## Platform-specific ecologies beyond Facebook and Twitter/X

Several papers insist that visibility dynamics are not platform-agnostic. [[Gerbaudo2026-fo]] offers the broadest theoretical claim: TikTok's shift from "networked" to "clustered" publics — item-centric, opaque, behaviorally inferred — constitutes a second-generation transformation of social media publics that other platforms are now imitating. [[Nangle2026-yo]] and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] apply this platform-sensitivity to far-right communication: Nangle shows Instagram Reels normalize extremism through positive affect and algorithmic proximity, while Bouchafra's cross-platform analysis of the Sweden Democrats' EU campaign shows deliberate tailoring — argumentative posts on Facebook/X versus memetic, foreign-footage TikTok content aimed at younger, more securitized audiences. [[Rieder2026-pp]] and [[Copland2025-em]] examine how deplatforming and cross-border amplification interact with recommendation systems: Tate's "ambient ideology" persists on YouTube via decentralized replatforming that outpaces moderation, while Sky News Australia leverages a dual Facebook/YouTube strategy to become a node in transnational right-wing "network propaganda." [[Zhu2026-tn]] shows that alternative-mainstream media boundaries are themselves context-dependent — dense audience overlap where populists have institutional access, isolated "enclaves" (Sweden) where they don't. [[Wedel2026-ix]] shifts to the audience side, showing young adults' "informativeness" judgments cut across professional/non-professional and platform boundaries, extending journalistic norms to influencers based on perceived expertise rather than credentialing. Affective engagement mechanics recur throughout: [[Anwar2024-34dba628]]'s review and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]]'s Scandinavian election study both confirm that anger reliably drives Facebook sharing more than "Love," with populist actors disproportionately benefiting from this affective asymmetry.

## Misinformation as infrastructure: voids, narratives, and multi-platform diffusion

A final cluster reconceives misinformation measurement itself. [[Scalco2026-bd]] operationalizes "information voids" as detectable demand-supply anomalies, showing these voids correlate with reduced high-credibility content and increased misinformation prevalence around vaccine milestones. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] reframes disinformation as recurring cross-outlet narratives rather than discrete claims, echoing [[Waight2025-al]]'s methodological argument above. [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] delivers perhaps the sharpest corrective to platform-effects research: the dominant "direct referrer paradigm" undercounts misinformation exposure by 1.5–15×, and counterfactual removal simulations reveal platform-specific, partisan-asymmetric effects (Twitter for Republicans, YouTube for Democrats) — while also showing that banning platforms would disproportionately harm high-quality news access, complicating simple deplatforming policy. [[Gerard2025-br]]'s cross-platform discourse-network embedding similarly reveals a tiny set of "bridge users" responsible for the majority of narrative migration between Truth Social and X, structural findings invisible to interaction-based network methods. [[Oswald2025-km]] supplies the epistemological caveat underlying all of this: the production-consumption gap means visible online discourse is a highly skewed "tip of an iceberg," a caution that should temper inferences drawn from any of the trace-data studies above.

## Journalism quality, generative AI, and what counts as news

The topic's arc closes on journalism's own transformation under these platform pressures. [[Dodds2026-df]] frames AI hype itself as a systemic infrastructure reshaping journalistic sourcing, legitimacy, and labor visibility, while [[Gilardi2026-hw]] finds empirically that audiences rate AI-generated and AI-assisted news as equivalent in quality to human-written news, with disclosure boosting short-term engagement without changing longer-term acceptance. [[Arora2025-tx]] pushes framing analysis itself to keep pace with these shifts, arguing that text-only computational methods miss the multi-modal, cross-modal editorial choices that increasingly define partisan framing of the same events. Together with [[Wedel2026-ix]]'s audience-centered "informativeness" and [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]]'s quality-reward finding, these papers suggest that journalistic quality is not merely being algorithmically rewarded or penalized but is itself being redefined — by audiences, by AI production, and by the platforms that mediate what millions ultimately see.
