# Information Disorder & Fake News Theory

## The Line of Argument

The papers filed here trace a discipline in the process of reinventing its own foundational vocabulary. If there is a single throughline, it is a sustained argument against treating "fake news" or "misinformation" as a stable, discrete, content-level property — false statements to be spotted, labeled, and corrected — and toward treating information disorder as an emergent property of hybrid, networked, narrative, and increasingly synthetic media systems. The classic taxonomic move that anchors the whole topic is [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], which explicitly rejects creator-intent-based typologies of fake news and relocates analysis onto propagators, arguing that truthfulness judgments cascade unpredictably through a hybrid media system via micro (individual judgment), meso (injector/propagator combinations), and macro (emergent cascade) levels. Nearly every other paper in this collection can be read as either extending, complicating, or empirically testing some piece of that founding move.

### From facts to narratives

A major cluster pushes the "beyond discrete falsehoods" argument toward narrative theory. [[Marwick2026-qd]] reframes disinformation as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic *story* — with catchphrases, characters, and moral arcs — arguing that fact-checking and domain-blocklisting fail precisely because they target claims rather than stories. [[Sadler2025-vu]] supplies the theoretical scaffolding this narrative turn needs, critiquing loose, common-sense uses of "narrative" in disinformation studies and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narratological framework (drawing on Ricoeur and Phelan) that can assess content as referentially or ethically problematic without reducing it to true/false. [[Starbird2025-jj]] and [[Prochaska2025-ef]] operationalize this narrative-and-framing lens empirically on election rumors, showing that misleading content emerges from the interaction of (often accurate) evidence with partisan frames, and that "deep stories" are performed collaboratively by elites, influencers, and audiences across platforms and years — with 2022 rumors leaning on the interpretive scaffolding built in 2020. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]] and [[Goel2025-iq]] extend the narrative-diffusion argument into measurement: the former shows repeated narrative exposure (not isolated claims) predicts belief, while the latter demonstrates that *true* mainstream content is routinely repurposed into misleading narratives, exposing the blind spot of source-level (domain-list) misinformation metrics. Together these papers constitute a coherent counter-tradition to information-centric, fact-checkable conceptions of fake news.

### Weaponization and rhetorical function

A second thread treats "fake news" itself as a political weapon rather than an object of study. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show empirically that "fake news"/"disinformation" accusations function largely as delegitimizing rhetoric — reshaping Hallin's spheres of consensus/controversy/deviance, or serving as tools of negative campaigning — rather than as descriptions of fabricated content. This dovetails with [[Hurcombe2025-cs]]'s discourse analysis of Meta's own Newsroom, which shows the platform strategically externalizes blame for "problematic content" onto inauthentic outsiders while positioning itself as capable fixer, and with [[Graham2025-gp]]'s case study of #IStandWithPutin, arguing that propaganda now parasitizes rather than merely evades the "infrastructure of truth." [[Makeev2026-ma]] extends this weaponization logic to state propaganda's dual domestic/foreign objectives in wartime.

### Platform infrastructures, governance, and accountability

A substantial arc concerns how platforms and regulators construct, measure, and govern "misinformation" as a category. [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] is the conceptual ancestor here, showing how the very term "platform" performs discursive work that forecloses accountability — a move later echoed structurally in [[Crosset2026-mq]]'s analysis of EU/US content-moderation law as "security bureaucratisation" and in [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]'s tracing of platform election-policy phases from protection to civic-integrity expansion to backlash. [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], [[Farkas2026-lr]], and [[Pierri2025-hm]] turn to the fact-checking institution itself: fact-checkers defend platform partnerships rhetorically even as removal outcomes remain rare and platforms retreat from cooperative moderation, while DSA-mandated researcher access remains hobbled by resource asymmetries. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes this whole arc, arguing that "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models rather than an aberration, tracking the rise and post-2021 retreat of platform moderation infrastructure. [[Bruns2026-yv]] narrates a parallel institutional decline specific to Twitter/X, and [[Giada2026-fc9a3833]] proposes "Deceptive Information Operations" as a category broader than false-news taxonomies, capturing coordination and affordance-exploitation regardless of factual content — directly extending Giglietto's propagator-centered logic into platform-forensic method. [[Thiele2025-ol]] offers a parallel theoretical contribution on the "attribution problem" for coordinated manipulation campaigns, modeling principals' strategic tradeoffs between influence and detectability.

### Measurement, evidence, and empirical corrections to alarmism

Running alongside the conceptual reinvention is a skeptical, evidence-auditing strand. [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] scrutinize claims about the scale and algorithmic drivers of misinformation exposure, arguing public discourse has outpaced the evidence. [[Luhring2025-od]] performs a methodological audit of NewsGuard, showing how binary trustworthiness classifications can distort findings — a caution that resonates with [[Renault2025-uh]]'s finding that partisan asymmetries in flagged misinformation persist even under crowdsourced Community Notes, undermining claims of fact-checker bias. [[Karlsson2026-hd]] and [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] examine second-order effects: media narratives *about* interference can themselves polarize, and citizens exhibit a "hostile misinformation effect" mirroring the classic hostile media effect. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] complements this by showing, via diary methodology, that lay perceptions of misinformation diverge sharply from objective falsity and center on mainstream "clickbait," not fringe sources — a finding that quietly validates the anti-source-list, propagation-centered arguments made elsewhere in this cluster.

### Everyday, embodied, and identity-based knowledge production

Several papers push the topic toward the sociology of belief and identity. [[Marwick2025-ov]] and [[Marwick2025-vx]] examine conspiracy and far-right "redpilling" as processual, community-embedded socialization rather than single-exposure effects, framing disinformation as "evidence" mobilized within a "generous epistemology" that grants both marginalized standpoint knowledge and racist/conspiratorial belief equal rhetorical footing. [[Adam2026-tz]] supplies a complementary quantitative account of how populism and mistrust drive selective engagement with and avoidance of conspiracy-relevant media, integrating predispositions and media effects into a single contagion/mitigation model. [[Rohrbach2026-rc]] and [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]] extend this to journalism's own complicity — in inadvertently fostering conspiracy beliefs, and in politically polarized sourcing of scientific expertise during COVID-19.

### Emerging technological complications: AI, deepfakes, and synthetic reality

A forward-looking cluster confronts generative AI's destabilization of the entire epistemic apparatus this topic has been building. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] proposes "emergent facts" as a new epistemic category beyond evidence-based, interpretative, and rule-based facts, needed because GenAI outputs are plausible but referentially unanchored. [[Emilio2026-ik]] scales this concern up to "synthetic realities" and the "Generative AI Paradox," in which ubiquitous synthetic content may cause societies to rationally discount all digital evidence. [[Choi2026-bz]] and [[Hameleers2026-mc]] empirically temper alarmism about visual/deepfake persuasiveness, showing that modality effects are context-bound and that low-tech decontextualized video can rival or beat AI-generated imagery. [[DeVerna2025-dl]] extends the AI question to fact-checking infrastructure itself, showing LLMs are unreliable fact-checkers without curated retrieval — a sobering counterpoint to techno-solutionist hopes voiced in the Delphi study.

### Toward synthesis and intervention

The topic's meta-level papers attempt to reconcile this fragmentation. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study explicitly diagnoses the field's siloed, individual-level, present-focused character and calls for coordinated multi-level intervention across platforms, journalism, and civil society. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] makes the same diagnosis theoretically, arguing that dominant analogies (infodemic, information warfare, information pollution) each illuminate only one level of a complex adaptive system and that portfolio-style, complexity-aware interventions are needed. On the intervention side, [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], [[Szabo2026-rd]], [[Ramos2026-qo]], and [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] test concrete responses at different levels: scaled prebunking ads, conversational-AI inoculation, narrative/argument-checking beyond fact-checking, and structural "elite-blocking" interventions that reduce misinformation susceptibility by reshaping feed architecture rather than individual cognition. [[Rogers2026-cy]] operationalizes much of the topic's network-and-narrative thinking into a practical mapping methodology for fact-checkers, situating "post-truth spaces" among coordinated inauthentic behavior, alternative influence networks, and participatory propaganda. [[Xue2025-bp]] and [[Esau2025-tf]] round out the intervention conversation by probing fact-checking's own affective register and by theorizing "deliberative reciprocity" as an antidote to destructive polarization.

### Peripheral extensions

Several papers extend the topic's logic into adjacent domains that stress-test its generality: [[Swartz2026-zb]] reframes platform scams as a governing economic logic rather than peripheral fraud, echoing the volume's recurring claim that manipulation is structural rather than anomalous; [[Rothut2026-wt]] applies a hybrid online/offline lens to radicalization research that parallels the hybrid-media-system framing of fake news; [[Bosch2024-hj]] and [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] track platform- and event-specific adaptations of propaganda (TikTok sound, appropriation of emerging crises); [[Di-Domenico2026-zq]] locates influencer status, rather than content, as a driver of toxic reception; [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] extend the empirical net to WhatsApp and elite social-media behavior, respectively; and [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] closes the arc on an explicitly normative note, insisting platforms bear accountability for the democratic backsliding this entire literature has been diagnosing.

Read together, these papers chart a field moving decisively away from the "detect-and-correct-the-false-claim" paradigm of the mid-2010s "fake news" moment, and toward a systems-level, narrative-sensitive, infrastructure-aware understanding of information disorder — one in which platforms, propagators, genres, identities, and now generative AI are treated as co-constitutive of what counts as true.
