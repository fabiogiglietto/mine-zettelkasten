# Information Disorder Theory

## Line of argument: from "fake news" to information disorder as a systemic property

The papers filed here trace a discipline's slow abandonment of a simple, intent-based definition of falsehood in favour of increasingly systemic, relational, and infrastructural accounts of how misleading communication moves through society. The starting point is definitional trouble: [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] shows that creator-intent-centred typologies of "fake news" cannot explain how content actually cycles through the hybrid news system once propagators, not injectors, are placed at the centre of analysis; [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Allcott2017-yz]] independently arrive at similar conclusions from political-communication and economics respectively, treating disinformation as a market and institutional phenomenon rather than a content category. This definitional instability recurs across the corpus — [[Mosca2026-yh]] and [[Yoo2026-ev]] both show "fake news" mutating from an analytic term into a rhetorical weapon of delegitimization, while [[Sadler2025-vu]] argues the whole true/false binary is inadequate to narratives that are ethically but not referentially false. [[Dierickx2026-tw]] extends this epistemological unsettling into the generative-AI era, proposing "emergent facts" as a category beyond evidence-based, interpretative, or rule-based truth.

## Foundational architectures: hybridity, platforms, and the disinformation order

Much of this literature's theoretical scaffolding rests on [[Chadwick2013-ns]] and [[Chadwick2015-ii]], whose concept of the *hybrid media system* — old and new media logics bound by adaptation and interdependence rather than displacement — underwrites almost every subsequent claim that misinformation cannot be studied at a single media layer. [[Bennett2018-ys]] translates this into the "disinformation order," linking strategic falsehood to a legitimacy crisis of democratic institutions rather than to technology per se, a thesis extended empirically by [[Benkler2018-lw]], whose network-mapping of the 2016 US election locates the crisis in an asymmetric, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem rather than in Russian interference or Facebook's algorithm. [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] supply the platform-studies leg of this architecture, showing how the very term "platform" performs ideological work that obscures editorial power — a foundation later radicalized by [[Gillespie2022-jx]], who argues that algorithmic *reduction* (rather than removal) is the dominant, least-visible form of content moderation. This platform-critical thread culminates in the collection's most recent and most unsettled entries: [[Tornberg2026-lc]] and [[Boyd2026-op]] both declare the "social media" paradigm itself obsolete, arguing that algorithmic broadcasting, parasociality, and AI-mediated interaction have replaced the reciprocal, network-based sociality that grounded two decades of misinformation theory — a shift [[Swartz2026-zb]] reframes provocatively as social media entering a "scam age" where boundary-work between legitimate and illegitimate economic activity has collapsed.

## Measuring the problem: prevalence, concentration, and the "misinformation is smaller than you think" cluster

A methodologically coherent cluster interrogates the *scale* of the misinformation problem empirically, largely converging on concentration and modesty. [[Allen2020-nj]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Eady2023-xg]], and [[Allcott2019-gn]] each show, across different platforms and datasets, that fake-news exposure and sharing are heavily concentrated among small, disproportionately older and more conservative subpopulations, and dwarfed by mainstream news consumption — a pattern synthesized programmatically by [[Budak2024-ef]], which explicitly diagnoses a divergence between public discourse and empirical evidence. [[Tsfati2020-uo]] supplies the crucial bridging mechanism: mainstream media's own coverage of fake news, intended to debunk, may be the primary vector through which falsehoods reach mass publics — anticipating [[Goel2025-iq]]'s finding that mainstream, factually accurate articles are disproportionately co-shared to support misleading narratives, and [[Marwick2026-qd]]'s argument that disinformation operates as cross-platform *narrative* rather than discrete claim. This measurement debate is complicated rather than resolved by [[Yang2023-cg]], which shows textual/link-based methods have drastically undercounted image-based misinformation, and by [[Dahlke2026-sl34]], which shows "direct referrer" methodologies undercount platforms' embedded, indirect contribution to exposure by 1.5–15x. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Vosoughi2018-at]] anchor the diffusion side of this debate, the latter's landmark finding that falsehood travels farther, faster, and deeper than truth online sitting in productive tension with the concentration literature.

## Psychological and identity-based mechanisms

A second major strand locates the drivers of belief and sharing in cognition and identity rather than exposure alone. [[Kunda1990-cg]] supplies the foundational motivated-reasoning mechanism — biased memory search and belief construction constrained by plausibility — which [[Pennycook2021-jq]] then partially challenges by showing that poor truth discernment stems more from inattention and lack of reflective reasoning than from partisan motivation, and that sharing and belief are strikingly dissociated. [[Osmundsen2021-et]] complicates this picture again, finding partisan out-group animus, not ignorance, to be the dominant driver of fake-news sharing specifically. [[Iyengar2019-jj]] supplies the affective-polarization backdrop against which [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]]'s "hostile misinformation effect" and [[Berlinski2023-rj]]'s finding that fact-checks fail to counteract partisan-congenial fraud claims should be read. [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]] remains the field's canonical account of why corrections so often fail (the continued influence effect), a problem [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] try to solve technologically and rhetorically respectively — the former via LLM-based "conversational inoculation," the latter via narrative- and argument-level correction beyond claim-by-claim fact-checking, echoing [[Prochaska2025-ef]] and [[Starbird2025-jj]]'s CSCW-rooted "deep story" and evidence-frame frameworks for collective sensemaking around US election-fraud rumors.

## Coordination, attribution, and state actors

A distinct sub-literature treats misinformation as a problem of *coordination* rather than content. [[Keller2019-nk]] establishes the principal-agent lens for detecting astroturfing via coordination traces rather than bot-like features, a foundation extended by [[Thiele2025-ol]]'s typology of coordinated social media manipulation and by [[Mannocci2026-kc]]'s comprehensive survey reconciling academic and platform definitions of coordinated inauthentic behavior. [[Starbird2019-qv]] pushes back against bot/troll reductionism by reframing information operations as *participatory* and collaborative crowd work, a claim empirically extended by [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s organizational history of the IRA "troll factory" and [[Kulichkina2026-zk]]'s study of dual-use coordination during China's COVID protests. [[Makeev2026-ma]], [[Graham2025-gp]], [[Gaber2022-bk]], and [[Beacken2026-zb]] extend this state/elite-strategic register into wartime propaganda, hashtag campaigns, "strategic lying," and generative-AI-enabled propaganda in weakened democracies respectively, while [[Tornberg2025-ir]] and [[Tai2026-qk]] quantify which parties and elected officials actually spread misinformation, converging on radical-right populism as the strongest predictor.

## Radicalization, mainstreaming, and the far right

A closely linked cluster examines how coordinated and organic online dynamics feed extremist mobilization. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of online-radicalization research establishes internet exposure as catalyst rather than cause, a framework Rothut2026-tz operationalizes through the concept of "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" in the Querdenken case, and which [[Marwick2025-vx]] complicates from the inside via the "redpilling" narrative, showing radicalization as gradual socialization rather than sudden conversion. [[Bennett2025-xs]] supplies the institutional counterpoint, arguing that individual-level disinformation research has neglected how digital "surrogate organizations" reorganize party politics toward illiberalism — a macro-level argument that resonates with [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s blunt claim that platforms bear direct accountability for democratic backsliding.

## Comparative, cross-national, and Global South correctives

A recurring self-critique is the field's WEIRD-centrism. [[Humprecht2020-gd]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] establish resilience as a structural, cross-national property rather than an individual trait; [[Nenno2025-xa]] extends this to news-values analysis across 24 countries; [[Tonneau2025-bv]] documents systemic underinvestment in Global South-language content moderation; and [[Ventura2025-sw]] and [[Wack2026-bt]] shift attention to WhatsApp in Brazil and deepfakes in Kenya respectively, arguing that messaging apps and low-resource information environments require distinct theorizing rather than extrapolation from US/Facebook-centric findings.

## Synthesis attempts and the generative-AI horizon

The corpus's most recent and most self-consciously synthetic entries attempt to consolidate this fragmentation. [[Frischlich2025-vn]] argues that no single analogy (virus, warfare, pollution) captures misinformation's multilevel complexity, proposing a four-level complex-systems framework; [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study operationalizes this synthesis across ten thematic areas via expert consensus; and [[Hourigan2026-oc]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] return the field to audience-centred, "everyday" and "informativeness" perspectives that resist both topic- and platform-bound definitions. Finally, a wave of papers — [[Emilio2026-ik]], [[Kasianenko2026-tn]], [[Hameleers2026-mc]], [[Choi2026-bz]], [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] — registers the generative-AI inflection point, from Ferrara's "synthetic reality" stack and "epistemic security" agenda to AI Overviews' uneven conspiracy moderation and elite-exposure interventions, suggesting that the field's next foundational reconceptualization — following the arc from intent-based "fake news" through hybrid-systemic disorder to platform/coordination analysis — will need to grapple with information environments in which the very distinction between human and synthetic communication, on which most prior theory depends, no longer reliably holds. [[Scalco2026-bd]]'s operationalization of "information voids" and [[De_Leon2025-qn]]'s concept of "networked exposure" suggest the field's methodological future lies in modeling absence and circulation as rigorously as it has modeled presence and content — a fitting endpoint for a literature that began by asking what is false and has ended by asking what infrastructures make falsity, truth, and their felt difference possible at all.
