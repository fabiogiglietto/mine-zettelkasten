# Information Disorder Theory

## Information Disorder Theory

### From "fake news" to information disorder: conceptual foundations

The topic's theoretical spine runs through a sustained critique of intent- and content-centric definitions of "fake news." [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] is foundational here, arguing that scholarship's fixation on creator intent cannot explain how falsehoods actually circulate in a hybrid news system; drawing on second-order cybernetics and Chadwick's hybrid media system framework, it relocates analytical attention from injectors to propagators, proposing a micro-meso-macro model of judgment and cascade. This propagation-centric move anticipates a broader theoretical arc across the corpus: disinformation is increasingly theorized not as discrete false claims but as *narrative*. [[Marwick2026-qd]] makes the strongest case for this shift, showing that successful disinformation behaves as cross-platform, identity-affirming, cathartic story—with characters, catchphrases, and moral stakes—that eludes fact-checking and domain-level detection precisely because it travels through memes, satire, and comedy rather than discrete claims. [[Sadler2025-vu]] pushes this narrative turn further, critiquing the field's "naïve" use of narrative and proposing a hermeneutic-realist, narrative-ethics framework (via Ricoeur and Phelan) for content that is referentially and ethically suspect without being straightforwardly false—directly challenging the true/false binary that underwrites most information-disorder taxonomies. [[Suau_Martinez2026-lv]], [[Prochaska2025-ef]], and [[Starbird2025-jj]] operationalize this narrative view empirically: election-rumor and "deep story" research shows that misinformation's persuasive power lies in repeated exposure to recurring frames and in the interaction between (often accurate) evidence and partisan framing, not merely false facts, with quote-tweet/comment structures making these evidence-frame dynamics visible. [[Goel2025-iq]] extends the same insight into measurement, showing that source-based (domain-list) approaches miss how users repurpose true mainstream content to support misleading narratives—a direct empirical vindication of Giglietto's propagation-centric critique.

### Terminology as rhetorical weapon

A second cluster interrogates "fake news" and "misinformation" not as neutral descriptors but as contested, weaponizable labels. [[Yoo2026-ev]] and [[Mosca2026-yh]] both show, using Hallin's spheres and a new "Strategic Delegitimization and Selective Amplification" model respectively, that accusations of fake news function primarily to delegitimize political opponents rather than describe fabricated content—continuous with Giglietto's warning that the label positions its user as truth-claimer. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] extends this to platform self-discourse, showing how Meta's Newsroom deploys floating signifiers like "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" and "authenticity" to externalize responsibility for problematic content while diffusing its own vocabulary into regulatory and academic discourse—a finding that should give scholars pause about uncritically adopting platform-coined categories, a concern also raised by [[Thiele2025-ol]] and [[Mannocci2026-kc]] with respect to "coordinated inauthentic behavior" more broadly.

### Coordination, inauthenticity, and attribution

Where the narrative turn theorizes *content*, a parallel strand theorizes *coordination*. [[Mannocci2026-kc]] provides the field's most systematic survey, reconciling fragmented platform and academic definitions into a general framework built on actors, synergic actions, and intent, and crucially decoupling authenticity from harmfulness—coordination can be authentic-but-harmful or inauthentic-but-harmless. [[Thiele2025-ol]] complements this with a rational-choice model addressing the "attribution problem": inferring covert principals from observable campaign features (scale, elaborateness, disguise) rather than content, which principals can manipulate. [[Graham2026-fb]] provides a valuable counter-case at the micro level, showing through Reddit's "botting" phenomenon that the human/bot binary is itself unstable and that authenticity is a culturally negotiated performance rather than a technical property—problematizing the very categories that CIB frameworks assume. Empirically, [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] and [[Poliakoff2026-fa]] ground these theoretical typologies: the former identifies over 13,000 coordinated accounts around China's COVID protests, illustrating platforms' "dual-use" character for both mobilization and repression, while the latter's OSINT reconstruction of the Internet Research Agency reframes troll farms as ordinary, labor-market-embedded media organizations rather than exceptional covert units—useful ballast against inflated "troll army" mythology. [[FitzGerald2025-nv]] and [[Makeev2026-ma]] extend the coordination lens temporally and strategically, tracing how manipulation campaigns persist by appropriating emerging events and how state propaganda pursues dual domestic/foreign objectives.

### Mainstreaming, radicalization, and the hybrid media ecosystem

A third arc theorizes how fringe content and extremist framing migrate into the mainstream via hybrid infrastructures. [[Rothut2026-wt]]'s scoping review of 25 years of radicalization scholarship offers a meta-theoretical anchor, arguing that online/offline separation is untenable and proposing a macro/meso/micro, Lasswell-inflected model. Rothut2026-tz applies this at the meso level, introducing "protest-facilitated mainstreaming" to show how Germany's Querdenken movement became structurally embedded in far-right and conspiracist Telegram networks via anti-elite framing rather than explicit ideology. [[Marwick2025-vx]] complements this with a micro-level account of "processual redpilling," dismantling the popular "exposure equals belief" model in favor of gradual socialization in which disinformation functions as legitimizing "evidence" for extremist self-conception. [[Bennett2025-xs]] scales this up institutionally, arguing that technocentric and institutionalist accounts of democratic backsliding must be integrated via a "communication-as-organization" framework in which digital surrogate organizations (QAnon, Stop the Steal) pull parties toward illiberalism—a claim echoed by [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]]'s blunter assertion that platforms must be held accountable for democratic retreat. [[Grusauskaite2026-po]] provides a cultural-sociological counterpoint at the level of identity: Great Reset discourse is sustained less by platform affordances than by symbolic boundary work and affective solidarity, reclaiming "conspiracy theorist" as a badge of honor—an argument for centering identity formation over informational content that resonates with Marwick's cultural-narrative approach.

### Measurement, exposure, and the limits of platform-level metrics

A methodologically self-critical cluster interrogates how information disorder is measured. [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Dahlke2026-sl34]] both push back against inflated public narratives: the former argues exposure to problematic content is concentrated rather than pervasive and algorithms less causally central than assumed, while the latter's "embedded referrer paradigm" shows that direct-referral measures dramatically underestimate platforms' true contribution to misinformation exposure—platforms matter more than naive metrics suggest, but banning them would disproportionately cut high-quality news too. [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] adds diffusion-tree evidence on the concentrated, actor-driven virality of Facebook misinformation during the 2020 election. [[Luhring2025-od]] audits NewsGuard itself, showing that binary trustworthy/untrustworthy classifications can badly distort downstream findings—a caution for the entire source-based measurement tradition this topic underpins. [[Scalco2026-bd]] innovates a demand-supply "information void" metric linking gaps in reliable content to misinformation prevalence, while [[Vincent_undated-re]] offers platform-monitoring evidence (TikTok's rising ~25% mis/disinformation share) directly relevant to DSA-era accountability regimes invoked by [[Bechmann2026-dr]], which itself argues for reconceiving platform "collective behavior" as democratic infrastructure rather than a chain of individual causal effects—a fitting theoretical capstone for the measurement debates.

### Audience reception, belief formation, and everyday misinformation

Several papers relocate information disorder from content/platform properties to audience sensemaking. [[Adam2026-tz]] models COVID conspiracy belief formation as a "marriage" of media exposure and predisposition, distinguishing contagion from mitigation effects and reinforcement from counterarguing/backfire pathways. [[Van_Erkel2026-mk]] extends hostile-media-effect theory into a novel "hostile misinformation effect," in which partisans see their own side as disproportionately targeted by misinformation accusations. [[Hourigan2026-oc]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] both argue for de-centering researcher-imposed definitions in favor of audience perception: misinformation is perceived across far more topics (business, celebrity) and sources (mainstream, not fringe) than scholarship assumes, while "informativeness" is shown to be a richer audience-decoded construct than institutional "newsness." [[Nenno2025-xa]] and [[Humprecht2025-ml]] push the field toward comparative, non-WEIRD analysis, finding that news-value patterns in flagged misinformation vary meaningfully by country, with the US and Brazil as outliers.

### Elites, parties, and the political economy of misinformation sharing

A distinct strand locates information disorder in elite behavior rather than mass psychology. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s cross-national analysis of 32 million parliamentarian tweets finds that neither populism nor right-wing ideology alone predicts misinformation sharing—only their conjunction, radical-right populism, does, reframing misinformation as elite political strategy tied to a legitimacy crisis rather than generalized platform pathology. [[Tai2026-qk]] and [[Renault2025-uh]] extend this elite focus: the former to institutional/ideological "checks" on officials' sharing behavior, the latter showing that Community Notes still flags Republican posts 2.3x more often than Democratic ones, undermining bias-based rationales for abandoning professional fact-checking. [[Donovan2025-ws]] historicizes this whole political economy, arguing "misinformation-at-scale" is a structural feature of engagement-driven business models rather than an aberration, tracing platforms' shift from non-intervention to CIB-focused moderation and subsequent retreat. [[Gattermann2025-yx]] links far-right electoral performance directly to voter disinformation concern, while [[Gaber2022-bk]] theorizes "strategic lying" (Brexit, 2019 UK election) as an evolution of PR spin exploiting the fact that rebuttal often amplifies the original lie.

### Generative AI and the epistemics of the "post-fact" era

The newest layer of theory grapples with generative AI's destabilization of factuality itself. [[Emilio2026-ik]] proposes "synthetic reality" as a four-layer stack (content, identity, interaction, institutions) and names the "Generative AI Paradox": ubiquitous synthetic media may cause societies to rationally discount all digital evidence, raising the "cost of truth." [[Dierickx2026-tw]] responds epistemologically, arguing traditional fact categories (evidence-based, interpretative, rule-based) fail for AI outputs and proposing "emergent facts" as a new category requiring relational rather than binary assessment. Empirically, [[Hameleers2026-mc]], [[Choi2026-bz]], and [[Wack2026-bt]] test how AI-generated and deepfake content actually performs relative to text and low-tech video decontextualization, generally finding that fears of unprecedented AI persuasive power are overstated relative to context-bound, audience-negotiated effects—directly consonant with [[Frischlich2025-vn]]'s broader argument that virus/warfare/pollution analogies for misinformation each capture only partial, single-level dynamics and obscure the complex, multilevel adaptive system in which AI and older manipulation forms coexist. [[Kasianenko2026-tn]] shows this dynamic operating in search infrastructure itself, where AI Overviews unevenly moderate established versus emergent conspiracy theories.

### Interventions and their theoretical presuppositions

Finally, applied countermeasure research both draws on and tests this theoretical apparatus. [[Szabo2026-rd]] and [[Ramos2026-qo]] extend inoculation theory beyond static content toward conversational and argument-level interventions, the latter explicitly arguing that fact-checking must be supplemented by narrative monitoring and argument-checking to counter disinformation's narrative resilience—closing the loop back to Marwick and Sadler's narrative-theoretic critiques. [[Jaidka2026-sl34]] reframes misinformation susceptibility as a problem of elite-centered exposure architecture rather than individual trait, showing that blocking political elites causally improves belief accuracy—a structural intervention consonant with Bechmann's infrastructural framing. [[Mahl2026-hc]]'s Delphi study synthesizes much of this fragmentation explicitly, finding expert consensus that no single-level fix (individual literacy, fact-checking, platform governance) suffices and that conspiracy theories and misinformation, though conceptually distinct, must be treated as practically intertwined—a fitting summary claim for a topic whose collective trajectory has been a steady move from discrete-falsehood models toward multilevel, narrative-, network-, and infrastructure-sensitive theories of information disorder. At the margins, essays like [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Swartz2026-zb]] gesture toward an even broader reconceptualization, suggesting that "social media" and "scam" themselves may be the more apt organizing categories for the platform dynamics that generate information disorder in the first place, while foundational work like [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] reminds the field that even the term "platform" was never neutral, but always already doing discursive and political work that shapes what counts as manipulation, moderation, and truth.
