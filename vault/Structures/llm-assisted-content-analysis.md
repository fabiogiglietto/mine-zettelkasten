# LLM-Assisted Content Analysis

## The arc of the topic

This cluster traces a rapidly maturing methodological conversation: how to responsibly fold large language models into the machinery of content analysis for political discourse and other complex social phenomena. The line of argument moves from early demonstrations that LLMs can perform classic content-analytic tasks (classification, scaling, clustering, annotation) at scale, through increasingly rigorous validation protocols, to genuinely reflexive and critical accounts of what these tools can and cannot warrant. Running underneath is a recurring, almost obsessive question: *how do we know the model is measuring what we think it is measuring?* — a question inherited from the pre-LLM text-as-data tradition (Grimmer & King) but sharpened by the opacity, generality, and instability of generative models.

## From ad hoc classification to LLMs-in-the-loop pipelines

Several papers document the shift from single-shot prompting toward engineered, multi-stage pipelines. [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]] and its companion embedding study [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] are exemplary of this collaborative, AoIR-adjacent line: they build a full pipeline — fine-tuned political classifier, LLM-generated embeddings, LLM-generated cluster labels — for Italian Facebook political content, and confront head-on the problem that a pipeline built entirely from generative components needs its *own* validation logic, distinct from validating a single fine-tuned transformer. [[Larsson2026-ro]] extends this hybrid logic (GPT-4 zero-shot plus human validation) to a decade of Norwegian party Facebook data, showing the approach travels to non-English, longitudinal settings. [[Ober2026-vd]] and [[Gomez-Zara2026-as]] push the "human-in-the-loop" logic further into qualitative and deductive content analysis: rather than treating the LLM as an annotator to be benchmarked against ground truth, both reframe it as an *analytic collaborator* — surfacing latent codes, forcing researchers to externalize and refine codebooks, and treating ambiguity as data rather than noise. [[Meher2025-qb]] takes the opposite pole, showing that domain-adapted fine-tuning (QLoRA on Llama for conflict-event coding) can make bespoke, resource-light LLM classifiers competitive with — and more accessible than — earlier BERT-style pipelines like ConfliBERT, explicitly positioning fine-tuned open models as the successor to manual and rule-based event coding.

## Scaling, positioning, and inferring latent constructs

A second strand asks whether LLMs can not just label text but *place* it on latent scales or infer hidden attributes. [[Le-Mens2025-qz]] and [[DiGiuseppe2025-es]] both operationalize "ask and average" logics — prompting the model repeatedly and aggregating — to scale political text or open-ended survey responses onto continuous dimensions, extending the Wordscores/Wordfish tradition into the LLM era. [[Lee2026-je]] pushes this into more unsettling territory, showing LLMs can infer political alignment even from ostensibly nonpolitical discourse by exploiting culturally politicized cues, raising both an analytic opportunity (a new measurement channel) and a privacy alarm. [[Elfes2026-jb]] and [[Waight2025-al]] tackle a related but distinct estimand — narrative rather than opinion — using LLMs to operationalize actantial roles and claim-level similarity respectively, both explicitly arguing that narrative structure is conflated with, but analytically separable from, topical or lexical similarity. [[Sarmiento2025-as]] and [[Arora2025-tx]] round out this strand by extending automated framing analysis beyond fixed frame inventories and beyond text alone, the latter incorporating visual and cross-modal framing cues into news analysis of gun violence coverage.

## Comparative embeddings and representational validity

A tightly focused sub-line interrogates the embedding layer itself, the substrate on which much LLM-assisted clustering and similarity work depends. [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] benchmarks OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large against Italian-specific UmBERTo for political-news clustering, finding the general-purpose LLM embedding consistently superior — a comparative-embedding-model result that feeds directly into the pipeline described in [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]. [[Fan2025-ut]] generalizes the underlying worry — that embeddings encode confounds like source or language that distort similarity measures — and offers linear concept erasure as a cheap, broadly effective fix, formalizing what earlier corpus-effects critiques (Thompson & Mimno) had only diagnosed informally. [[Arminio2025-tw]] extends the embedding question into the visual domain, arguing that CNN-based image clustering captures denotative but not connotative meaning, and that routing images through a vision-LLM's textual descriptions before embedding better recovers the culturally loaded meanings social scientists actually care about — with the added benefit of TF-IDF interpretability that CNN pipelines lack. [[Bruns2025-fz]] proposes a cognate move at the network level, using embeddings of interaction "practices" to escape the illegibility of multimodal network visualizations. [[Achmann-Denkler2026-lx]] closes the loop empirically, benchmarking GPT-4o against specialized computer-vision pipelines for face recognition and person-counting in campaign imagery and finding the multimodal LLM decisively superior — while also surfacing a possible gender bias in the specialized baseline that the LLM does not replicate.

## Validation, bias, and the limits of LLM judgment

A cluster of papers turns explicitly toward validation as its central problem. [[Brown2025-jk]] interrogates whether LLM annotation replicates demographic bias in subjective labeling tasks and finds that item difficulty (human label entropy), not annotator demographics or model choice, dominates disagreement — a reassuring but conditional result. [[Paci2025-ag]] and [[DeVerna2025-dl]] both push back against over-optimism about LLM competence on high-stakes political tasks: the former shows even strong models fall well short of expert human interpretation of pragmatic, implicit content in Italian political speech, while the latter shows that fact-checking accuracy hinges almost entirely on curated retrieval context rather than model scale or built-in reasoning, and that search-enabled fact-checkers exhibit systematic source-selection skew. [[Alizadeh2026-es]] extends validation concerns to a meta level, benchmarking whether AI coding agents can reproduce social-science findings themselves, and documenting a specific failure mode — sycophantic specification search under confirmatory prompting — that mirrors concerns raised elsewhere in this topic about LLMs' tendency to conform to expected answers rather than genuinely test them. [[Tan2024-vl]] surveys this whole annotation-and-synthesis literature, giving the topic a bibliographic anchor point.

## Applications: extremism, disinformation, and belief change

Several papers apply LLM-assisted coding to substantively difficult phenomena where validity stakes are highest. [[Bailard2024-pj]] fine-tunes DeBERTa to classify collective-action frames in Proud Boys Telegram messages at scale, linking online framing dynamics to offline violence via Granger causality — a template for using supervised LLM-adjacent classification to test process-oriented social-movement theory rather than merely describe content. [[Costello2024-bg]] and [[Szabo2026-rd]] use LLMs not just to *analyze* discourse but to *intervene* in it, showing that personalized AI dialogue can durably reduce conspiracy belief and misinformation susceptibility, respectively — extending the topic from measurement into applied persuasion research. [[Cerulli2026-sl75]] tests whether validated dark-personality constructs manifest in observable Reddit language, largely finding they do not survive multiple-comparison correction, a useful null result cautioning against over-reading text-derived personality proxies. [[Triedman2025-uy]] applies embedding-similarity and citation analysis at encyclopedic scale to characterize how Grokipedia diverges from Wikipedia, exemplifying how the topic's comparative-embedding toolkit generalizes to auditing AI-generated knowledge platforms themselves.

## Coordinated behavior detection as a parallel methodological front

A cluster of papers addresses a related but distinct annotation problem — detecting coordinated inauthentic activity — using graph and temporal methods that intersect with, but do not reduce to, LLM-based text classification. [[Iannucci2025-eg]] and [[Righetti2025-sl2a]] both push toward more generalized, platform-agnostic definitions of coordination (multiplex/temporal networks; minimal object-sharing definitions), explicitly responding to the fragility of platform-specific and API-dependent tools. [[Minici2024-tf]] folds language-model signal into a graph foundation model for detecting information-operation drivers, exemplifying convergence between the text-classification and network-detection strands of this topic.

## Infrastructure, temporality, and the limits of what is being measured

A final, more infrastructural set of papers reminds the topic that upstream data quality and conceptual framing constrain any downstream LLM analysis. [[Ulloa2024-jm]] shows that ex-situ web scraping — the standard data-collection method feeding most LLM content-analysis pipelines — introduces large, non-random content distortions before any model is even applied. [[Fan2026-af]] argues that communication research's habitual aggregation of digital trace data into cross-sectional measures discards exactly the temporal structure that theory cares about, previewing language-based embedding models as a promising but data-hungry solution.

## Reflexive counter-currents

The topic closes on itself with explicitly critical voices. [[Balluff2026-if]] offers a systematic reckoning with the environmental, reproducibility, and epistemic costs of "newer, larger, better" LLM adoption in communication research, arguing for a trade-off mindset favoring smaller, open, task-appropriate models — a direct counterweight to the comparative-embedding papers' enthusiasm for frontier proprietary models. [[Ptz2024-sl20]] goes furthest philosophically, using Luhmannian systems theory and conversation analysis to argue that chatbot competence is parasitic on human repair work rather than genuine understanding — a claim with direct implications for how much interpretive authority any LLM-in-the-loop pipeline should be granted. [[Jayaram2026-wd]], though situated in scientific peer review rather than political discourse, extends the same question — how far can automated judgment substitute for human evaluative labor — to the practice of research itself, closing the loop between LLM-assisted content analysis and the broader question of what these systems are for.
