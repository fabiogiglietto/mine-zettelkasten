# Platform Data Access & Governance

## The infrastructure question, reframed

Research on social media has always depended on what platforms will allow to be seen. The papers in this collection track a decisive turn: from a period in which "seeing" was largely negotiated informally between platforms and researchers, to one in which access is being (unevenly, contentiously) codified as a legal right. [[Yang2026-tq]] supplies the macro-empirical baseline for this shift, showing across 59 journals that the share of social-science papers using social media data tripled from 2010 through the early 2020s before plateauing and declining in 2022–2024 — a decline it attributes directly to API restrictions. [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] and Bastos2025-ya narrate the Twitter/X case in detail: a "Golden Age" of open and Academic APIs (2006–2022) followed by a 2023 pay-to-play regime whose $42,000/month pricing produced a measurable, quantifiable contraction in scholarly output. [[Freelon2024-sc]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]] generalize this into periodizations spanning six platforms, arguing that data access has become "permanently contingent" — a "post-API age" (or, in Giglietto and Puschmann's phrase, a move "from the Wild West to the Walled Garden") in which platforms can grant or revoke access at will, shaped by scandal, leadership change, and commercial calculation rather than principled commitment to research.

## CrowdTangle's afterlife and the "clean room" turn

Meta's shutdown of CrowdTangle is the collection's recurring case study in what happens when a semi-open tool disappears. [[Cullen2026-cb]] *[Cullen2026-cb]* develops the "Seeing like an API" framework from interviews with CrowdTangle users, arguing the tool's technical design and governance rules actively shaped — and constrained — what researchers could observe and conclude, well before its formal discontinuation. Its successor infrastructures receive sharp scrutiny: [[Bruns2026-pn]] documents how the Meta Content Library's "clean room" model, while addressing legitimate privacy concerns, privileges code-fluent quantitative researchers, forecloses longitudinal and cross-platform work through mandatory data deletion, and widens inequities between well-resourced and Majority World institutions. Yet the same infrastructure is put to productive use elsewhere: [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] mines 2.5 million Meta Content Library posts to detect the actual rollout timeline of Meta's political content reduction policy, finding it began roughly ten months before Meta's announced date and was never disclosed in DSA transparency reports — a finding only possible, the author stresses, because of DSA-enabled access, even as it exposes Meta's transparency deficits. This tension — new access regimes as both constraint and opportunity — runs through [[Lukito2026-nb]], which catalogs 72 data-access tools and identifies a triad of "price, proficiency, or permission" barriers, and [[Zheng2026-bi]], whose TubeStats/TokStats tools sidestep official APIs entirely by exploiting platform ID schemes to build representative random samples in the absence of adequate sanctioned mechanisms.

## The URL Shares Dataset as instrument and artifact

A distinct thread interrogates one specific Meta-provided resource in forensic detail. [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] shows that the 100-public-shares anonymization threshold in the URL Shares Dataset produces near-identical monthly trajectories across 46 disparate countries — a platform-wide artifact (likely tied to Feed ranking changes) that can masquerade as substantive cross-national convergence, a serious warning for any comparative or longitudinal use of the dataset. [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] uses the same dataset (paired with NewsGuard ratings) to extend Guess et al.'s US findings to Germany, France, and Italy, finding rising untrustworthy-URL shares in election years but stable View proportions — while explicitly flagging the dataset's US-skewed source coverage as a limit on cross-national comparison. The successor "Privacy-Protected Full URLs Dataset" receives its own audit in [[Giglietto2026-632ef967]], which shows sharing-to-viewing amplification is not a stable structural constant but fluctuates with Meta's known governance interventions (the 2020 "break the glass" measures), evidence that the platform is an active, temporally variable curator rather than a neutral conduit — a claim that directly complicates the assumptions underlying the Meta-2020 election studies. [[Bergeron-Boutin2026-yh]] closes this thread with platform-wide 2020 Facebook/Instagram data showing untrustworthy-source exposure was low on average but concentrated among a subset of users, with no detectable attitudinal effects from experimentally reducing it — a sobering empirical counterpoint to alarmist misinformation narratives, made possible only by privileged platform-provided data.

## The DSA as regulatory experiment, and its friction

A dense cluster of papers assesses Article 40 of the EU Digital Services Act as the field's most consequential live regulatory intervention. [[Ohme2026-nv]], [[de-Vreese2026-zx]], and [[Pierri2025-hm]] together offer a "state of the marathon" assessment: access is a binding legal right in principle, but implementation is beset by opaque procedures, inconsistent timelines, and platform strategic ambiguity — friction that de Vreese and Tromble insist should be read as evidence the regulation is targeting real power asymmetries, not proof of its failure. [[Peters2026-mo]] shows how "data quality," ostensibly a methodological matter, became a contested discursive object in the Delegated Regulation's drafting, with academics and NGOs fighting (partially successfully) to get it recognized against platform resistance — Meta notably inverting the argument to claim access itself was futile. Platform-specific audits substantiate the skepticism: [[Entrena-Serrano2025-gw]] and [[Ohme2026-nv]] on TikTok's Research API, [[Rieder2025-ju]] on YouTube's "forgetful by design" search endpoint, and [[Tonneau2025-bv]] on the stark cross-lingual disparities in DSA-mandated moderator-allocation transparency data all converge on a single finding: formal compliance does not guarantee substantive research usability. [[Jurg2025-ur]] extends this into a content-moderation audit of YouTube's election-period behavior, arguing for reconceiving transparency as "observability" — attending to the infrastructural conditions of knowledge production rather than treating disclosure as self-evidently sufficient. [[Ahuja2025-ku]] and [[Bouchaud2026-lr]] show DSA provisions (Article 25's dark-pattern prohibitions; profiling concerns) being operationalized as analytic frameworks in their own right, while [[Schulte2026-df]] and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] use German and cross-platform election data to argue that the deeper problem is the absence of a reusable, standardized research infrastructure for multi-platform observability, rather than a simple lack of studies.

## Industry entanglement and the limits of collaboration

A separate but tightly related line interrogates the epistemics of platform-academic collaboration itself. [[Munger2025-cz]] delivers the collection's central methodological critique via the Meta2020 partnership: even the best-resourced, most consensual collaboration produced narrow estimates with poor external and "poetic" validity, because platforms change faster than social science can characterize them — a structural argument for proactive disclosure mandates rather than reactive study. [[Allen2025-ot]] responds by valorizing "platform-independent" experimental methods (browser-extension LLM reranking) as a necessary adaptation to the closing data environment. [[Heiss2026-qv]], [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]], and [[Bak-Coleman2026-mk]] escalate this into a systematic critique of industry capture: the latter's bibliometric analysis of Science/Nature/PNAS papers finds roughly half have undisclosed industry ties, concentrated among a small elite of repeatedly-engaged authors and editors, with an estimated 80% "industrial saturation" once reviewers are included — directly paralleling tobacco- and pharma-industry research capture. [[McNally2025-dn]] pushes back against the "black box" framing itself, showing Facebook's News Feed algorithm's effects are empirically detectable through lagged engagement signals, and argues this detectability is precisely what makes DSA-mandated systemic audits (Article 40(4)) feasible in principle.

## Content moderation retreat and the political stakes of visibility

The final arc concerns what platforms actually do with the moderation power researchers are fighting to observe — and how that power is narrating, and retreating from, itself. [[Hurcombe2025-cs]] dissects Meta's Newsroom discourse as a strategic externalization of blame ("inauthenticity," "enforcement") that occludes the platform's own architectural role, language ("coordinated inauthentic behaviour") that has since diffused into regulatory texts themselves — a discursive precursor to the empirical retreat documented by [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]] and [[Cazzamatta2026-lo]], who track platforms' shift from civic-integrity expansion (2020–2022) to policy rollback and "conflictual cooperation" with fact-checkers after 2022. [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] shows this rollback operationalized at the definitional level: Meta's narrowing of "coordinated inauthentic behaviour" to only "adversarial threat actors" has let a wide range of manufactured-attention practices continue unchecked. [[Giglietto2025-1765bb4f]] and [[Simeone2025-vo]] supply causal, dataset-driven case studies of the consequences: political content reduction suppressed mainstream politicians' reach while extremist accounts compensated through volume, and targeted deplatforming produced network-wide "ripple effects" disproportionate to the accounts actually removed. [[Bouchaud2026-np]], [[Moran2025-qn]], [[Donovan2025-ws]], and [[Karo2026-dn]] extend the moderation-retreat story to Community Notes, the collapse of the "trust and safety" profession, and platform-specific evasion tactics (jihadist content on TikTok), while [[Crosset2026-mq]] offers the broadest theoretical frame, arguing that across EU and US legislation, content moderation has become a form of "security bureaucratisation" — governance of information flows through administrative procedure rather than direct content control. Read together with [[Bechmann2026-dr]]'s call to study platforms as democratic infrastructure rather than sites of isolated causal effects, and [[Helmond2026-ll]]'s essay on platformization's shift toward an "AI-centered" phase beyond the social graph altogether, this cluster suggests the object of study is itself mutating faster than the access regimes built to observe it — the abiding condition, and provocation, of this entire literature.
