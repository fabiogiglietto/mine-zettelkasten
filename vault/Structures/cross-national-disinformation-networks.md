# Cross-National Disinformation Networks

## The View Beyond Italy

Where other clusters in this archive trace disinformation's Italian case study in fine grain, this set widens the aperture to ask whether the mechanisms identified there — coordinated amplification, platform-specific propaganda, elite strategic falsehood — travel across political systems, media regimes, and levels of democratic consolidation. The answer that emerges is neither "yes, universally" nor "no, every country is sui generis," but something more interesting: a set of recurring structural logics (brokerage, laundering, platform-specific affordance exploitation) that manifest differently depending on a country's media system, regime type, and position in the global information order.

## Brazil as the Deep Case

Brazil functions almost as a second home base in this collection, rivaling Italy in density of study, and the papers here trace a near-complete arc of the country's information crisis. [[Inacio-da-Silva2026-zf]] opens the timeline with 2018-era volunteer auditing of Facebook ads, already worried about targeted-ad abuse migrating south from the US 2016 experience. By the pandemic, [[Rodarte2026-dk]] shows Brazilian parliamentarians themselves becoming media producers, contesting journalistic gatekeeping through derivative reporting, authority claims, and local brokerage during the Manaus collapse — a dynamic extended by [[Rodarte2026-sl16]], which finds that platform engagement metrics now actively punish parliamentarians for talking about legislative work and reward antagonism, producing a genuine "democratic cost" to virality-seeking. The 2022 election cycle generates the thickest empirical seam: [[Rossini2026-jn]] and [[Rossini2026-mj]] use panel surveys to show that belief in electoral fraud claims independently erodes both political tolerance and trust in results, with messaging apps as a key indirect vector; [[Kansaon2025-id]] and [[Ventura2025-sw]] push into WhatsApp itself, showing coordinated networks mobilizing real-world action (including the January 8 uprising) and causally shaping belief via a deactivation experiment; and [[Marino2026-slef]] complicates the echo-chamber assumption by showing that pro-Bolsonaro affective engagement was volatile rather than stable, cracking open around Lula's inauguration and the coup attempt. [[Ventura2026-yc]]'s study of Brazil's 2024 X ban closes the loop by showing that even state-imposed platform discipline produces partisan "sorting ratchets" rather than depolarization — a sobering coda picked up at the macro level by [[Slack1787858551-sl4d]], which frames Brazil's failed Fake News Bill as exemplary of a broader "privatization of public discourse" in which US platforms out-lobby Global South regulatory sovereignty.

## Authoritarian Machinery: Russia, Belarus, Serbia, China, Korea

A second cluster examines how authoritarian and hybrid regimes build and operate coordination infrastructure directly. [[Poliakoff2026-fa]]'s OSINT reconstruction of the St Petersburg IRA via leaked CVs is foundational here, revealing the IRA as an outsourced, labor-market-embedded propaganda firm rather than a shadowy intelligence cell — a finding that complicates simple state-attribution narratives. [[Kuznetsova2025-nu]] and [[Kulichkina2025-sl09]] extend this to Telegram and Twitter respectively, showing pro-regime channels coordinating to amplify narratives and pro-government accounts engaging in modular, team-based "connective counteraction" against Navalny-aligned protest. [[Kulichkina2026-zk]] generalizes the dual-use insight to China's COVID protests, where the same platform infrastructure enables both mobilization and repression. [[Jovanovic-Harrington2026-ze]] pushes the typology further with Serbia's SNS, arguing that mimetic, decentralized CIB is a harder-to-detect evolution beyond the centralized bot-farm model — a claim that resonates with [[Makeev2026-ma]]'s theoretical account of dual-track (domestic/foreign) wartime propaganda in Russia. [[Keller2019-nk]]'s ground-truthed South Korean NIS case remains a methodological anchor for the whole cluster, showing that coordination (not bot-like automation) is the real signature of astroturfing — and that even well-resourced campaigns may have limited measurable discourse effects, a conclusion echoed decades later by [[Eady2023-xg]]'s finding that IRA exposure on US Twitter was concentrated among already-partisan users and uncorrelated with attitude change. [[Kim2026-br]]'s two-decade Naver News analysis of Korean trolling complicates the "foreign praise" model further, finding that moral condemnation — not promotion — is suspected trolls' most effective and most engaged-with rhetorical strategy.

## Brokerage, Infrastructure, and the Global South

A distinct comparative thread treats influence operations not as content phenomena but as political-economic infrastructure. [[Gaw2025-ru]]'s interviews with Philippine IO operatives reconceive influence campaigns as a form of brokerage between political clients and voters, a frame that dovetails with [[Beacken2026-zb]]'s comparative survey of generative-AI propaganda across Bolivia, Hungary, India, Nigeria, Tunisia, and Turkey, which insists that GenAI's political uses are structurally contingent rather than technologically determined. [[Wack2026-bt]]'s large Kenyan survey experiment on deepfakes adds an audience-side corrective: a synthetic video's persuasive power is not fixed at production but "collectively sensemade" through surrounding comments, with partisanship moderating whether skepticism or credulity wins out — a crucial corrective for any theory assuming deepfakes operate uniformly across media ecosystems. Together these papers insist that Global South disinformation research needs its own theoretical vocabulary (brokerage, infrastructure, collective sensemaking) rather than imported Global North templates, a point also central to [[Tonneau2025-bv]]'s audit showing that content moderation workforces systematically underserve Spanish, Portuguese, and Arabic relative to English — the resourcing asymmetry that makes much of this activity possible in the first place.

## Germany and the European Electoral Laboratory

Germany anchors the European comparative strand. [[Rothut2026-or]] traces how the Querdenken protest movement became a connective tissue mainstreaming far-right and conspiracist ideas on Telegram, while [[Klinger2023-qu]] documents an earlier, parallel dynamic: intermediary networks "laundering" Identitarian anti-migration content until the AfD could adopt it without direct coordination. [[Kakavand2026-kt]] demonstrates that the German far right does not reproduce a single network across platforms but adapts tactics to each platform's distinct affordances, a finding given sharp empirical teeth by [[Tjaden2026-sl8e]]'s sock-puppet audit showing AfD content structurally advantaged in TikTok's incidental-exposure feed. [[Schulte2026-df]]'s account of the 2025 federal election reframes much of this as an infrastructure problem: cross-platform comparison is bottlenecked less by theory than by inconsistent data access, a concern shared by [[Slack1783507532-sl10]]'s preliminary look at coordinated ad behavior ahead of the 2021 vote. Zooming out to the EU level, [[Iris2026-pg]], [[Gattermann2025-yx]], [[Oprea2025-lf]], [[Jurg2025-ur]], and [[Bouchafra2026-ts]] collectively map the 2024 European Parliament elections: disproportionate radical-right media visibility across five countries, a link between far-right electoral strength and disinformation anxiety, Romanian hyperactive-user amplification networks, YouTube's inconsistent authority-labeling, and the Sweden Democrats' platform-tailored securitizing visuals on TikTok. [[Tornberg2025-ir]]'s 26-country parliamentary Twitter analysis supplies the connective theoretical claim binding this strand together: it is not populism or right-wing ideology alone but their *interaction* — radical-right populism specifically — that predicts elite misinformation sharing.

## Transnational Media Ecosystems and Comparative Frameworks

A final set of papers steps back to ask how national information disorders become transnational infrastructure, and how to measure resilience comparatively. [[Copland2025-em]] and [[Groebner2026-pc]] trace how Sky News Australia and allied Anglophone hyperpartisan outlets (Fox, Rebel News, GB News) build genuinely transnational audiences and narrative pipelines centered on the US right, while [[Heft2021-ky]] finds that right-wing alternative sites hyperlink into — rather than away from — the mainstream media environment, undercutting the insulated-echo-chamber thesis. [[Righetti2025-slf9]] shows the same transnational logic animating anti-gender mobilization via CitizenGO's centrally coordinated multilingual network. Measurement and methods papers knit the whole topic together: [[Humprecht2020-gd]] offers the foundational macro-structural framework for comparative resilience across eighteen Western democracies; [[Nizzoli2020-cf]] and [[Appel2026-qr]] refine how coordination and exposure should be measured rather than assumed; [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] extends US news-quality metrics to the European newsfeed; and [[Waight2025-al]] and [[Gerard2025-br]] supply new LLM- and embedding-based tools for tracking narrative diffusion and bridge users across languages and platforms where old interaction-based methods fail. Rounding out the picture, [[Wang2025-zy]], [[Richter2026-bt]], and [[Mota2026-sl59]] push the comparative lens onto an adjacent but rapidly converging frontier — national AI discourse and sentiment — suggesting that the infrastructural, elite-driven, and platform-specific dynamics mapped here for disinformation are already being reproduced in how societies narrate AI itself, and may become the next terrain on which cross-national comparative disinformation research must operate.
