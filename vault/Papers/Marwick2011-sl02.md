# I tweet honestly, I tweet passionately: Twitter users, context collapse, and the imagined audience

> Marwick, A. E., & danah boyd (2011). I tweet honestly, I tweet passionately: Twitter users, context collapse, and the imagined audience. *New Media & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444810365313
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444810365313)

## Summary

Marwick and boyd investigate how Twitter users manage the problem of "context collapse" — the flattening of otherwise distinct social audiences (friends, family, colleagues, strangers) into a single stream where none can be directly seen or known. Drawing on 226 responses from 181 Twitter users, they argue that users cope by conjuring an *imagined audience* that guides their linguistic, topical, and self-presentational choices. The paper develops the concept of the "networked audience" as a hybrid of the writer's imagined reader and the mass broadcast audience, and shows that authenticity on Twitter is not an intrinsic property but a strategically managed performance, frequently defined in opposition to the micro-celebrity and personal branding practices that users nevertheless adopt.

## Key Contributions

- Articulates **context collapse** as a defining condition of many-to-many social media communication.
- Introduces the **networked audience** as a new audience model distinct from both writerly and broadcast conceptions.
- Extends Goffman's dramaturgical framework and Meyrowitz's situationism into networked platforms.
- Offers an empirically grounded typology of audience-management tactics: self-censorship, targeting, balancing personal/professional, and imagining an "ideal reader" or "nightmare reader."
- Frames authenticity itself as a socially constructed, context-dependent performance intertwined with — and rhetorically opposed to — micro-celebrity and personal branding.

## Methods

Qualitative, interpretive study conducted *on* Twitter: the authors posted open-ended questions from their own accounts and sent @reply queries to a purposive sample spanning the public timeline, the 249 most-followed accounts, and users with 1,000–15,000 followers. They analyzed 226 responses from 181 users received via DMs and @replies, identifying high-profile respondents by name and anonymizing others. Analysis drew on symbolic interactionism and Goffman's impression management rather than statistical generalization.

## Findings

- Respondents imagined their audience variably as friends, fans, themselves, an ideal reader, or a tweet-specific situational audience.
- Highly-followed users described audiences pragmatically as communities or tribes and used tools like hashtags and Twittersheep to segment content.
- Self-censorship clustered around sex, family conflict, workplace grievances, religion, and politics — often triggered by an imagined "nightmare reader" (parent, boss, partner).
- Popular users mixed personal and professional tweets, treating disclosure as a strategic marker of humanness that shored up authenticity.
- Micro-celebrity practices were widely adopted but also widely disparaged as inauthentic, producing a persistent tension.
- Some users rejected the notion of an audience altogether, framing "tweeting for oneself" as itself a marker of authenticity — though still operating within networked visibility.
