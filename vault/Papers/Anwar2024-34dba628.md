# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2023) that study user engagement with Facebook's Reactions (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in 2016 as an expansion of the Like button. Organizing the corpus into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — the authors argue that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that carry richer affective meaning than Likes while remaining low-effort. Across studies, a consistent pattern emerges: lifestyle and entertainment content elicits predominantly positive reactions, whereas political and controversial content draws a broader, more negative emotional spectrum, with anger in particular driving higher engagement — a dynamic especially pronounced for populist and far-right actors.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research spanning 2016–2023 across political, social, health, and business domains.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the role of negative affect (especially anger) in amplifying engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absence of unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, underexplored event-based and tabloid contexts.
- Agenda-setting suggestions, including LLM-assisted categorization of reaction patterns and applications in misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism research.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, locating studies, selection and appraisal, analysis, and reporting. Searches on Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines were run monthly (July–September 2023) using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators, limited to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023 (n = 64). Each article was coded thematically using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method, then grouped into three top-level categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection, etc.).

## Findings

- The 64 articles break down into 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics/far-right groups, and 39 on other social issues.
- In electoral contexts (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament), negative emotional appeals — especially anger — outperformed positive appeals in engagement and sharing.
- Populist and far-right leaders disproportionately attract "Angry" and "Haha" reactions relative to mainstream politicians; anger-triggering posts boost circulation.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news exhibit a bivalent emotional logic: Love and Anger rarely co-occur, while Anger co-occurs with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions have been operationalized as proxies for emotion classification, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment polarity, though reaction-to-emotion mappings remain ambiguous — Haha and Wow can invert meaning in political contexts.
- Reactions serve as viable sentiment labels for low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle/entertainment content skews positive; sociopolitical content spans a wider, more negative range.
- Cultural context matters: audiences of China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral outlets in Hong Kong show distinct reaction profiles.
