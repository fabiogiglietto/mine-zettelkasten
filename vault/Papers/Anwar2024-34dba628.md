# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *FRONTIERS IN SOCIOLOGY*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2023) on Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in 2016 as an emotional expansion of the Like button. Anwar and Giglietto organize the fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — and derive cross-study patterns via meta-analytic reading. Their central argument is that Reactions function as paralinguistic digital affordances that reveal systematically different affective profiles by content type: lifestyle content skews positive, while political and controversial content — especially from populist and far-right actors — draws broader and more negative emotional responses, with anger consistently driving higher engagement and sharing.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research from 2016 to 2023 across politics, health, business, and other social domains.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes a heterogeneous, cross-disciplinary literature into three coherent clusters and finer sub-topics.
- Identification of a recurring cross-study regularity: negative emotional content, especially anger, systematically boosts engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological fragmentation — inconsistent data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and unstable reaction-to-emotion mappings.
- An agenda for future work, including LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation and digital activism studies.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's five-step systematic review protocol: formulating questions, searching, selecting, analyzing, and reporting. Searches were conducted in Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines between July and September 2023, using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators. Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023, yielding 64 items. Each article was then coded via Armstrong's comparative naturalistic method by research question, results, and conclusions, and grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Distribution of the corpus: 6 articles on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics and far-right groups, and 39 on other social issues.
- Negative emotional appeals — particularly anger — outperform positive content for engagement and sharing across studies of Mexican, Brexit, and EU Parliament campaigns.
- Populist and far-right leaders attract disproportionately more Angry and Haha reactions than mainstream politicians; anger-triggering posts are more widely shared.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news exhibit a bivalent affective logic: Love and Angry rarely co-occur, while Angry clusters with Sad and Wow.
- Reactions can operationalize controversy (via entropy of the reaction distribution), sentiment polarity, and emotion classes, though Haha and Wow are semantically ambiguous — Wow can signal disbelief in political contexts.
- Reactions serve as useful sentiment labels for low-resource languages such as Sinhala and Bangla.
- Lifestyle and entertainment posts predominantly elicit positive reactions; sociopolitical posts elicit a wider, more negative spectrum.
- Cultural context matters, as shown by divergent reaction patterns among China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral audiences in Hong Kong.
