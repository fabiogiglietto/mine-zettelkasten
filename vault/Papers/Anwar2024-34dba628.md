# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *FRONTIERS IN SOCIOLOGY*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed studies published between 2016 and 2023 on Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in February 2016 as an emotional expansion of the "Like" button. Framing Reactions as paralinguistic digital affordances that convey nuanced affect at low cognitive cost, the authors organize the fragmented literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues (psychological, healthcare, business, and miscellaneous) — and derive cross-study regularities via comparative naturalistic coding. The central argument is that Reactions offer a valuable, if methodologically underexploited, window onto affective engagement online, with sociopolitical content consistently eliciting broader and more negative emotional profiles than lifestyle or entertainment content, and negative emotion (especially anger) reliably amplifying engagement for populist and far-right actors.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research from 2016 to 2023 across political, social, health, and business domains.
- A thematic taxonomy that organizes an interdisciplinary and fragmented body of work into navigable categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the role of anger and negative valence in boosting engagement with populist and hyperpartisan content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absent unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparability, and underexplored event-based/tabloid topics.
- A research agenda pointing toward LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: question formulation, literature search, study selection, analysis, and reporting. Searches were conducted monthly from July to September 2023 across Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines using Boolean combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction"-related terms. Inclusion was restricted to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings (2016–2023), yielding 64 articles, which were then thematically coded using Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method and grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics.

## Findings

- Distribution of the corpus: 6 articles on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics and far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- In political contexts (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), negatively charged appeals — especially anger — generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders draw disproportionately more "Angry" and "Haha" reactions than mainstream politicians; anger-triggering messages amplify sharing.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news exhibit a bivalent affective logic: "Love" and "Angry" rarely co-occur, while "Angry" typically co-occurs with "Sad" and "Wow".
- Reactions have been used computationally to predict emotion, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment, though "Haha" and "Wow" carry ambiguous valence in political contexts.
- Cross-linguistic work (Sinhala, Bangla) shows Reactions functioning as sentiment labels for low-resource languages.
- Lifestyle/entertainment content skews positive; sociopolitical content spans a wider, more negative emotional spectrum.
- Cultural context matters: Hong Kong studies show distinct reaction profiles across China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral outlets.
