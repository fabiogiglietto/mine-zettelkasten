# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles (2016–2023) studying user engagement with Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in February 2016 as an expansion of the Like button. The authors conceptualize Reactions as *paralinguistic digital affordances* that convey nuanced emotional meaning at low cognitive cost, and organize the fragmented literature into three thematic clusters: the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues (psychological/emotional, healthcare, business, and miscellaneous). A central through-line is that emotionally charged content—especially anger-eliciting political and populist messaging—drives disproportionate engagement regardless of valence, while lifestyle and entertainment content skews positive.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research across politics, social issues, health, and business.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a scattered, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories for future researchers.
- Identification of cross-study regularities, notably the role of negative emotion in driving engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: no unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexplored event-based/tabloid topics.
- Future directions including LLM-assisted categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation, fact-checking, and digital activism.

## Methods

Five-step systematic review following Briner and Denyer (2012): formulating questions, finding, selecting/evaluating, analyzing, and reporting. Database searches (Scopus, Google Scholar, and others) were run monthly July–September 2023 using "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators, limited to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023. The resulting 64 articles were thematically coded via a comparative naturalistic method and grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Of 64 articles: 6 on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics and far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), anger-based appeals generated significantly more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders elicit disproportionately more "angry" and "haha" reactions than mainstream politicians; anger-triggering communication boosts sharing.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news follow a bivalent logic: "love" and "anger" rarely co-occur, while "anger" co-occurs with "sad" and "wow."
- Reactions have been used computationally to predict emotion classes, controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and sentiment, though reaction-to-emotion mappings remain ambiguous ("Wow" as disbelief, "Haha" as derision).
- Cross-linguistic studies (Sinhala, Bangla) use Reactions as sentiment labels for low-resource languages.
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts attract positive reactions; sociopolitical posts draw a wider, more negative spectrum, with cultural context shaping distributions.

## Connections

This review consolidates work on reactions and other engagement metrics as proxies for affect and controversy, connecting to studies of engagement signals and their platform logics such as [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and the broader affordance-and-visibility tradition of [[Bucher2017-qh]] and [[Cotter2019-js]]. Its emphasis on negative emotion—particularly anger—driving sharing of political and populist content resonates strongly with work on moral/emotional contagion in [[Brady2026-ln]] and the virality of false and outrage content in [[Vosoughi2018-at]]. The methodological groundwork on engagement data and Facebook metrics by the second author also situates this review alongside [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]] and [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]].
