# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed studies (2016–2023) on Facebook's "Reactions" feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry) to map how these paralinguistic digital affordances have been studied across political and social domains. Organizing the field into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues — the authors argue that Reactions function as low-effort but semantically richer affective cues than the "Like" button, and that their distributional patterns systematically differ across content genres. A central cross-study regularity is that negative emotional content, especially anger, disproportionately drives engagement with political and populist messaging, while lifestyle and entertainment content skews positive.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research from 2016 to 2023, spanning politics, health, business, and psychology.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Identification of cross-study regularities — notably the anger–engagement link in populist and far-right communication.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: no unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexplored event-based/tabloid contexts.
- Agenda-setting for future work, including LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation and digital activism research.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, retrieving studies (Scopus, Google Scholar, and other search engines, July–September 2023, using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction"), selecting peer-reviewed articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023, applying Armstrong's (2010) comparative naturalistic method to code each article by research question, results, and conclusions, and finally grouping the 64 included studies into three major categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Distribution of the corpus: 6 articles on the introduction of Reactions, 19 on politics/far-right groups, 39 on other social issues.
- Negative emotional appeals — particularly anger — outperform positive content in political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections) in generating engagement and shares.
- Populist and far-right leaders receive disproportionately more "Angry" and "Haha" reactions than mainstream politicians.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news exhibit a bivalent emotional logic: "Love" rarely co-occurs with "Anger", while "Anger" clusters with "Sad" and "Wow".
- Reactions have been used computationally to predict emotion classes, quantify controversy (via entropy of reaction distributions), and label sentiment, though "Haha" and "Wow" carry ambiguous political meanings (e.g., "Wow" as disbelief).
- Reactions serve as effective sentiment-analysis labels even in low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Cultural context modulates reaction patterns, e.g., differences between China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral media audiences in Hong Kong.
- Lifestyle/entertainment posts skew positive; sociopolitical posts elicit a broader and more negative emotional spectrum.

## Connections

This review consolidates a body of work that intersects with ongoing research on emotional engagement metrics and platform-mediated political communication, complementing empirical Facebook-based studies of coordinated behavior and problematic information such as [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]]. Its emphasis on anger-driven engagement in populist communication resonates with work on far-right and hyperpartisan dynamics like [[Larsson2026-ro]] and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]], while its methodological framing as digital-methods synthesis situates it alongside platform-affordance and measurement discussions such as [[F2020-6278a4aa]] and [[Iannelli2018-ebd918b7]].
