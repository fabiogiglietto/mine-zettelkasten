# Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review

> Anwar, S., & Giglietto, F. (2024). Facebook reactions in the context of politics and social issues: a systematic literature review. *Frontiers in Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3389/fsoc.2024.1379265)

## Summary

This systematic literature review synthesizes 64 peer-reviewed articles published between 2016 and 2023 on user engagement with Facebook's Reactions feature (Love, Haha, Wow, Sad, Angry), introduced in February 2016 as an emotional expansion of the Like button. Anwar and Giglietto organize the fragmented, interdisciplinary literature into three thematic clusters — the introduction of Reactions, politics and far-right groups, and other social issues (psychological, healthcare, business, miscellaneous) — and identify cross-study regularities. Their central argument is that Reactions operate as paralinguistic digital affordances whose distribution varies systematically by content domain: lifestyle and entertainment content elicits predominantly positive reactions, whereas sociopolitical content draws a broader, more negative emotional spectrum, with emotionally charged (especially angry) posts attracting disproportionate engagement.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive systematic synthesis of Facebook Reactions research from 2016–2023 spanning political, social, health, and business domains.
- A thematic taxonomy organizing a fragmented interdisciplinary literature into coherent categories.
- Cross-study identification of negative emotion — particularly anger — as a consistent driver of engagement with political and populist content.
- Diagnosis of methodological gaps: absence of unified data sources, limited cross-cultural comparison, and underexploration of tabloid and event-based topics.
- Agenda-setting suggestions for future work, including LLM-based categorization of reaction patterns and applications to misinformation and digital activism.

## Methods

The authors follow Briner and Denyer's (2012) five-step systematic review protocol: formulating research questions, locating studies, selecting and evaluating them, analyzing findings, and reporting. Database searches (Scopus, Google Scholar, and other engines) ran monthly from July to September 2023 using combinations of "Facebook" and "Reaction" with Boolean operators. Inclusion was limited to peer-reviewed journal articles and conference proceedings from 2016–2023, yielding 64 articles. Each article was coded via a comparative naturalistic method (Armstrong, 2010) by research question, results, and conclusions, then grouped into three major categories with finer sub-topics (political news, populism, healthcare, business, emotion detection).

## Findings

- Of 64 articles, 6 addressed the introduction of Reactions, 19 politics and far-right groups, and 39 other social issues.
- In political campaigns (Mexico, Brexit, EU Parliament elections), negative emotional appeals — especially anger — generated substantially more engagement and sharing than positive content.
- Populist and far-right leaders disproportionately elicit 'Angry' and 'Haha' reactions relative to mainstream politicians.
- Hyperpartisan and junk news display a bivalent emotional logic: 'Love' and 'Angry' rarely co-occur, while 'Angry' co-occurs with 'Sad' and 'Wow'.
- 'Love' and 'Haha' generally index positive response; 'Sad' and 'Angry' index negative, though 'Haha' and 'Wow' can carry ironic or disbelieving valence in political contexts.
- Computational studies use Reactions to predict emotion classes, measure controversy via the entropy of reaction distributions, and label sentiment in low-resource languages (Sinhala, Bangla).
- Lifestyle and entertainment posts skew positive, sociopolitical posts skew broader and more negative.
- Cultural context modulates reaction patterns, as shown in Hong Kong studies comparing China-critical, China-supporting, and neutral media audiences.

## Connections

This review synthesizes an empirical tradition that intersects strongly with work using Facebook engagement signals to study political communication and problematic information, notably [[Giglietto2019-882f1900]], [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], and [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]] on coordinated link-sharing and junk news, as well as [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] on Facebook as a political arena. Its emphasis on anger as an engagement driver connects to broader debates on populist and hyperpartisan communication represented in this register by [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]] and [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], while its methodological reflections on Reactions as affective proxies speak to platform-data availability concerns addressed in [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]].
