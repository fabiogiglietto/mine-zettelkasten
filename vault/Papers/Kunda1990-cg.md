# The case for motivated reasoning

> Kunda, Z. (1990). The case for motivated reasoning. *Psychological Bulletin*, *108*, 480–498. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.108.3.480)

## Summary

Kunda's landmark theoretical paper argues that motivation shapes reasoning not by magically overriding the facts, but by biasing the ordinary cognitive processes people use to access, construct, and evaluate their beliefs. She distinguishes two kinds of motivational goals. **Accuracy goals** push people toward the strategies they deem most appropriate, generally yielding deeper and more careful processing. **Directional goals** steer people toward preferred conclusions—but crucially, people do not simply believe whatever they wish. Directional reasoning is bounded by the need to construct a justification that would persuade a dispassionate observer, allowing people to maintain an "illusion of objectivity." Kunda's central claim is that a single mechanism—biased memory search and belief construction—can account for the whole diverse landscape of motivated reasoning.

## Key Contributions

- An integrative mechanistic account (biased accessing and construction of beliefs and rules) that unifies previously scattered motivated-reasoning findings.
- Rehabilitates the motivational view against 1970s cognitive critiques by specifying processes that make "hot" and "cold" cognition distinguishable yet jointly operative.
- Sharpens the theoretical distinction between accuracy goals and directional goals and their different mechanisms.
- Introduces the emphasis on **justification construction** and the **illusion of objectivity**, explaining why motivated bias is bounded by available evidence.
- Extends biased-memory-search ideas beyond self-serving attribution to a wide range of goals and reasoning tasks.

## Methods

Conceptual synthesis and integrative literature review rather than new empirical work. Kunda organizes prior research into accuracy-driven and directional reasoning, drawing across dissonance research, self-perception, attribution, outcome dependency, likelihood estimation, statistical heuristic use, evaluation of scientific evidence, and persuasion/involvement research. Throughout she asks whether findings can be reinterpreted in purely cognitive (nonmotivational) terms, giving special weight to studies with random assignment that rule out prior-belief explanations, and distinguishing indirect evidence (inferred from judgment outcomes) from more direct evidence such as response latencies, recalled memory content, and the cognitive complexity of written descriptions.

## Findings

- Accuracy motivation reduces biases such as primacy effects, stereotyping, anchoring, and the fundamental attribution error, yielding more complex and accurate judgments.
- Accuracy manipulations reduce bias only when delivered *before* encoding, indicating effects on initial processing rather than self-presentation.
- Accuracy goals do not always help: some biases resist them, and greater complexity can sometimes worsen judgment (e.g., the dilution effect).
- Directional goals bias self-characterizations of traits, attitudes, and past behavior—but changes are constrained by prior self-knowledge.
- Direct evidence of biased memory search: people are faster to generate and endorse desired self-relevant memories and traits.
- Outcome dependency biases liking and evaluations of others, but the bias fails when information is too impoverished to justify the desired impression.
- Goals can bias the selection of statistical heuristics (base rates, the law of large numbers).
- People judge threatening scientific evidence as more methodologically flawed, yet remain responsive to genuine strengths—showing that bias is constrained by plausibility.

## Connections

As a foundational treatment of how goals bias cognition, this paper underpins much subsequent work on politically and identity-driven belief formation in the misinformation literature. It provides the psychological grounding for research on partisan resistance to correction and inoculation against manipulation, such as [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Pennycook2021-jq]], and speaks to accounts of why exposure to misinformation is often partisan and motivated rather than merely accidental, as in [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Grinberg2019-ua]].
