# The Privatization of Public Discourse

> Recuero, R., & Tavares, C. Q. (2025). The Privatization of Public Discourse. *The Routledge Companion to Digital Media and Democracy*. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003477570-5
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003477570-5)

## Summary

This book chapter argues that a handful of transnational social media platforms have effectively **privatized the public sphere**, centralizing formerly decentralized online communication under private ownership and subjecting national public debate to supranational (largely US-based) private rules and algorithmic governance. Recuero and Tavares contend that this arrangement generates systemic risks for democracy, and that these harms land disproportionately on the **Global South**, where weak regulatory infrastructure amplifies asymmetries of power between platforms and nation-states. Anchored in the Brazilian case—especially the failed "Fake News Bill" (PL 2630)—and comparative Latin American examples, the chapter links platformization, datafication, and data colonialism to threats against digital sovereignty and democratic participation.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and develops the concept of the **"privatization of public discourse"** to describe platforms' control over what discourse is made visible or hidden.
- Extends systemic-risk and public-sphere debates to a **Global South perspective**, centering Brazil and Latin America.
- Documents concrete instances of **platform lobbying against national regulation** as evidence of platforms' structural and discursive power.
- Connects **digital sovereignty**, platform neutrality, and **data colonialism** into a framework for assessing platform threats to democratic governance.

## Methods

The chapter combines conceptual and theoretical synthesis (public sphere, political participation, platformization, digital sovereignty) with case study analysis grounded in the Brazilian context, including 2022 election disinformation and the attempted coup. The authors also perform documentary analysis of platform lobbying—including author-captured screenshots of Google and Meta ads opposing PL 2630—and draw comparative references to Latin American regulatory cases, notably Argentina's data protection law debates and Meta's involvement.

## Findings

- The shift from decentralized internet protocols to centralized platforms moved mediation power to a few private companies whose algorithms determine content visibility.
- Algorithmic governance fosters echo chambers, polarization, radicalization, and the circulation of toxicity and disinformation, fragmenting the collective public sphere.
- In Brazil, Google (via its search homepage) and Meta (via Facebook/Instagram ads) ran campaigns framing PL 2630 as censorship, dividing public opinion and stalling the bill; courts ordered the messages removed.
- In Argentina, Meta engaged more subtly through policy papers and stakeholder influence to oppose stricter data protection amendments.
- Data operates as a form of capital; cross-border storage and third-party sharing produce data sovereignty problems and **data colonialism** that disadvantage Global South nations positioned as data "colonies."
- Global North jurisdictions (US, EU, China) have gained regulatory leverage through instruments like the GDPR and proposed ADPPA, while Global South states remain largely unable to enforce their laws on platforms.
