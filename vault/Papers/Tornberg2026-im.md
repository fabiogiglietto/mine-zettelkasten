# From the platform society to the AI society

> Törnberg, P., & Uitermark, J. (2026). From the platform society to the AI society. *Dialogues on Digital Society*. https://doi.org/10.1177/29768640261487931
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/29768640261487931)

## Summary

Törnberg and Uitermark argue that generative AI—and large language models especially—is driving a tendential shift from the "platform society" to an emerging "AI society." Rather than a clean break, they theorize this as a transformation that grows out of platform-era infrastructures, data, and political economies while partially rupturing them. Tracing the transition across three entangled domains—economic, epistemic, and political—they reconsider which concepts from critical platform studies should be carried forward or revised for a dedicated critical AI studies. The overarching thesis is that where platform capitalism enclosed social interaction, AI capitalism encloses the infrastructures of thought—an enclosure of cognition itself.

## Key Contributions

- Theorizes generative AI as emerging from, yet partially rupturing with, the platform society, via three linked transformations: network-based to relational monopoly, predictive to generative knowing, and data politics to alignment politics.
- Introduces and systematizes a critical vocabulary: **cognitive lock-in**, **research-as-a-service**, **synthetic social science**, **infrastructuralization of theory**, **alignment politics**, AI as media infrastructure, and **epistemic empire**.
- Proposes a methodological program for critical AI studies, including **alignment audits**, the **talkthrough** method (adapting the platform-studies "walkthrough"), and infrastructural/organizational research.
- Outlines public-interest governance principles (transparency, pluralism, redress) reimagined for generative media infrastructures, including calls for researcher-access architectures for LLMs.

## Methods

Theoretical and conceptual synthesis drawing on critical platform studies, critical data studies, STS, media studies, and emerging critical AI scholarship, framed through Foucauldian notions of epistemes and governmentality. The core analytic move is a comparative framing (summarized in a stylized table) contrasting the platform society and the AI society across organizational form, monopoly mechanism, mode of extraction, epistemic mode, representation, and political mode. The argument proceeds through illustrative examples rather than new empirical data (e.g., the GPT-4o deprecation backlash, ChatGPT shopping prompts, language-switching bias, and public interrogations of Grok).

## Findings

- Network effects no longer structure digital monopoly; value increasingly flows from a dyadic user–system relationship, enabling **cognitive lock-in** where rent is extracted from the accumulated history of user–system collaboration.
- Research conditions are shifting from "data-as-a-service" to "research-as-a-service," deepening scholars' dependence on opaque, proprietary, nondeterministic commercial systems.
- The Big Data era's "end of theory" reverses into an "end of the end of theory": conceptual labor returns but entangled with models trained on the scholarly canon.
- Data doubles are being supplanted by synthetic agents and "AI twins" that manufacture rather than sample publics, raising validation, representation, and democratic-legitimacy concerns.
- Alignment operates through at least four mechanisms—training-data composition, reinforcement learning/fine-tuning, system prompts/orchestration, and content policies/guardrails—each with distinct actors and governance implications.
- Commercial incentives toward lock-in encourage "dark patterns" such as sycophancy and affective dependence as structural pathologies.
- Regulation is uneven: the EU DSA provides researcher data access for platforms, but the AI Act offers no equivalent framework for independent scrutiny of LLMs.

## Connections

This is a foundational theory piece for a critical AI studies, extending platform-governance and data-access scholarship—its concern with researcher access to proprietary models directly complements empirical and policy work on platform data access and governance such as [[Rieder2025-ju]] and [[Gillespie2022-jx]]. Its genealogy of platform capitalism and the political economy of digital infrastructures builds on the "platform society" framework associated with [[van-Dijck2018-up]]. The paper's attention to AI discourse, imaginaries, and the enclosure of cognition resonates with work on AI industry imaginaries like [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]].
