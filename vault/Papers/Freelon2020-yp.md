# Disinformation as Political Communication

> Freelon, D., & Wells, C. (2020). Disinformation as Political Communication. *Political Communication*, *37*, 145–156. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2020.1723755
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2020.1723755)

## Summary

This introductory essay to the *Political Communication* special issue "Beyond Fake News: The Politics of Disinformation" situates disinformation within a broader "crisis of public communication" and a longer-developing "disinformation order." Freelon and Wells define disinformation by three criteria—deception, potential for harm, and intent to harm—following the European Commission's 2018 High Level Expert Group definition, distinguishing it from misinformation (which lacks malicious intent), propaganda, and other "problematic information." They argue that "fake news" should be retired as an analytical concept, trace the very short scholarly history of disinformation research against the older traditions of propaganda and misinformation study, and set out a forward-looking research agenda. The essay frames disinformation as a normative threat to democratic practice requiring precise conceptual distinctions.

## Key Contributions

- A working, criteria-based definition of disinformation (deception + harm + intent) clarifying its boundaries relative to misinformation, propaganda, and other problematic information.
- An argument for abandoning "fake news" as an analytical term because it has been weaponized (notably by Trump) and refers ambiguously to too many phenomena, including satire.
- A concise intellectual history of disinformation, propaganda, and misinformation research across communication and adjacent fields.
- A content-versus-reception typology for organizing disinformation scholarship.
- A research agenda emphasizing effects, subpopulation and partisan asymmetries, cross-national comparison, and empirical testing of whether the mis/disinformation distinction matters.

## Methods

The essay is a conceptual and definitional analysis paired with a historical literature review, tracing propaganda research (from the Institute for Propaganda Analysis, 1937–1942, through Cold War and Herman-Chomsky traditions) and misinformation research (originating in cognitive psychology). The authors use a simple bibliometric indicator—Google Scholar title counts—to demonstrate the post-2016 surge in scholarship, and they synthesize and classify the seven special-issue articles into content studies and reception studies.

## Findings

- US public confidence that news media report fully, accurately, and fairly has declined steadily since the 1970s, bottoming in 2016 and then sharply polarizing.
- Over 70% of Google Scholar titles containing "disinformation" from 2010–2019 appeared after 2016, evidencing a rapid surge in interest.
- Fewer than 10% of consumers recognize native advertising as paid content, and learning its nature reduces perceived credibility of the producer.
- Junk news reached parity with professional sources in Twitter shares during the 2016 election, while professional news gained ground during the 2018 State of the Union.
- Reception studies show fact-checking can reduce disinformation credibility, ostracized users persist in endorsing attitude-congruent falsehoods, and low trust in political/media systems increases disinformation belief and can shift voters toward the far-right AfD.
- US partisan asymmetry exists, with conservatives disproportionately targeted and susceptible, and Black Americans especially targeted by the Russian Internet Research Agency.

## Connections

This essay's definitional and conceptual work anchors much of the surrounding literature-disorder literature; its call to abandon "fake news" and specify problematic-information categories connects directly to work refining these definitions and effects, including [[Marwick2025-vx]], [[Hameleers2026-mc]], and [[Tsfati2020-uo]]. Its emphasis on the "disinformation order," platform political economy, and network asymmetry resonates with [[Bennett2018-ys]], [[Benkler2018-lw]], and [[Starbird2019-qv]], while its treatment of belief, motivated reasoning, and susceptibility links to [[Kunda1990-cg]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]], and [[Osmundsen2021-et]]. The empirical claims about spread and prevalence bridge to foundational studies such as [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Guess2019-ym]].
