## Summary

This is the second edition (2010) of the first volume of Manuel Castells' trilogy *The Information Age: Economy, Society, and Culture*, augmented with a substantial new preface. Castells argues that a new social structure — the **network society** — has emerged from the convergence of the information technology revolution, the restructuring of capitalism, and the cultural movements of the late twentieth century. Drawing on comparative research across the Americas, Asia, and Europe, he develops an integrated account of how digital networking has transformed the economy, work, communication, space, and time, producing new forms of globalization, inequality, and cultural experience. The 2010 preface revisits these claims in light of the 2008 financial crisis, the mobile Internet, Web 2.0, and accelerating global urbanization.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces a durable theoretical vocabulary — *network society*, *space of flows*, *timeless time*, *informationalism*, *network enterprise*, *real virtuality*, *mass self-communication* — that has diffused widely across sociology, geography, and communication studies.
- Refines classical macro-sociology by distinguishing **modes of production** (capitalism/statism) from **modes of development** (industrialism/informationalism).
- Reframes globalization as a *selective networked process* that includes and excludes particular territories and populations rather than a uniform flattening.
- Advances a network-based theory of urbanism centered on polycentric metropolitan regions.
- The 2010 preface offers a retrospective assessment tying the original theses to the 2008 financial crisis, mobile Internet diffusion, and contemporary urbanization.

## Methods

Castells synthesizes comparative empirical research across the USA, Asia, Latin America, and Europe with secondary statistical data on productivity, trade, FDI, employment, occupational change, and demographics for the G-7 (1920–2005). He combines case studies of technological milieus (Silicon Valley), East Asian business networks, and firms like Cisco with statistical indicators of Internet and mobile diffusion, migration, and urbanization. On this evidentiary base he constructs new conceptual categories to interpret the transformation, explicitly rejecting futurology in favor of grounded, non-predictive social-scientific interpretation.

## Findings

- Networked organizational forms enabled by digital technologies broke through traditional constraints of size and complexity, allowing globalization on a qualitatively new scale.
- The global economy operates as a **"global automaton"** of interdependent financial markets that has largely escaped nation-state regulation — as the 2008 crisis exposed.
- In the US (1998–2008), productivity grew ~30% while real wages stagnated; financial-sector profits rose from 10% to 40% of all profits; household debt/disposable income climbed from 3% to 130%.
- Internet users grew from <40 million (1995) to ~1.5 billion (2009); wireless subscriptions from ~16 million (1991) to >3.4 billion (mid-2008); the OECD/developing digital divide narrowed from 80.6:1 (1997) to 5.8:1 (2007), with China surpassing the US in absolute users by 2008.
- Labor markets polarized between **self-programmable** and **generic** workers, with growing part-time, temporary, and self-employed arrangements.
- Over half the world's population is now urban, with polycentric metropolitan regions (e.g., Pearl River Delta ~60 million; Southern California) emerging as the dominant spatial form.
- Mass media converge with horizontal, user-generated Internet platforms, producing **mass self-communication** and a culture of *real virtuality*.

## Connections

Castells provides much of the macro-theoretical scaffolding — network logic, mass self-communication, space of flows — that later empirical studies of platform sociability build on and challenge. It connects to work on network-mediated coordination and information diffusion such as [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]] on cross-platform link sharing, and to studies of platform-based sociality and identity like [[Boyd2026-op]] and [[Sofia2012-802a21dc]]; [[Ptz2024-sl20]] extends the network-society lens into questions of algorithmic governance and platform power. Together these papers can be read as testing and refining Castells' foundational claims against the concrete dynamics of contemporary digital platforms.
