The summarized work is a major sociological synthesis theorizing the emergence of a global "network society" arising from the convergence of the information technology revolution, restructured capitalism, and social movements from the late twentieth century onward. It argues that digital networks have reorganized economy, work, communication, space, and time, producing a new mode of development — informationalism — that older industrial-era frameworks cannot adequately grasp.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces a foundational conceptual vocabulary — *network society*, *space of flows*, *timeless time*, *informationalism*, *network enterprise*, *real virtuality*, and *mass self-communication*.
- Offers an integrated, empirically grounded account of the co-transformation of technology, economy, culture, and space.
- Distinguishes *modes of production* (capitalism/statism) from *modes of development* (industrialism/informationalism), refining classical macro-sociology.
- Reframes globalization as a selective, networked process that includes some territories and populations while excluding others.
- Advances a network-based theory of urbanism grounded in comparative research on polycentric metropolitan regions.
- Adds a 2010 preface reassessing the trilogy's claims in light of the 2008 financial crisis and the mobile Internet era.

## Methods

The work synthesizes comparative, multi-regional empirical research (USA, Asia, Latin America, Europe) with secondary statistical data on productivity, trade, employment, occupational structure, and demographics for the G-7 (1920–2005). It draws on case studies of technological milieus (e.g., Silicon Valley), East Asian business networks, and firms such as Cisco, alongside indicators on Internet and mobile diffusion, migration, and urbanization. Rather than prediction, it pursues grounded social-scientific interpretation, constructing new conceptual categories to interpret assembled evidence.

## Findings

- Digital networking enabled organizations to overcome traditional size and complexity limits, allowing globalization at new scale.
- US cumulative productivity grew nearly 30% between 1998 and 2008 while real wages stagnated; financial-sector profits rose from 10% to 40% of all profits.
- US household debt rose from 3% (1998) to 130% (2008) of disposable income, feeding the credit and housing bubble.
- Global Internet users grew from under 40 million (1995) to ~1.5 billion (2009); wireless subscriptions surpassed 3.4 billion by mid-2008.
- The OECD/developing-country digital divide narrowed from 80.6:1 (1997) to 5.8:1 (2007); China surpassed the US in absolute Internet users by 2008.
- Over half the world's population is now urban, with polycentric metropolitan regions emerging as the dominant spatial form.
- Labor markets polarized between "self-programmable" and "generic" workers, with growing precarious arrangements.
- Traditional mass media converged with horizontal Internet networks, giving rise to user-generated platforms.
