# Feed them slop: Alignment, attunement, and the affective lure of slopaganda

> Topinka, R. (2026). Feed them slop: Alignment, attunement, and the affective lure of slopaganda. *European Journal of Cultural Studies*. https://doi.org/10.1177/13675494261468632
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/13675494261468632)

## Summary

Topinka theorises "slopaganda" (or "agitslop") — AI-generated political content that is patently fake yet affectively resonant, exemplified by the "Trump Gaza" video and the "Kirkinator" meme videos. The central argument is that slopaganda should not be understood through the lens of belief-manipulation (as deepfakes are), but as an affective *lure* that hooks fickle attention — including negative attention — by attuning to the ambient moods and affordances of platform feeds. Drawing on Rickert's ambient rhetoric and Massumi's affect theory, Topinka contends that AI models, through training, labelling, and alignment via RLHF, are optimised for rhetorical attunement: they function as "ideological attunement machines" that fold into and intensify existing ideological and technical formations rather than disrupting them. Slopaganda remains political even when incoherent, because to circulate it must attune to digital culture's regimes of legibility and plausibility.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes AI slop from an "error" or "excess" into an ambient rhetoric highly attuned to dominant norms and technical incentives.
- Introduces the concept of AI models as "ideological attunement machines," pushing critique beyond the standard "bias in training data" argument.
- Distinguishes deepfakes (which trade on mistaken reality) from slopaganda (obviously fake but affectively real).
- Bridges rhetorical/affect theory with AI political economy (fine-tuning, alignment, RLHF, prompting) to explain slop's resonance.
- Offers an analytical method attentive to how affective lures coordinate ideology even when political destinations are unpredictable.

## Methods

A conceptual and theoretical essay in cultural studies, not an empirical study. Topinka performs close rhetorical and cultural readings of specific AI slop artefacts — the "Trump Gaza" video, Trump/DHS/White House posts, the "Kirkinator" Agartha videos, AI Blackface content, and an Iran-produced "Lego Trump" video. The analysis synthesises Rickert's ambient rhetoric, Sampson's affective lure, Massumi's affect/emotion distinction, Noordenbos and Tuters' ambient propaganda, and Meyer's platform realism, alongside critical accounts of AI industry alignment practices.

## Findings

- The "Trump Gaza" video began as a joke by an LA studio testing AI speed and reached Trump via Mel Gibson — spreading through shitpost/engagement logics rather than deliberate propaganda design.
- Slop targets are disproportionately women and racial others (immigrants, Black protestors, Black lawmakers), giving form to racist and colonial fantasies.
- Training and labelling practices encode rhetorical attunements: image datasets were labelled overwhelmingly by workers from "WEIRD" countries, and structured commercial data skews outputs toward consumer expectations.
- Alignment relies on hyperexploited RLHF workers, mostly in the Global South, who attune outputs to human expectations.
- AI Blackface slop revives minstrelsy-style tropes, synthesising pre-existing racist fantasies into novel but nostalgic outputs.
- The "Kirkinator" videos coordinate affect and orient attitudes (via esoteric Nazi "Agartha" imagery) while refusing coherent claims — explaining their appeal even to left-wing meme accounts.
- Ambient attunements to slop archives have framed real-world violence, e.g. "for Agartha" inscribed at a Jakarta mosque attack.

## Connections
