# Selective Exposure in the Age of Social Media: Endorsements Trump Partisan Source Affiliation When Selecting News Online

> Messing, S., & Westwood, S. J. (2014). Selective Exposure in the Age of Social Media: Endorsements Trump Partisan Source Affiliation When Selecting News Online. *Communic. Res.*, *41*, 1042–1063.
>
> [View paper](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Selective%20Exposure%20in%20the%20Age%20of%20Social%20Media%3A%20Endorsements%20Trump%20Partisan%20Source%20Affiliation%20When%20Selecting%20News%20Online)

## Summary

This paper challenges the pessimistic narrative that the internet inevitably deepens media fragmentation and partisan selective exposure. Messing and Westwood argue that social media's defining feature — aggregated social endorsements such as "likes," recommendation counts, and "most emailed" rankings — fundamentally alters news consumption by shifting the selection decision from choosing a habitual partisan *source* to choosing individual *stories* recommended by a politically heterogeneous social network. Drawing on bounded-rationality and heuristic-processing theory, they conceptualize endorsements as utility-signaling cues that trigger a bandwagon heuristic. Across two experiments, they show that stronger endorsements increase the probability of selecting content and that the mere presence of endorsements reduces partisan selective exposure to levels indistinguishable from chance.

## Key Contributions

- Recasts the internet's role in polarization, offering experimental evidence that social media can *reduce* rather than amplify partisan selective exposure.
- Provides what the authors describe as the first direct social-science comparison of social cues versus source cues on news selection.
- Extends information-utility models of news selectivity by treating social endorsements as heuristic cues that signal a story's social value.
- Draws out implications for agenda-setting theory, suggesting a diffusion of agenda-setting power from newsrooms to social networks (a modern two-step flow).
- Flags a policy concern: filtering algorithms and filter bubbles could offset the diversifying potential of endorsements.

## Methods

Two randomized experiments used custom web applications mimicking social media / news aggregator interfaces, replicating and extending Iyengar and Hahn (2009). **Study 1** (N = 739, Amazon Mechanical Turk) used a three-condition between-subjects design (partisan-label-only, social-endorsement-only, combined), with articles pulled live from RSS feeds across four categories; source labels included partisan brands (Fox, MSNBC) and non-partisan sources (USA Today, Reuters), and endorsements were manipulated as low (0–1,000) versus high (>10,000 recommends). **Study 2** (N = 141 undergraduates) used a within-subjects design in a richer interactive interface with 80 daily-harvested stories, a countdown timer, and real Facebook "like" counts and "most emailed" rankings. Analysis relied on mixed multinomial (conditional) logit models (Study 1) and mixed logistic regression (Study 2), plus comparisons against chance; a separate manipulation check confirmed perceived source ideology.

## Findings

- In the partisan-label-only condition, selectivity emerged: Republicans chose Fox more than Democrats (0.38 vs 0.21), and Democrats chose MSNBC more than Republicans (0.30 vs 0.20).
- When endorsements were present, selection rates for the two parties became nearly identical — partisan selectivity dropped to levels indistinguishable from chance.
- Partisans were more than twice as likely as chance to select strongly endorsed articles from ideologically dissonant sources.
- The endorsement effect was robust and not diluted by partisan cues; effects were, if anything, slightly stronger when both cue types were present.
- Republicans used the high social cue more than Democrats, hinting at partisan differences in selection processes (possibly conformity-linked).
- Study 2 replicated the endorsement effect in a realistic browsing environment while showing scant partisan source selectivity.

## Connections

This paper is an early empirical counterpoint to the echo-chamber and filter-bubble thesis, and it pairs directly with [[Bakshy2015-rn]]'s large-scale evidence on exposure to cross-cutting content via social networks. Its optimistic conclusions can be read against later, more mixed assessments of algorithmic curation and cross-cutting exposure in [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], and [[Guess2021-ym]], and against the affective-polarization framing of [[Iyengar2019-jj]]; its heuristic account of endorsement cues also resonates with work on social sharing and misinformation such as [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]].
