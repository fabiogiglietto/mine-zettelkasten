# Retheorizing Context Collapse for Social Media

> Chancellor, S., Kaur, H., Terveen, L., & Milton, A. (2026). Retheorizing Context Collapse for Social Media. *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3816991
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3816991)

## Summary

This conceptual paper argues that *context collapse*—the flattening of multiple audiences into one on social media—has drifted into conceptual ambiguity over its roughly fifteen-year life, oscillating between an overly broad theory of all social-media sociality and a narrow account of audience disclosure. The authors contend that the concept's original audience-oriented framing no longer fits platforms dominated by algorithmic recommendation, multimodal content, and consumption rather than social posting. They retheorize context collapse by proposing a structural "grammar" of context built on three dimensions—the user, the platform, and the mediating data/interaction—and redefine a "collapse" as the flattening of contextual information between *any* relevant dimensions, not just between a user's identity and their audience. Modeling their intervention on how affordance theory was disciplined and renewed, they aim to restore analytical precision while preserving the generative flexibility that made the concept durable.

## Key Contributions

- A reusable definition of *context* for social computing, adapted from ubiquitous-computing accounts (Dey et al., Dourish, Suchman) to social media.
- A structured "context grammar" of dimensions and sub-dimensions for analyzing collapse.
- A broadened redefinition of "collapse" as flattening between any relevant context dimensions.
- Reframing of three canonical collapses (audience, time, interaction) and three novel ones (identity, algorithmic, situational).
- An agenda for operationalization, measurement, cross-platform comparison, design directions, and emerging actors such as AI agents and AI-generated content.

## Methods

This is a conceptual/theoretical retheorization rather than an empirical study. The authors perform a bottom-up review of prior context-collapse research to derive their dimensions and sub-dimensions, adapt Dey et al.'s context definition to social computing, and draw on Sartori's notion of conceptual stretching to justify disciplined reconceptualization. They validate the grammar through two sets of case studies: translating well-known collapses into the new vocabulary, and proposing novel collapses grounded in existing empirical literature from CSCW, CMC, and HCI (including work on affordances, folk theories, and algorithmic experience).

## Findings

- **Three context dimensions** are proposed: the *user* (state—situational identity, location, time, activity; and self-concept—role identities, historical narrative, beliefs/values, traits); the *platform* or its "DNA" (design, network/content, algorithm); and *data/interaction* (profiles, personal data/settings, first-order content, active interactions, passive/consumption interaction, metadata).
- Two cross-cutting influences—business-model ecosystems and governance ecosystems—shape all dimensions.
- Three known collapses are reframed: **audience collapse** (self-concept vs. network/content), **time collapse** (self-concept across time), and **interaction collapse** (user state vs. platform design).
- Three novel collapses are theorized: **identity collapse** (algorithms compressing a multifaceted self), **algorithmic collapse** (interfaces flattening algorithmic complexity), and **situational collapse** (algorithms overriding in-the-moment needs with aggregated behavior models).
- Collapses can co-occur and compound—e.g., "stakeholder intention collapse" and "cross-platform data collapse" span multiple dimensions driven by business models and corporate data aggregation.

## Connections

This paper directly extends the foundational context-collapse and self-presentation work it cites, including [[Marwick2011-sl02]] and boyd's related writing in [[Boyd2026-op]], updating that audience-oriented lens for algorithmic platforms. Its emphasis on how algorithmic systems and their opaque interfaces reshape user self-understanding connects it to folk-theory and algorithmic-experience research such as [[Bucher2017-qh]], [[Cotter2019-js]], and [[Petre2019-zh]], while its attention to platform "DNaA" and governance ecosystems resonates with [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]].
