# Causes and consequences of mainstream media dissemination of fake news: literature review and synthesis

> Tsfati, Y., Boomgaarden, H. G., Strömbäck, J., Vliegenthart, R., Damstra, A., & Lindgren, E. (2020). Causes and consequences of mainstream media dissemination of fake news: literature review and synthesis. *Annals of the International Communication Association*, *44*, 157–173. https://doi.org/10.1080/23808985.2020.1759443
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/23808985.2020.1759443)

## Summary

This article synthesizes research across political communication, journalism studies, and cognitive/social psychology to advance a counterintuitive thesis: mainstream news media are a major, if unintended, vector for the spread of fake news. The authors observe an apparent puzzle — direct exposure to fake news websites and social-media sharing is confined to a small slice of the population (roughly 1–10%), yet large segments of the public know about and even believe prominent falsehoods like Pizzagate. The most plausible bridge is mainstream coverage: when journalists report on and attempt to debunk fake news, they inadvertently amplify it to audiences far larger than the original falsehoods ever reached. The paper structures its argument around three questions — what role mainstream media play in disseminating fake news, why they cover it, and how audiences process that coverage.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes the disinformation debate to spotlight the underexamined, paradoxical role of mainstream media in *spreading* (not merely correcting) fake news.
- Offers an integrated framework for why mainstream outlets cover fake news, combining journalistic role perceptions, news values, the social psychology of news decisions, and existing content-monitoring infrastructure.
- Bridges disinformation research with the cognitive/social psychology of misinformation correction (mere-exposure, truth effects, continued influence) to theorize the audience consequences of coverage.
- Sets a research agenda: content analyses of fake news coverage, newsroom ethnographies, and experimental/linkage studies on audience processing, media trust, and attitude certainty.

## Methods

Narrative literature review and synthesis rather than original empirical work. The authors integrate findings across fields and draw on prior survey data (e.g., Pizzagate polling, BuzzFeed studies), traffic and network analyses, and content analyses. They use illustrative LexisNexis searches of 12 U.S. mainstream outlets for terms like "fake news" and "Pizzagate," apply Allcott and Gentzkow's dataset of 156 election-related fake news articles to show how such stories satisfy news values, and lean on meta-analytic findings (e.g., a 32-study meta-analysis on correction) to theorize audience effects.

## Findings

- Public belief in fake news is substantial: over a third of Americans thought Pizzagate probably/definitely true; 75% of recalled fake headlines were rated somewhat/very accurate, with similar patterns in Brazil and Israel.
- Exposure is highly concentrated: 80% of fake news on Twitter was consumed by 1% of users; ~60% of fake news site visits came from the 10% most conservative online diets; real news sites averaged 40x more monthly visitors than fake news sites.
- Sharing is rare — only 8.5% of matched Facebook users shared fake news, and 0.1% of Twitter users produced 80% of sharing — and fact-checks rarely reach fake news consumers due to selective diffusion.
- Fake news stories are highly newsworthy (negativity, elite actors, surprise, entertainment, celebrity) and appear engineered to maximize spread and align with national agendas.
- Partisan media absorb and amplify fake news more heavily than mainstream media, partly because it fits their ideological tendencies.
- Misinformation exhibits "continued influence" that resists correction; the modal audience response was *uncertainty*, with correction undermined by repetition, lexical complexity, rich false mental models, prior-attitude congruence, and low media trust.

## Connections

This synthesis sits within information-disorder theory and the meta-science of misinformation research, and directly builds on empirical traffic and exposure work such as [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym], and [[Allcott2017-yz]], as well as the foundational framing of [[Lazer2018-mm]] and the diffusion dynamics in [[Vosoughi2018-at]]. Its argument about the limits and backfire risks of correction connects to work on continued influence and motivated reasoning in [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]], and [[Kunda1990-cg]], while its concern with mainstream amplification and the "disinformation order" resonates with [[Chadwick2013-ns]], [[Benkler2018-lw]], and [[Bennett2018-ys]]. The concentration-of-exposure findings echo scale-and-prevalence arguments in [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Allen2020-nj]].
