# Politics in the age of hybrid media

> Chadwick, A., Dennis, J., & Smith, A. P. (2015). Politics in the age of hybrid media. *The Routledge Companion to Social Media and Politics*, 7–22. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315716299-2
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781315716299-2)

## Summary

This chapter argues that political communication in Western democracies is best understood as a **hybrid media system** — an environment defined by the interactions, adaptations, and coevolution of older and newer media logics rather than the wholesale displacement of one by the other. Drawing on the cross-disciplinary ontology of hybridity, the authors build a framework organized around **power, systems, and media logics**, and apply it across three domains: news and journalism, election campaigning, and engagement/mobilization. Reviewing a growing body of empirical work, they show how older media are "renewed" as they absorb newer logics, and conclude that the resulting polycentric environment is, on balance, more inclusive than the twentieth-century broadcast/newspaper duopoly — while stressing that participation does not equal power.

## Key Contributions

- Consolidates and extends the **hybrid media system** framework as an analytic lens spanning journalism, campaigning, and mobilization.
- Pluralizes the singular concept of "media logic" into **hybrid media logics** suited to a polycentric, post-broadcast environment.
- Introduces **political information cycles** (versus traditional news cycles) and foregrounds **temporal power** — the mastery of timing and real-time intervention — as an under-researched object of study.
- Surveys the empirical uptake of the approach across many national contexts (Germany, Norway, US, UK, Spain, Portugal, Italy, Sweden).
- Moves the field beyond utopian/dystopian and displacement/absorption dichotomies toward a "not only, but also" analysis of coevolution.

## Methods

The chapter is a **conceptual and theoretical synthesis** that treats hybridity as a cross-disciplinary organizing principle (hybrid regimes, cultures/genres, organizations, actor-network theory). It reworks concepts of power (Castells, Dahl), systems (Easton, McNair), and media logic (Altheide and Snow, Dahlgren), and draws on assemblage theory. The authors advocate a **mixed-methods repertoire** — conceptual work, historical and documentary analysis, real-time "live" online research, and insider ethnography — and ground the argument in a literature review plus illustrative case analyses (Obama 2008, the Jeremiah Wright/Sarah Palin coverage, the Snowden/*Guardian* webchat, Kony2012).

## Findings

- Obama's 2008 campaign succeeded by integrating online, broadcast, and physical activism — raising $500m of $750m online yet spending only 4% of the ad budget online — demonstrating the continuing power of older media.
- Studies of Twitter around televised debates show a hybrid logic that both meshes with and deviates from broadcast events, with celebrities acting as "bridging elites."
- Norwegian journalists rarely use candidates' social media as sources, and larger parties still generate news via traditional media events.
- News production is reshaped by blurring roles, post-digital norms of transparency and risk-taking, and new organizational forms — producing "political information cycles" rather than fixed news cycles.
- Mass media logic still prevails: television remains the dominant news source even among heavy social media users.
- Hybrid mobilization movements (38 Degrees, Avaaz, MoveOn, GetUp!) strategically switch between logics and time campaigns to mainstream media prominence.
- **Dual screening** peaks around media events, involves "lay tutelage" and real-time frame contestation, and predicts online political participation.
- Hybridity can amplify messages but also cause loss of control over meaning, as when Kony2012 spread far beyond its intended audience.

## Connections

This chapter is the theoretical anchor for the hybrid media system concept elaborated in [[Chadwick2013-ns]], and its emphasis on the interplay of platform and broadcast logics connects to work on platform power and governance such as [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and [[Gillespie2022-jx]]. Its treatment of connective action and networked mobilization aligns directly with [[Bennett2018-ys]], while its account of how attention and misinformation move through hybrid channels resonates with the propaganda-ecosystem argument in [[Benkler2018-lw]] and the diffusion dynamics documented in [[Vosoughi2018-at]].
