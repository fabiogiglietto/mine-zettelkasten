# After the ‘APIcalypse’: social media platforms and their fight against critical scholarly research

> Bruns, A. (2021). After the ‘APIcalypse’: social media platforms and their fight against critical scholarly research. *Disinformation and Data Lockdown on Social Platforms*, *22*, 1544–1566. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003206972-2
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003206972-2)

## Summary

Bruns analyses the aftermath of the 2018 Cambridge Analytica scandal, when Facebook and Twitter drastically curtailed researcher access to their APIs — an event he names the "APIcalypse." His central argument is that these restrictions, and the alternative access schemes offered in their place, function less as sincere privacy protections than as instruments for frustrating critical, independent, public-interest scholarship capable of holding platforms accountable for hate speech, abuse, and disinformation. Writing from an explicitly "data activist" stance within critical platform studies, Bruns characterises Facebook and its peers as "Big Social," a corporate sector that, like tobacco, pharma, and fossil fuels, deploys fear-uncertainty-doubt tactics to discredit its critics.

## Key Contributions

- Coins and develops the concept of the **"APIcalypse"** to frame the post-Cambridge Analytica collapse of research data access.
- Structured critique of the **Social Science One** corporate data-philanthropy model and its constraints on scholarly independence.
- Situates platform behaviour within a lineage of corporate FUD campaigns, proposing the notion of **"Big Social."**
- Offers a **typology of four strategic responses** for researchers: walk away, lobby, accommodate/acquiesce, or break the rules.
- Issues a call to action urging scholars to demand transparent, research-specific data access and sustained pressure on platforms and policymakers.

## Methods

Critical review of platforms' own corporate statements and their coverage in mainstream media, combined with analysis of scholarly responses, open letters, and interventions by researchers, foundations, NGOs, and former insiders. Bruns performs a close reading of the unpublished "King-Persily paper" outlining Social Science One, and traces documentarily the shutdown or degradation of specific research tools (TwapperKeeper, Netvizz, Texifter, TCAT). The analytical posture is explicitly that of a data activist.

## Findings

- Facebook expanded API shutdowns (Instagram, Events, Groups, Pages) with little warning, disabling tools like Netvizz.
- Twitter added developer registration hurdles and terms restricting research on sensitive topics (political beliefs, race, religion), and let the scholarly-focused Texifter lapse.
- Facebook obfuscated its HTML to block non-API scraping, including ProPublica's political-ad plugin — showing evasion beyond APIs.
- Platform "data grants" fund a tiny fraction of applicants (e.g., 6 of 1,300; 2 of 230+), amounting to "data lotteries."
- A *New York Times* investigation revealed Facebook hired opposition-research firm Definers to discredit critics, including invoking George Soros — evidence of bad-faith conduct.
- None of the four researcher pathways currently offers a sustainable long-term strategy.
