# The Rise of the Network Society

> Castells, M. (2009). The Rise of the Network Society. https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444319514
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444319514)

## Summary

This is the first volume of Manuel Castells' *Information Age* trilogy, presenting a systematic theory of the "network society" — a new social structure that crystallized at the end of the twentieth century around digital information and communication technologies. Castells argues that networks, freed from prior limits on scale and complexity by digital technology, have become the dominant organizing logic of the global economy, labor, culture, space, and time. This second edition (2010) adds a new preface reassessing the original 1996 thesis against subsequent developments — most notably the 2008 financial crisis, the acceleration of mobile/internet communication, and unprecedented global urbanization — arguing these events confirm rather than undermine the network society hypothesis.

## Key Contributions

- Develops a comprehensive theoretical vocabulary for the information age: network society, space of flows, timeless time, real virtuality, network enterprise, self-programmable vs. generic labor, and (in later work) mass self-communication.
- Integrates economy, technology, work, culture, and spatial/temporal transformation into one coherent account of macro-structural social change, filling a perceived gap left by classical sociological theory on post-industrial transition.
- Introduces the "metropolitan region" as the characteristic urban form of network society, offering a networked theory of urbanism against "end of cities" predictions.
- Establishes a landmark, widely cited reference framework for studying globalization and ICTs across disciplines.

## Methods

Castells combines grounded comparative empirical social science with theoretical synthesis, deliberately avoiding futurology. The analysis draws on cross-regional research (US, Asia, Latin America, Europe), extensive statistical analysis of G-7 employment/occupational structure (1920–2005), macroeconomic and financial data on productivity, trade, FDI, and capital flows, and case studies of organizational forms (Toyotism, Cisco, East Asian business networks) and communication technologies (Minitel, Internet, wireless, YouTube, Second Life). He also synthesizes urban studies literature (Sassen, Hall, Taylor, Dear and Wolch) to build his spatial theory.

## Findings

- Technological change restructures rather than eliminates employment, producing a dualized labor market of "self-programmable" talent and "generic labor," fueling inequality.
- US productivity rose ~30% (1998–2008) while real wages stagnated (~2%), as financial services' share of profits quadrupled from 10% to 40%; household debt-to-income ratio soared from ~3% to 130% over the same period, underpinning the financial crisis.
- Internet users grew from under 40 million (1995) to ~1.5 billion (2009); wireless subscriptions exceeded 3.4 billion; the OECD/developing-world digital divide narrowed sharply from 80.6:1 (1997) to 5.8:1 (2007).
- The world crossed the 50% urban threshold, generating polycentric "metropolitan regions" (e.g., the ~60-million-person Pearl River Delta) rather than the demise of cities.
- Global networks are multilayered with distinct geographies; nodes of overlapping networks (London, New York) become dominant "mega-nodes," while face-to-face milieus remain critical for elite decision-making.
