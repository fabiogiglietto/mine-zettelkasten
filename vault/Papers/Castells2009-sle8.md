# The Rise of the Network Society

> Castells, M. (2009). The Rise of the Network Society. https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444319514
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781444319514)

## Summary

The first volume of Manuel Castells' *Information Age* trilogy advances a systematic theory of the **network society** — a qualitatively new social structure that crystallized at the close of the twentieth century around digital information and communication technologies. Castells argues that these technologies overcome traditional limits on the size and complexity of networks, allowing networked forms of organization to expand and reconfigure endlessly across economy, work, culture, space, and time. The result is a globally interconnected but selectively inclusive world in which some people and places are wired into value-generating flows while others are switched off. The 2010 second edition adds a substantial new preface revisiting the original hypotheses in light of the 2008 financial crisis, the communication revolution, and accelerating global urbanization.

## Key Contributions

- Supplies a comprehensive conceptual vocabulary for the information age: *network society*, *space of flows*, *timeless time*, *real virtuality*, *network enterprise*, *self-programmable vs. generic labor*, and *mass self-communication*.
- Integrates economy, technology, work, culture, and spatio-temporal transformation into a single coherent account of structural social change.
- Introduces the **metropolitan region** as the distinctive urban form of the network society, embedded in a networked theory of urbanism.
- Establishes a landmark, heavily cited reference for the social-scientific study of globalization and information technology.

## Methods

Castells describes his approach as grounded, comparative empirical social science rather than futurology — identifying observable trends and interpreting them theoretically. The analysis spans multiple regions (the USA, East and Pacific Asia, Latin America, Europe) and draws on extensive statistics: G-7 employment and occupational structure (1920–2005), macroeconomic and financial data (productivity, trade, FDI, capital flows, stock valuations), and case studies of organizational forms (Toyotism, the Cisco model, East Asian business networks) and communication technologies (Minitel, the Internet, wireless, YouTube). The theory of space synthesizes urban research from Sassen, Hall, the Loughborough group, and others.

## Findings

- Technological change restructured rather than reduced employment, producing a dual labor market of *self-programmable* "talent" and *generic* labor, driving rising inequality.
- US productivity grew nearly 30% cumulatively (1998–2008) while real wages rose only ~2%; financial services' share of profits climbed from 10% in the 1980s to 40% by 2007.
- US household debt as a share of disposable income rose from ~3% (1998) to ~130% (2008), feeding the mortgage and financial crisis — read as an outgrowth of the networked "global automaton" beyond regulatory control.
- Internet users grew from under 40 million (1995) to ~1.5 billion (2009); wireless subscriptions passed 3.4 billion (~52% of world population) by 2008; the OECD-vs-developing access ratio fell from 80.6:1 (1997) to 5.8:1 (2007).
- Contrary to "end of cities" predictions, the world crossed the 50% urban threshold, entering the largest urbanization wave in history, organized into polycentric metropolitan regions (e.g., the ~60-million Pearl River Delta).
- Global networks are multilayered with divergent geographies; overlapping "mega-nodes" (London, New York) dominate, yet face-to-face milieus remain essential for high-level decision-making.
