# Three Consequences of Big Data on the Practices and Scholarships of Political Communication

> Giglietto, F. (2020). Three Consequences of Big Data on the Practices and Scholarships of Political Communication. *COMUNICAZIONE POLITICA*. https://doi.org/10.3270/96423
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3270/96423)

## Summary

Giglietto offers a conceptual essay identifying three interlocking transformations that big data and ubiquitous digital platforms impose on political communication: the blurring of organizational boundaries through networked collective action, the proliferation of micro-targeted political advertising, and the strategic manipulation of attention through amplification. The paper argues that each vector reshapes both political practice and the methodological toolkit of scholars, and that the three interact in ways that undermine attempts to regulate any one of them in isolation. Drawing on cases from Cambridge Analytica to the Five Star Movement to Trump's Twitter, the essay maps how classical political communication frameworks strain against a high-choice, algorithmically mediated environment.

## Key Contributions

- Articulates a three-vector framework (networked publics, micro-targeted ads, attention amplification) for understanding big data's impact on political communication.
- Surfaces concrete methodological challenges: fragmented sources, incidental exposure, opaque ad targeting, and unreliable self-reported news exposure.
- Provides a critical read of platform transparency instruments such as Facebook's Ad Library, noting their partial coverage of targeting parameters.
- Bridges contemporary phenomena to classical media theory (Lazarsfeld, Granovetter, Meyrowitz) while engaging debates on connective action, networked propaganda, and the attention economy.

## Methods

Conceptual/theoretical synthesis rather than empirical study. Giglietto builds the argument through case-based discussion (Cambridge Analytica, Five Star Movement, Podemos, Occupy Wall Street, Yellow Vests, Umbrella Movement, Trump's Twitter) and by reviewing platform policies and prior scholarship on hybrid media systems, connective action, and attention markets.

## Findings

- Weak-tie networked publics let fringe or extremist groups support mainstream candidates without formal ties, granting plausible deniability and complicating boundary-drawing around "the campaign."
- Facebook's Ad Library, while valuable, omits targeting criteria and thus constrains inference about hidden variables.
- Despite public alarm, empirical evidence for the persuasive impact of micro-targeted ads remains thin.
- Attention feedback loops amplify already-popular content algorithmically and often spill it into legacy media.
- The homogenized visual format of social feeds erodes brand recognition, undermining traditional self-report measures of news exposure.
- Engagement signals (likes, shares) are weak proxies for actual reading or exposure.
- Amplification is harder to regulate than paid advertising because it leaves no money trail.
