# The public sphere on the beach

> Hartley, J., & Green, J. (2006). The public sphere on the beach. *European Journal of Cultural Studies*, *9*, 341–362. https://doi.org/10.1177/1367549406066077
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1367549406066077)

## Summary

This article introduces the concept of a **cultural public sphere** — an "interzone" where the politics of private life, culture, and identity become visible to mainstream politics — and distinguishes it from Habermas's rational-critical public sphere, from "counterpublics," and from Michael Warner's "discourse publics." Hartley and Green argue that cultural-political conflict originates in private and leisure pursuits (ethnic identity, music, sport, and in Australia especially the beach), circulates through media largely invisible to mainstream politics, and erupts into news only spectacularly. Using the December 2005 Cronulla Beach "race riots" in Sydney as a case study, they contend that mainstream media stage conflict through its most extreme participants — the "lunar right" and "alienated cultural Muslims" — to let the "missing middle" of ordinary citizens work through cultural tensions that lie beyond the reach of rational public debate.

## Key Contributions

- Articulates and operationalizes a **cultural public sphere** as an interzone between culture and politics, extending McGuigan's and Hartley's earlier formulations.
- Proposes a process model linking culture, politics, new media (which *provoke*), mainstream media (which *react*), and "troublemakers" (who *fight*) to explain how private cultural conflict enters public political debate.
- Extends the notion of the **counterpublic sphere** to right-wing and racist activism, not merely left-wing or progressive identity movements.
- Integrates Warner's "discourse public" into cultural studies, de-privileging print/reading and emphasizing circulation across media platforms.
- Reinterprets "the beach" — a longstanding object in Australian cultural studies — as a political site and stage for the cultural public sphere rather than a mere myth of ordinariness.
- Shows how "sub-radar" media (SMS, email, talk radio, websites) help form discourse publics outside mainstream control.

## Methods

The paper is a theoretical synthesis combined with a qualitative case study. It draws on Habermas, Sennett, Warner, Felski, McGuigan, Cunningham, and prior Hartley/McKee work, then applies these to the Cronulla riots. Methodologically, it uses a deliberately non-representative discourse analysis of newspaper, radio, television, and online sources (broadsheets and tabloids, "shock jock" talk radio, white-supremacist websites, Wikipedia), chosen to illustrate discursive positions rather than as a statistical sample. A contrasting illustrative case — a Melbourne beach celebration of a Danish royal birth — is used to show the cultural public sphere need not be violent.

## Findings

- The Cronulla riot was communal violence over cultural identity and "ownership" of the beach and nation, not a conventional riot against state institutions.
- The 4 December 2005 attack on two lifeguards became the editorialized catalyst ("an attack on us all"), linking beach etiquette to national belonging.
- Mobilization ran through media peripheral to mainstream news — SMS, email, "shock jocks," and sites like Stormfront — which both provoked and were later reported by the press.
- Mainstream media shifted from voicing outrage ("We want our beach back") to championing law and order ("Riots: Our Disgrace") after the violence.
- The events exemplify a "conservative redefinition" of the Australian imagined community that denies Muslim-Australians full membership (e.g. "I grew here, you flew here").
- Romantic egalitarianism and racist egalitarianism are presented as "two sides of the same coin" of affective "mateship" at home on the beach but not in rational political debate.
- Attempts to reframe the riots as mere criminality or a "police matter" reveal how poorly the political public sphere manages conflicts of identity and culture.

## Connections

This is an early-2000s cultural-studies reframing of public-sphere theory, and its emphasis on how peripheral, "sub-radar" media ignite conflict that mainstream outlets later amplify prefigures later work on hybrid and platformed media ecologies — connecting to [[Chadwick2013-ns]] on the hybrid media system and [[van-Dijck2018-up]] on platform society. Its extension of the "counterpublic" to right-wing and racist mobilization resonates with research on right-wing online networks and affective polarization such as [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]], while its framing of digital-native communal identity and belonging anticipates later platform-ethnographic concerns in [[Marwick2011-sl02]].
