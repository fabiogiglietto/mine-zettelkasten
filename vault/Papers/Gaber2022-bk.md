# “strategic lying”: The case of Brexit and the 2019 U.k. election

> Gaber, I., & Fisher, C. (2022). “strategic lying”: The case of Brexit and the 2019 U.k. election. *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, *27*, 460–477. https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161221994100
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161221994100)

## Summary

This conceptual paper theorizes **"strategic lying"** as a distinct campaigning tactic that has emerged from the evolution of political spin in the digital, post-truth era. Drawing on the 2016 Brexit referendum and the 2019 UK general election as case studies, Gaber and Fisher argue that strategic lying functions as an agenda-setting and priming device: a deliberate falsehood is deployed not to persuade on its own terms but to set and frame the news agenda, with the truth-value of the claim being irrelevant to its effectiveness. Crucially, they position strategic lying as an *intensification* of established public relations practices — enabled by professionalized political communication and the amplifying affordances of networked media — rather than a radical break from spin. The central paradox they identify is that rebutting a strategic lie inadvertently amplifies and legitimizes it, spreading the original claim further than any correction.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and theorizes **"strategic lying"** as a specific development within the tradition of political spin.
- Distinguishes it from adjacent concepts: "fake news," "wicked content," the reactive "dead cat strategy," and ordinary spin.
- Situates the phenomenon within agenda-setting, priming, and intermedia agenda-setting frameworks, showing that amplification-via-rebuttal is *exploited* rather than accidental.
- Argues for continuity-and-evolution (versus rupture) in the spin-to-post-truth debate, linking the tactic to populism and hybrid media systems.

## Methods

A conceptual/theoretical paper grounded in agenda-setting theory (McCombs and Shaw), priming, and framing. It builds two qualitative case studies — the 2016 Brexit referendum and the 2019 UK general election, with the Trump presidency as a supplementary US comparison — synthesized with cross-disciplinary literature from political communication, PR, populism studies, and behavioral science (confirmation bias, cognitive dissonance, repetition effects). Documentary evidence includes Dominic Cummings's blog, fact-checking reports (Full Fact, First Draft), Labour Together's post-election review, news coverage, and social media data.

## Findings

- The Leave campaign's marquee claims — the £350m-a-week EU contribution and Turkey joining the EU — were misleading statements deployed to "provoke argument" and keep sovereignty and immigration on the agenda.
- The 2019 Conservative campaign systematically used strategic lies: a doctored Keir Starmer video, an exaggerated £1.2 trillion Labour spending figure (versus ~£98bn per the IFS), false claims of 40 new hospitals and 50,000 new nurses, and denial of Northern Ireland border checks.
- The Conservatives created deceptive assets, including a fake "Factcheck UK" account (@CCHQPress) during a leaders' debate; First Draft found 88% of Conservative Facebook ads contained suspect information versus 6.7% for Labour.
- Fact-checking has limited effect: corrections fade while the original lie persists, and partisans discount corrections ("they might be a liar, but they are my liar").
- Social media, via distributed spin and network logic, accelerates the spread of falsehoods, which travel farther and faster than truth.
- Post-campaign polling and media monitoring showed Brexit remained the most salient issue, correlating with (though not causally proven by) the strategic lying campaigns.
