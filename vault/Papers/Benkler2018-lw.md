# Network propaganda: Manipulation, disinformation, and radicalization in American politics

> Benkler, Y. R., Farris, R., & Roberts, H. (2018). Network propaganda: Manipulation, disinformation, and radicalization in American politics. *Aquila Digital Community (University of Southern Mississippi)*.
>
> [View paper](https://aquila.usm.edu/katrinagulfcoast_photos/42)

## Summary

*Network Propaganda* is a data-driven investigation into the sources of disinformation, manipulation, and epistemic dysfunction in American political communication, centering on the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the first year of the Trump presidency. Drawing on the analysis of roughly four million political stories collected between 2015 and 2018, Benkler, Faris, and Roberts argue that the American "epistemic crisis" is fundamentally **asymmetric and partisan** in shape rather than a symmetric case of two-sided polarization. Their central claim is that an insulated, radicalized right-wing media ecosystem — not technology, Russia, or Facebook alone — is the primary engine of confusion and distrust. To explain this, they develop the concept of a **propaganda feedback loop**: a dynamic in which the right-wing media sphere became structurally cut off from truth-correcting journalistic norms and therefore uniquely susceptible to lies and half-truths.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **"network propaganda"** and the **"propaganda feedback loop"** as analytical frameworks linking media-ecosystem architecture to susceptibility to manipulation.
- Provides large-scale empirical evidence for the **asymmetric** (not symmetric) nature of U.S. media polarization, challenging both "the internet polarizes everyone" and false-equivalence narratives.
- Revives precise definitions of propaganda, disinformation, misinformation, bullshit, and disorientation grounded in the intellectual history of propaganda studies.
- Advances a **"political economy view of technology"** — a transferable method for studying any national media ecosystem in its local institutional context rather than through context-free technological determinism.
- Reframes disparate political events (Roy Moore's nomination, calls to purge the FBI, Fox–White House alignment) as facets of a single conflict between Trumpism and Reaganism over the Republican Party.

## Methods

Large-scale computational analysis of ~4 million political stories (April 2015–March 2018) using hyperlink analysis plus Twitter and Facebook sharing data, collected and parsed via the **Media Cloud** platform. Network structures were mapped and visualized with Gephi, and supplemented with TV Archive/GDELT data, polling (YouGov, Gallup), and existing literature. The authors combine macro-level network mapping with targeted case studies (immigration/Islamophobia framing, Fox News coverage, the propaganda pipeline), applying robustness checks and multiple specifications to their findings.

## Findings

- Influence within the right-wing ecosystem is highly skewed toward the far right and insulated from the rest of the network; **no comparable asymmetry exists on the left**.
- Prominent left-leaning sites are tightly integrated with norm-adhering mainstream outlets (NYT, Washington Post), which act as a corrective check — a function absent on the right.
- Mainstream 2016 coverage was dominated by horserace framing and largely adopted the agenda of the right-wing media and Trump campaign; Clinton coverage foregrounded scandals while Trump coverage foregrounded his issues.
- **Facebook** appears more polluted than Twitter or the open web, with Facebook-prominent sites more prone to false and hyperpartisan content on both sides.
- Radical sites (Breitbart, Infowars, Gateway Pundit, Zero Hedge) drove radicalization without depending on decentralized alt-right networks, which were relatively isolated.
- Right-wing sites attacked even core Republican figures (e.g., a smear of Jeb Bush's "Nazi ties"), justifying the term **radicalization** over polarization.
- The asymmetric architecture **predates Trump**, tracing back to Rush Limbaugh's 1988 talk-radio model, with Trump acting as "catalyst in chief."
