# “gaming the system”: Platform paternalism and the politics of algorithmic visibility

> Petre, C., Duffy, B. E., & Hund, E. (2019). “gaming the system”: Platform paternalism and the politics of algorithmic visibility. *Social Media + Society*, *5*, 205630511987999. https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305119879995
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305119879995)

## Summary

This article interrogates how technology platforms discursively frame cultural producers' visibility-enhancement practices—SEO, reciprocal linking, automated engagement-boosting—as illegitimate "gaming the system." Through qualitative analysis of news coverage and platform guidelines surrounding three high-profile crackdowns, the authors argue that the boundary between legitimate strategy and illegitimate manipulation is nebulous, continually shifting, and ultimately reflective of platforms' commercial interests—even as it is justified in strongly normative, moral terms. They introduce **platform paternalism** to describe how platforms unilaterally issue rules and punishments framed as being in users' best interest, thereby legitimizing their own power and deepening inequalities among cultural workers.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and theorizes **platform paternalism** as a mechanism by which platforms establish and legitimize institutional *and moral* authority.
- Reframes "gaming the algorithm" as a politically charged, contested, and shifting category rather than a fixed, apolitical descriptor of practices.
- Shifts scholarly attention from content moderation toward the understudied phenomenon of system-gaming accusations and punitive "crackdowns."
- Empirically demonstrates how authenticity discourse and metaphor perform normative boundary work serving platforms' commercial interests.
- Connects platform governance to the reproduction of inequality in cultural production, showing how gaming accusations deepen winner-take-all dynamics.

## Methods

Comparative analysis of three heuristic case studies ("data outcroppings") in which major platforms accused cultural producers of algorithmic manipulation: Google demoting Rap Genius for link schemes (2013), Facebook demoting "engagement bait" (2017), and Instagram shutting down the botting service Instagress (2017). The authors conducted qualitative content analysis of 105 news articles (gathered via time-specified Google News searches) and 7 official platform statements and content-creation guidelines, using inductive coding in Dedoose following a grounded-theory approach. Cases were deliberately selected to vary by the accused parties' status and resources—a well-funded startup, a universal policy application, and independent creators—to examine interaction with existing material inequalities.

## Findings

- Platforms and press construct a **moral boundary** between condemned "gaming" and permitted practices, consistently organized around the ideal of authenticity.
- Three recurring metaphor clusters emerge: *organic versus artificial* (co-opting associations with virtue and neutrality), *crime and deviance* (creators as "offenders," platforms as law enforcement), and *games and cheating* (platforms as fair referees).
- Platforms invoke an archetypal "Strict Father" authority (Lakoff), expecting obedience and meting out retributive punishment via demotions, penalty boxes, and bans.
- Facebook's abrupt reversal—labeling as "engagement bait" the very prompts it had earlier recommended as best practices—illustrates the arbitrary, ephemeral nature of the gaming/legitimate boundary.
- Media coverage largely reproduced platform framing uncritically and held individual creators culpable.
- Punishments disproportionately harmed less-resourced producers; venture-backed Rap Genius could fund recovery while independent creators who lost Instagress turned to similarly disparaged "pods."

## Connections

This paper extends critical work on algorithmic visibility and the politics of being seen, connecting directly to [[Bucher2017-qh]]'s algorithmic imaginary and [[Cotter2019-js]]'s analysis of how creators learn and respond to platform ranking logics. Its concern with authenticity as a disciplinary ideal resonates with [[Marwick2011-sl02]] on self-presentation under platform conditions, while its account of unaccountable platform governance speaks to broader debates on the platform society such as [[van-Dijck2018-up]].
