# Does the method affect the outcome? How measures of partisan slant of media outlets and affective polarization drive results about polarization in the United States

> Schemer, C., Langmann, K., Hasell, A., & Weeks, B. (2026). Does the method affect the outcome? How measures of partisan slant of media outlets and affective polarization drive results about polarization in the United States. *Political Communication*, 1–25. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2699105
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2699105)

## Summary

This study asks whether the well-documented inconsistencies in findings about partisan media and polarization in the United States stem from genuine substantive disagreement or from methodological choices in measurement. Using three-wave panel survey data from the 2020 U.S. presidential election, the authors deploy specification curve analysis (SCA) across 504 combinations of ways to operationalize media partisanship (the independent variable) and affective/belief polarization (the dependent variables). They find that these measurement decisions substantially move the strength and statistical significance of the observed relationships—though rarely their direction—demonstrating that whether partisan media appear to be a democratic problem is heavily contingent on how scholars measure both constructs. The paper is explicitly a methodological reflexivity contribution, urging the field to justify its choices and report robustness checks.

## Key Contributions

- A systematic, empirical demonstration via 504 specifications in an SCA framework of how measurement choices shape conclusions about partisan media and polarization.
- Documentation and quantification of the inflationary effect of audience-based partisanship measures relative to content-based ones.
- Exposure of partisan and candidate-level asymmetries hidden by bipolar partisanship scores and difference-score polarization measures.
- Concrete best-practice recommendations: justify measurement choices, run sensitivity analyses, report both bipolar and disaggregated results, and prefer content-based measures when studying content effects.
- A contribution to the normative debate about whether partisan media and polarization are serious social problems, showing interpretations hinge on method.

## Methods

Original three-wave YouGov online panel survey during the 2020 U.S. election (September–October 2020; wave-1 n = 1,800, 1,401 reinterviewed in wave 2). Media use was captured with a list-frequency technique across 63 prominent U.S. outlets. The authors compiled 17 Media Partisanship Scores (MPS) from prior literature spanning three method types—analytic content-based (e.g., Groseclose, Gentzkow), holistic content-based (e.g., Budak, AllSides, MBFC), and audience-based (e.g., Bakshy, Robertson, Pew, Kim)—yielding 42 independent-variable specifications via weighting and categorization. Twelve dependent-variable specifications covered nine affective polarization measures (feeling thermometers and trust for Trump, Biden, and their supporters) and three belief polarization measures. SCA (R package `specr`) ran all 504 combinations with variance decomposition, plus robustness checks excluding Independents and lagged-dependent-variable regressions.

## Findings

- Relationships were mostly positive: more conservative media diets predicted greater favorability of and trust in Trump and misperceptions favoring Trump.
- Coefficient sizes varied substantially (mean b = .33); the largest significant estimate (b = .47) was nearly seven times the smallest (b = .07).
- Audience-based MPS produced the largest coefficients (M = .35), analytic content-based the smallest (Groseclose M = .21, Gentzkow M = .26), with holistic in between (M = .33).
- Categorical MPS yielded larger estimates than non-categorical; frequency weighting generally made little difference.
- About 56% of coefficient variation was attributable to MPS choice and 42% to dependent-variable choice.
- Disaggregating bipolar scores revealed asymmetry: conservative diets showed stronger relationships (absolute M = .24) than liberal diets (M = -.19); centrist diets were small or nonsignificant.
- Trust and misperception measures produced stronger coefficients than feeling thermometers.
- Longitudinal (lagged) estimates were consistently small (largest b = .06); excluding Independents reduced estimates by roughly 20%.

## Connections

This paper's use of audience-based exposure measures and its concern with how media diets relate to partisan outcomes connects to work on exposure and selective consumption in social media platforms such as [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]. Its methodological focus on researcher degrees of freedom and measurement sensitivity resonates broadly with the partisan-media and polarization literature in this register, though most other listed papers address platform dynamics and content diffusion rather than the measurement of media slant directly.
