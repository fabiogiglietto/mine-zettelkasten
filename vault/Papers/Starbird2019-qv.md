# Disinformation as Collaborative Work: Surfacing the Participatory Nature of Strategic Information Operations

> Starbird, K., Arif, A., & Wilson, T. (2019). Disinformation as Collaborative Work: Surfacing the Participatory Nature of Strategic Information Operations. *Proc. ACM Hum.-Comput. Interact.*, *3*, 1–26.
>
> [View paper](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Disinformation%20as%20Collaborative%20Work%3A%20Surfacing%20the%20Participatory%20Nature%20of%20Strategic%20Information%20Operations)

## Summary

This foundational paper reconceives strategic information operations—disinformation, propaganda, and online manipulation—as a form of collaborative *work* enacted within and through online crowds, rather than something simply "done to" passive audiences. Drawing on a CSCW and sociotechnical lens attentive to the mutual shaping of technology, social structure, and human action, Starbird and colleagues argue that reductive framings centered on "bots" and paid "trolls" obscure the participatory reality of these campaigns, in which authentic actors and "unwitting agents" play crucial amplifying roles. Through three long-term case studies, they develop a typology of orchestrated, cultivated, and emergent operations and argue that the central analytic challenge—for researchers, platform designers, and policymakers alike—is distinguishing coordinated campaigns from organic crowd behavior.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes strategic information operations as collaborative "work," integrating organizational work (big-"W") and distributed crowd work (little-"w") through a sociotechnical lens.
- Offers a typology distinguishing **orchestrated**, **cultivated**, and **emergent/self-sustaining** operations, each illustrated by a case study.
- Recasts disinformation as a *campaign*—a persistent collection of information-actions built around a plausible "rational core"—rather than a property of individual content items.
- Critiques platform policies premised on "coordinated inauthentic behavior" as inadequate for operations involving authentic actors or self-sustaining community activity, arguing instead for campaign-level, provenance-based, substantive policy.
- Situates the phenomenon historically (Soviet *active measures*, *dezinformatsiya*) and demonstrates the relevance of CSCW theory and methods to disinformation research and policy.

## Methods

A grounded, interpretivist, mixed-method approach combines qualitative, quantitative, and visual analysis of digital trace data, adapting grounded theory and drawing on trace and network ethnography plus crisis-informatics techniques for rapid social media data collection. Analysis iterates between macro-level views (network graphs, descriptive statistics) and close reading of tweets, accounts, and articles, with explicit attention to researcher reflexivity across a multi-national investigator team. Three long-term case studies (each spanning a year or more) integrate original analyses with journalistic and other researchers' accounts: (1) #BlackLivesMatter and 2016 shooting discourse cross-checked against released RU-IRA account lists; (2) Twitter and cross-platform discourse around the White Helmets, including URL/link and media-ecosystem analysis; and (3) "crisis actors" / "false flag" rumors across 2013–2016 events with tweet-level coding and a domain co-sharing network graph.

## Findings

- **Case 1 (RU-IRA):** ~300 impersonation accounts (posing as African American activists and white conservatives) produced about a third of English-language tweets but drew 85% of retweets (18.5M); the operation was top-down yet strategically embedded into pre-existing organic movements it did not create.
- **Case 2 (White Helmets):** Anti–White Helmets voices out-numbered and out-produced supporters, but the core critical accounts were largely authentic "information activists" and Western "journalists," amplified by state media (RT, Sputnik). A tightly linked "echo-system" of websites copy-pasted identical content across ideological wrappers to game search—best described as *cultivated*.
- **Case 3 (crisis conspiracy theorizing):** Conspiracy narratives emerge organically from communities of "true believers" sharing an anti-institutional epistemology, supported by an alternative and state-affiliated media ecosystem—largely emergent and self-sustaining, but partly shaped by longstanding operations.
- Across cases, substantial overlap in supporting media domains reveals a **shared disinformation infrastructure**.
- Organic public participation can grow in importance relative to orchestrated efforts as users adopt disinformation-aligned narratives as expressions of identity.

## Connections

This paper is a cornerstone of the coordinated-inauthentic-behavior literature and directly informs later work by the same group, such as [[Starbird2025-jj]]. Its critique of the "coordinated inauthentic behavior" frame speaks to research operationalizing and detecting coordination in trace data, including [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]], [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], and [[Nizzoli2020-cf]], while its account of participatory identity-driven sharing connects to work on partisan disinformation dynamics such as [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]]. The emphasis on media ecosystems and provenance also resonates with structural analyses of information disorder like [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Bennett2018-ys]].
