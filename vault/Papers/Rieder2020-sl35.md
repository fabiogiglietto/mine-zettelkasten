# Towards platform observability

> Rieder, B., & Hofmann, J. (2020). Towards platform observability. *Internet Policy Review*. https://doi.org/10.14763/2020.4.1535
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.14763/2020.4.1535)

## Summary

This conceptual article challenges the dominant regulatory faith in **transparency** as the primary mechanism for holding digital platforms accountable. Rieder and Hofmann argue that transparency rests on flawed assumptions — that visibility yields insight, and that insight yields control — and that the ubiquitous "black box" metaphor badly misrepresents algorithmic systems, which are opaque, complex, distributed across space and function, and continuously changing. In its place they propose **observability**: a pragmatic, knowledge-focused approach that treats accountability as a continuous, mediated social relation embedded in material conditions. The paper sets out three regulatory principles and culminates in a proposal for a well-resourced European Platform Observatory backed by binding law.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **observability** as a conceptual alternative/complement to transparency, foregrounding the practices, conditions, and processes of *observing* rather than one-off disclosure.
- Articulates three regulatory principles: (1) expand the normative and analytical horizon, using **public interest** as the benchmark; (2) observe platform behaviour continuously over time; (3) strengthen capacities for collaborative knowledge creation.
- Offers a typology and critical assessment of four practical data-access approaches for studying platforms.
- Proposes institutional mechanisms — "centres of expertise" and a mandated, well-funded **European Platform Observatory** with regulatory backing.

## Methods

Conceptual and theoretical article built on a critical literature review rather than empirical study. It synthesises critical scholarship on transparency, accountability, algorithm and platform studies, and regulation (Ananny & Crawford, Burrell, Seaver, Bovens, Flyverbom, and others). Arguments are illustrated with concrete regulatory proposals (EU platform-to-business regulation, the Digital Services Act debate, the US Filter Bubble Transparency Act, the German Medienstaatsvertrag) and observational projects (Facebook Data for Good and Ad Library, Social Science One, developer APIs, and scraping projects such as AlgoTransparency, Datenspende, and Ad Observer). The authors also draw on their experience in the European Commission's Observatory on the Online Platform Economy.

## Findings

- Transparency requirements *reconfigure* rather than merely reveal their objects, often producing unintended effects — bureaucratisation, distrust, and "window dressing."
- Machine-learning systems resist meaningful disclosure: models are inductively learned from vast data, involve too many variables to trace to single factors, and are distributed across subsystems, locations, and time.
- Existing data-access initiatives (Facebook Data for Good, Social Science One) are hampered by delays and restrictions, with platforms retaining control over scope and modalities, keeping information asymmetries intact.
- Four access approaches — negotiated data-access agreements, dedicated accountability interfaces, repurposed developer APIs, and scraping — each have strengths and limitations, and all face privacy concerns and dependence on platform acquiescence.
- Effective accountability requires a "critical audience" and diverse, collaborative knowledge forms (quantitative, qualitative, journalistic, NGO-driven), not just data access or audits.
