# Communicative Feedback Loops in the Digital Society

> Trilling, D. (2024). Communicative Feedback Loops in the Digital Society. *VU Research Portal*. https://doi.org/10.34669/wi.wjds/4.2.4
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.34669/wi.wjds/4.2.4)

## Summary

This conceptual paper argues that the ubiquitous but under-specified metaphors of "echo chambers" and "filter bubbles" should be replaced with a **feedback-loop perspective** on communication in the digital society. Trilling contends that these metaphors, while empirically contested, contain a kernel of truth about self-reinforcing selection processes — and that a cybernetics-informed feedback framing better reconciles the paradox that self-reinforcing dynamics are real yet do *not* inevitably produce catastrophic, universal radicalization. Drawing on cybernetics, systems theory, complexity science, and computational social science, the paper develops a typology of communicative feedback loops, offers five testable propositions, and maps a concrete methodological toolbox for studying them.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes the echo chamber/filter bubble debate through an integrative feedback-loop lens that renders the old metaphors "moot" while preserving their valid concerns.
- Introduces a novel typology distinguishing **within-human**, **between-human**, **human–algorithm**, and **pure algorithmic** feedback loops, bridging otherwise disjointed psychological, sociological, and computer-science literatures.
- Advances five testable propositions to structure a research agenda on digital communicative dynamics.
- Provides a practical methodological toolbox (agent-based modeling, algorithm audits/sock puppets, experience sampling, data donation, online field experiments) with references to open tools, lowering the resource barrier to studying feedback loops.
- Argues for shifting research designs from "large N, small T" toward "small N, large T" to capture temporal dynamics.

## Methods

The paper is a conceptual and theoretical synthesis rather than an empirical study. It integrates cybernetics (Ashby), Slater's reinforcing spiral model, the dynamic-transactional approach, and gatekeeping theory, and reviews prior empirical work on recommender systems, misinformation, and radicalization. Two tables anchor the argument: a typology of feedback-loop types mapped to theoretical foundations, and a comparison of empirical approaches. A notable methodological claim is that standard causal-inference tools built on directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) explicitly forbid cycles and are therefore inadequate for feedback-loop research, motivating simulation- and audit-based alternatives.

## Findings

- Contemporary recommender systems introduce randomness and diversity, and users correct them by clicking non-recommended items, producing **negative (self-regulating) feedback loops** — a thermostat, not runaway personalization (Proposition 2).
- Reinforcing relationships cannot be modeled linearly over time; at extremes they break or reverse (Proposition 1).
- Evidence on algorithmic radicalization is mixed: increasingly extreme recommendations were found on YouTube but not on Reddit or Gab.
- Mis- and disinformation spread partly because *mainstream* media attention — even critical coverage — amplifies it beyond partisan in-groups (Proposition 4).
- Fringe groups exaggerate radical positions to gain newsworthiness, producing "fringe bubbles" and a "vicious cycle of counterpublics" (Proposition 5).
- Prior work found self-correcting loops in left-wing and non-partisan US media but escalating positive loops in right-wing media.
- Emergent system-level behavior often cannot be inferred from individual components alone (Proposition 3).
