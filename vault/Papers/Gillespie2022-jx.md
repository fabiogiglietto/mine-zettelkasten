# Do not recommend? Reduction as a form of content moderation

> Gillespie, T. (2022). Do not recommend? Reduction as a form of content moderation. *Social Media + Society*, *8*, 205630512211175. https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051221117552
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051221117552)

## Summary

Gillespie argues that debate about social media content moderation has been overly consumed by *removal*—deleting posts and banning users—while neglecting *reduction*: the growing practice of demoting the visibility or reach of problematic content without taking it down. Platforms deploy machine-learning classifiers to identify content deemed misleading, risky, or offensive enough to warrant demotion in rankings, recommendations, and search, but not deletion. The essay documents how major platforms implement and justify these techniques, offers a typology of reduction, and argues that reduction is a core—yet deliberately obscured—form of platform governance with troubling implications for transparency, accountability, and equity.

## Key Contributions

- Names and defines **reduction** as a distinct, under-theorized category of content moderation, chosen deliberately over loaded alternatives like "borderline content," "shadowbanning," or "suppression."
- Offers a concrete **typology** grounded in how recommender systems work: *do not recommend at all*, *do not recommend as much*, and *do not recommend to some users* (implemented at the inventory/candidate-generation stage or the ranking stage).
- Documents and compares reduction policies and justifications across eight major platforms, surfacing practices platforms have publicly hidden.
- Reframes content moderation and algorithmic recommendation as a **single governance problem** rather than two separate functions handled by separate teams.
- Identifies that reduction is largely **invisible to transparency/accountability regimes** and may be inherently unmeasurable.
- Provides a reflexive methodological account of "studying up" in secretive firms under NDA constraints.

## Methods

Qualitative analysis of company policies, corporate communications (blog posts, transparency documents), and technical literature from academia and industry. Gillespie closely reads platform announcements—YouTube's 2019 "borderline content" post and "Four Rs" framework, Facebook/Instagram policies, and statements from Twitter, LinkedIn, TikTok, Tumblr, and Reddit. He supplements this with informal background conversations with a dozen-plus Trust & Safety and adjacent employees across five platforms, treated as background (not formal interviews) to respect NDAs, with confidential details cross-verified against published policy before inclusion. The paper closes with a reflexive discussion of access, positionality, and the ethics of studying powerful, secretive firms.

## Findings

- YouTube announced borderline-content reduction in January 2019, framed as improving "recommendation quality" rather than as Trust & Safety, later claiming a 70% average drop in watch time of such content from non-subscribed recommendations—a claim nearly impossible to independently verify.
- Facebook and Instagram announced reduction in May 2018 ("reduce its spread in News Feed using ranking"), later publishing detailed distribution/"demotion" guidelines for borderline content, harmful misinformation, and low-quality junk.
- Twitter, LinkedIn, TikTok, Tumblr (hashtag/search blocking), and Reddit ("quarantine") all use family-resemblance reduction strategies; Reddit has been comparatively explicit, most others less transparent than their removal policies.
- Justifications include appeals to user preference, Zuckerberg's "natural engagement pattern" theory (engagement rises near any line drawn), and a consent-based lower standard of responsibility for recommended vs. self-chosen content.
- Unstated motives include lower political risk (avoiding "censorship" accusations), continued financial benefit from demand for such content, and flexibility to police fast-evolving harms while evading accountability.
- Facebook appears to reuse existing removal classifiers (demoting borderline scores), while YouTube built a separate classifier trained by human raters using Google's search-quality evaluator guidelines.
- Reduction concentrates *more* curatorial power in private intermediaries by obscuring it, and reifies the very "virality-as-natural" logic it purports to correct.
