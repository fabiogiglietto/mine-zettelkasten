# What has happened to trust in news in the long run globally?

> Fletcher, R., & Nielsen, R. K. (2026). What has happened to trust in news in the long run globally?. *Mainstream News Media Trust*, 55–76. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003618041-6
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003618041-6)

## Summary

This chapter offers an unusually ambitious empirical description of how public trust in news has changed over nearly half a century worldwide. Combining the World Values Survey/European Values Study (1981–2022) with the Reuters Institute Digital News Report (2015–2025), Fletcher and Nielsen analyze roughly 1.5 million responses across 115 countries. They document a clear, consistent, but not universal decline in trust in news — one that predates the internet yet appears to accelerate from around 2000 as digital media displaced television. Two of their most striking claims cut against conventional wisdom: the supposed "Golden Age" of high public trust is a myth (even in the 1980s fewer than half the public trusted the news), and countries with the least free media have actually seen trust *rise*. The analysis is deliberately descriptive rather than causal, treating trust in news as a subjective, context-dependent social fact rather than a measure of trustworthiness.

## Key Contributions

- Extends the most ambitious prior comparative-longitudinal work (Hanitzsch et al. 2018, 53 countries to 2014) to 115 countries across nearly 50 years (1981–2025).
- Provides a systematic, empirically grounded, societal-level (rather than individual-level) account of long-term global trust-in-news trends.
- Quantifies how trust trends vary by media freedom context, surfacing the counterintuitive finding of rising trust in unfree environments.
- Debunks the assumption of a past "Golden Age" of majority trust in news.
- Demonstrates a reusable methodological approach for harmonizing and pooling heterogeneous cross-national trust measures for longitudinal multi-level analysis.

## Methods

The authors harmonized two large survey datasets — WVS/EVS (confidence in "the press") and the DNR ("trust most news most of the time") — by dichotomizing responses into trust/lack of trust, following an approach developed for political-trust research. The combined dataset covers 115 countries (~three-quarters of world population), with 632,711 WVS/EVS and 858,668 DNR observations. They fit linear multi-level models with centred year predicting trust, using random intercepts for country and a pooled model nesting responses within datasets within countries; a B-spline model (5 df) tested for non-linearity, and 140 OLS models across 94 countries produced country-level trends. Freedom House press-freedom data classified countries as consistently Free/Not Free and as Improving/Deteriorating/Fluctuating, then interacted with year. The stance is explicitly non-causal.

## Findings

- Trust declined significantly in WVS/EVS (~49% in the 1980s to ~39% in the 2020s) and slightly more steeply in the DNR (44% in 2015 to ~40% in 2025).
- The pooled model confirms a significant negative trend; the spline shows decline from 1981 accelerating after ~2000.
- Of the country-dataset combinations, 78 show a significant negative trend, 34 a positive trend, and 28 no significant trend.
- The US is an outlier — an extreme historical decliner (50% in 1981 to 23% in 2011) but low-and-stable recently (32% in 2015 to 30% in 2025) — and should not be treated as globally representative.
- Countries consistently rated "Not Free" saw trust *rise* since the 1980s, while Free and all changing-freedom groups declined.
- Between-country and between-dataset variance vastly outweighs the temporal effect (marginal R² ~.001–.009), underscoring the strong contextual character of trust.
- Question wording matters: Japan shows higher "confidence in the press" than "trust in news," while the UK shows the reverse.
