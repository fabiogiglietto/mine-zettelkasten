# What has happened to trust in news in the long run globally?

> Fletcher, R., & Nielsen, R. K. (2026). What has happened to trust in news in the long run globally?. *Mainstream News Media Trust*, 55–76. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003618041-6
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003618041-6)

## Summary

This chapter offers the most ambitious longitudinal-comparative account to date of public trust in news, combining the World Values Survey/European Values Study (1981–2022) with the Reuters Institute Digital News Report (2015–2025) to analyze roughly 1.5 million responses across 115 countries over nearly half a century. The authors document a clear, consistent, but not universal decline in trust in news — one that predates the internet yet appears to accelerate from around 2000 as digital media displaced television as the dominant news source. Crucially, they debunk the myth of a past "Golden Age" of high trust (majority trust has always been the exception) and show that trends diverge sharply by media-freedom context, with authoritarian "Not Free" countries actually seeing trust *rise*. The analysis is deliberately descriptive rather than causal, treating trust in news as a subjective, relational, context-dependent social fact.

## Key Contributions

- Extends the prior benchmark comparative-longitudinal work (Hanitzsch et al. 2018, 53 countries to 2014) to 115 countries and ~50 years (1981–2025).
- Provides a systematic, empirically grounded, descriptive account of long-term global trust-in-news trends at the societal (rather than individual) level.
- Quantifies how trust trends vary by media-freedom context, including the counterintuitive rise of trust in unfree environments.
- Debunks the assumption of a past "Golden Age" of majority trust in news.
- Demonstrates a reusable methodological approach for harmonizing and pooling heterogeneous cross-national trust measures for longitudinal multi-level analysis.

## Methods

The authors harmonized two large survey datasets — WVS/EVS (confidence in "the press," 632,711 responses) and the DNR ("trust most news most of the time," 858,668 responses) — into a combined dataset of ~1.5 million observations covering about three-quarters of the world's population. Responses were dichotomized into trust/lack-of-trust following an approach developed for political-trust research (Valgardsson et al. 2025). They fit linear multi-level models with centred year predicting trust, using random intercepts for country and a pooled model nesting responses within datasets within countries; a five-degree B-spline model tested for non-linearity. Some 140 OLS models across 94 countries displayed country-level trends. Freedom House press-freedom data (1980–2016) classified countries as consistently Free/Not Free and as Improving/Deteriorating/Fluctuating, interacted with year in the pooled model. The stance is explicitly descriptive, not causal.

## Findings

- Trust declined significantly in WVS/EVS (b = −.002; ~49% in the 1980s to 39% in the 2020s) and slightly more steeply in DNR (b = −.004; 44% in 2015 to ~40% in 2025).
- The pooled model confirms a significant negative trend; a spline model shows a slight decline from 1981 that accelerates from around 2000.
- Of country-dataset combinations, 78 show a significant negative trend, 34 positive, and 28 non-significant — decline is general but not universal.
- The US is an outlier: a sharp historical decline (50% in 1981 to 23% in 2011) but low-but-stable in the DNR period; it should not be treated as representative of global trends.
- Consistently "Not Free" countries saw trust *rise* since the 1980s, while "Free" and all changing-freedom groups saw declines.
- Between-country and between-dataset variance vastly outweighs the temporal effect (marginal R² ~.001–.009), underscoring trust's strongly contextual character.
- Question wording matters: "the press" vs. "news" is interpreted differently (e.g., Japan vs. the UK show opposite dataset gaps).
