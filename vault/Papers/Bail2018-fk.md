# Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase political polarization

> Bail, C. A., Argyle, L. P., Brown, T. W., Bumpus, J. P., Chen, H., Hunzaker, M. B. F., Lee, J., Mann, M., Merhout, F., & Volfovsky, A. (2018). Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase political polarization. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *115*, 9216–9221. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1804840115
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1804840115)

## Summary

This preregistered field experiment tests one of the most optimistic assumptions in debates about online political division: that exposing partisans to opposing views will foster moderation. Democrats and Republicans who used Twitter at least three times weekly were paid to follow a bot that retweeted a steady stream of messages from elected officials, media, and opinion leaders of the opposing ideology for one month. Rather than reducing polarization, the intervention produced a **backfire effect**: Republicans became substantially more conservative, while Democrats showed a slight (non-significant) shift toward more liberal attitudes. The study pits intergroup contact theory against theories of motivated reasoning, and concludes that exposure-based interventions to bridge partisan divides may be ineffective or actively counterproductive.

## Key Contributions

- Provides experimental, real-world evidence of **asymmetric partisan backfire** from prolonged cross-partisan exposure on social media.
- Advances computational social science methodology by combining survey research, bot technology, and digital trace data with behavioral verification of treatment compliance.
- Introduces a **bot-based intervention** that disrupts selective exposure in a naturalistic longitudinal setting rather than a one-shot survey experiment.
- Offers a practical caution for depolarization efforts, indicating exposure-based interventions may backfire—especially those initiated by liberals.

## Methods

- Preregistered field experiment run separately for Democrats and Republicans, with 1,652 pretreatment respondents (901 Democrats, 751 Republicans) recruited via a professional survey firm.
- Block randomization stratified by party attachment, interest in current events, and Twitter use, using an "ostensibly unrelated" survey design.
- Treated respondents were paid $11 to follow a bot retweeting 24 opposing-ideology messages per day for one month; messages were sampled from 4,176 political accounts scored on a liberal/conservative dimension via correspondence analysis of following patterns.
- Ideology measured with a 10-item, seven-point policy attitude scale (α = .91) pre- and posttreatment.
- Compliance monitored through incentivized weekly surveys (up to $18), including identification of a daily animal picture, defining three compliance tiers.
- Analysis used multivariate models predicting posttreatment ideology controlling for pretreatment score plus 12 covariates, reporting both Intent-to-Treat (ITT) and Complier Average Causal Effects (CACE).

## Findings

- Treated Republicans became significantly **more conservative** (ITT = 0.12, p = 0.008), with effects rising with compliance.
- Fully compliant Republicans shifted 0.60 points more conservative (CACE, p < 0.01), roughly 0.11–0.59 standard deviations.
- Treated Democrats showed slightly more liberal attitudes increasing with compliance, but none reached statistical significance.
- Compliance was moderate: 64.9% of Democrats and 57.2% of Republicans accepted the bot; ~62% answered all weekly content questions and 50.2% identified the daily animal picture.
- Supplementary analyses argue results are not driven by Hawthorne effects, partisan learning, message extremity, or age-based platform differences.
