# Online Discourse is Falsely Polarized by Self-Selection

> Schulz, W., Robertson, C. E., Sjåstad, H., Ashokkumar, A., Oswald, L., Yang, J., Munzert, S., Bavel, J. J. V., & Guess, A. M. (2026). Online Discourse is Falsely Polarized by Self-Selection. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7z69m_v1
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7z69m_v1)

## Summary

This paper argues that online political discourse appears far more polarized than the underlying attitudes of social media users actually are — a phenomenon the authors term "false polarization." Rather than attributing this gap to platform algorithms or echo chambers, they locate its primary mechanism in **self-selection**: users with stronger or more extreme ideological attitudes are disproportionately likely to post political content. The visible distribution of opinion online is therefore a biased sample of the population's actual views, systematically overstating division. Combining a decade of linked behavioral data, controlled experiments, and a 40-country survey, the authors show this effect is robust, generalizable, and measurable.

## Key Contributions

- Provides comprehensive, multi-method evidence quantifying the magnitude and scope of false polarization on social media.
- Identifies **selective posting behavior** — not algorithmic curation or exposure — as a central mechanism generating the *appearance* of heightened polarization.
- Establishes cross-national generalizability through a large 40-country survey (N = 49,222).
- Reframes a prominent debate: observed online polarization may be partly an artifact of *who chooses to speak*, rather than evidence of genuine attitude divergence.

## Methods

The paper triangulates across three data streams:

- **Observational:** analysis of over a decade of social media behavior linked to individual ideological attitudes, comprising more than 1.7 million posts.
- **Experimental:** a series of controlled experiments probing posting decisions and perceptions of polarization.
- **Survey:** a global survey spanning 40 countries with a combined sample of N = 49,222.

## Findings

- Individuals with stronger ideological attitudes exhibit a greater propensity to post political content, biasing the observable online opinion distribution.
- The visible discourse consistently overstates the degree of polarization relative to the actual spread of user attitudes.
- The selective-posting-driven false polarization appears coherently across observational, experimental, and cross-national evidence, suggesting it is not an artifact of any single setting or platform.

## Connections

This work speaks directly to debates over whether online environments manufacture or merely reflect political division, complementing research on echo chambers and ideological segregation such as [[Bakshy2015-rn]], [[Barbera2015-fw]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], and [[Nyhan2023-gb]], while shifting the causal focus from algorithmic exposure to self-selected expression. Its account of a vocal, ideologically extreme minority distorting perceived consensus connects to work on affective polarization ([[Iyengar2019-jj]]) and on how social media interventions shape political attitudes ([[Bail2018-fk]]).
