# Online Discourse is Falsely Polarized by Self-Selection

> Schulz, W., Robertson, C. E., Sjåstad, H., Ashokkumar, A., Oswald, L., Yang, J., Munzert, S., Bavel, J. J. V., & Guess, A. M. (2026). Online Discourse is Falsely Polarized by Self-Selection. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7z69m_v1
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/7z69m_v1)

## Summary

This paper argues that the political polarization we observe on social media is substantially an *artifact of who chooses to speak* rather than an accurate reflection of underlying attitudes. The authors introduce and quantify "false polarization" online: the visible distribution of political opinion appears more divided than users' actual attitudes warrant. The central mechanism is self-selection in posting — people with stronger or more extreme ideological attitudes are disproportionately likely to share political content, thereby distorting the observable discourse. Combining more than a decade of linked behavioral data, controlled experiments, and a 40-country survey, the paper makes a case that online polarization is at least partly an illusion produced by selective participation.

## Key Contributions

- Provides comprehensive, multi-method evidence quantifying the magnitude and scope of false polarization on social media.
- Identifies selective posting behavior as a concrete mechanism generating the *appearance* of heightened polarization.
- Establishes cross-national generalizability of the effect through a large 40-country survey.
- Reframes debates about online polarization by distinguishing genuine attitude divergence from an artifact of who chooses to post.

## Methods

The paper triangulates across three data sources: (1) observational analysis of more than a decade of social media behavior linked to individual ideological attitudes, encompassing over 1.7 million posts; (2) a series of controlled experiments probing posting behavior and perceptions of polarization; and (3) a global survey spanning 40 countries with a combined sample of N = 49,222. The convergence of observational, experimental, and cross-national evidence is central to the argument for robustness and generalizability.

## Findings

- Individuals with stronger ideological attitudes exhibit a greater propensity to post political content, biasing the observable opinion distribution.
- The visible discourse consistently *overstates* the degree of polarization relative to underlying user attitudes.
- This selective-posting-driven false polarization appears consistently across time, experimental settings, and many countries.

## Connections

This paper speaks directly to work distinguishing perceived from actual polarization and to studies of misperceptions of online opinion, complementing analyses of who participates and how platform dynamics shape visible discourse such as [[Falkenberg2026-ka]] and [[Goldberg2026-eb]]. Its behavioral, self-selection account also connects to research on political sharing and engagement online, including [[Brady2026-ln]] and [[Mosleh2024-op]], and its cross-national framing relates to comparative work on political information environments like [[Eady2025-vm]]. It offers a corrective counterpoint to accounts that read observed online division as straightforward evidence of underlying attitude divergence.
