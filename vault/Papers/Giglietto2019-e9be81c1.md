# ‘Fake news’ is the invention of a liar: How false information circulates within the hybrid news system

> Giglietto, F., Iannelli, L., Valeriani, A., & Rossi, L. (2019). ‘Fake news’ is the invention of a liar: How false information circulates within the hybrid news system. *Current Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0011392119837536
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0011392119837536)

## Summary

This conceptual paper challenges the prevailing scholarly tendency to define "fake news" through the intent of content creators, arguing that such a creator-centric view fails to capture how false information actually circulates in a hybrid news system. Drawing on second-order cybernetics (von Foerster, Bateson, Shannon), Chadwick's hybrid media system framework, and Luhmann's systems theory, the authors propose shifting analytical attention from injectors/creators to propagators and the chains of judgment and decision that drive circulation. They develop a three-level analytical model — micro (individual judgments of truthfulness), meso (a 2×2 matrix of injector/propagator judgment combinations), and macro (emergent global cascades) — and illustrate it with three cases of misleading content spreading online. A central methodological move is distinguishing lowercase "true/false" (actors' own judgments) from capitalized "True/False" (the researcher's external assessment), articulating a constructivist-but-not-relativist stance.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes misinformation research by foregrounding **propagators** rather than creators as the central analytical object.
- Introduces a **second-order cybernetic** perspective (information as "a difference that makes a difference," observer-dependent judgments) into communication studies of misinformation.
- Proposes a **2×2 typology of propagation acts** based on injector and propagator truth judgments, capable of accommodating satire, "bullshit," and other hard-to-classify cases.
- Articulates a **multi-level (micro/meso/macro) model** linking individual judgment, dyadic propagation, and emergent cascades.
- Offers a theoretical critique of the term **"fake news"** itself as rhetorically loaded — a label that positions its user as a truth-claimer.

## Methods

This is a conceptual and theoretical paper, not an empirical study. It combines a literature review of classic and contemporary work on disinformation, misinformation, rumor, propaganda, conspiracy theory, satire, and "bullshit" with a synthesis of second-order cybernetics, Bateson's information theory, Chadwick's hybrid media system, and Luhmann's systems theory. The resulting framework is illustrated through analysis of three episodes: the photoshopped Veerender Jubbal image circulated after the 2015 Paris attacks; Chris Lamb's satirical Huffington Post piece on Trump and the Statue of Liberty; and the 2016 misidentified KKK "march" photo in Mebane, NC.

## Findings

- Most existing definitions treat the **generative act** of creating false content as decisive, neglecting downstream propagation dynamics.
- Four propagation typologies emerge from the matrix: pure disinformation, misinformation propagated through disinformation, disinformation propagated through misinformation, and pure misinformation.
- At the micro level, actors judge truthfulness based on **source** (authority, proximity), **content** (confirmation bias, congruence, relevance), and **context** (information overload, limited attention, "tweet first, verify later").
- Authority and proximity increasingly overlap on social media, weakening expertise-based source evaluation.
- Original intent does **not** determine the downstream cycle: satirical, mistaken, and deceptive items can all be propagated as true by mainstream actors.
- Global cascades are **emergent** phenomena — observable as autonomous wholes composed of heterogeneous individual propagations.
- Societal consensus about truthfulness is unlikely; coexisting opposing beliefs is the normal state of the hybrid news system.

## Connections

This paper's reliance on Chadwick's hybrid media system and its propagation-centric lens resonates with network- and cascade-oriented empirical studies of how false information spreads and who propagates it, such as [[Starbird2019-qv]], [[Osmundsen2021-et]], and [[Freelon2020-yp]]. Its constructivist critique of "fake news" as a rhetorically loaded label connects to definitional and conceptual work on disinformation and the weaponization of the term in [[Freelon2018-ao]] and [[Humprecht2020-gd]]. The authors' broader program on coordinated circulation within the hybrid system is continued in their own later work, including [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]] and [[Giglietto2026-855a54cb]].
