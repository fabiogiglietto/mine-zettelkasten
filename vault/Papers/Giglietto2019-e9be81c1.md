# ‘Fake news’ is the invention of a liar: How false information circulates within the hybrid news system

> Giglietto, F., Iannelli, L., Valeriani, A., & Rossi, L. (2019). ‘Fake news’ is the invention of a liar: How false information circulates within the hybrid news system. *Current Sociology*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0011392119837536
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0011392119837536)

## Summary

This conceptual paper argues that dominant approaches to "fake news" over-invest in the *intent of creators*, a framing ill-suited to a hybrid news system where content circulates through countless intermediary hands. Drawing on second-order cybernetics (von Foerster, Bateson), Luhmann, and Chadwick's hybrid media system, Giglietto and colleagues shift the analytical center of gravity from injectors to *propagators* and the chained judgments that carry false information through networks. They develop a three-level framework — micro (individual truthfulness judgments), meso (a 2×2 matrix of injector/propagator judgment combinations yielding four propagation typologies), and macro (emergent global cascades) — and illustrate it with three cases of misleading content. The upshot is that false information cycles are composite, that satire, mistake, and deception can all end up propagated as truth, and that the label "fake news" itself is a rhetorically loaded weapon rather than an analytical category.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes the study of false information around **propagators** rather than creators.
- Introduces a **2×2 propagation typology** (pure disinformation, misinformation-via-disinformation, disinformation-via-misinformation, pure misinformation) based on injector and propagator truth judgments.
- Articulates a **micro/meso/macro analytical model** linking individual judgment, dyadic sharing, and emergent cascades.
- Imports **second-order cybernetics** into communication research on misinformation, treating information as observer-dependent.
- Distinguishes lowercase actor-judged *true/false* from capitalized researcher-assessed *True/False*, staking out a constructivist-but-not-relativist stance.
- Provides a normative critique of the term "fake news" as inherently divisive and self-positioning.

## Methods

Purely theoretical. The authors synthesize literature on disinformation, misinformation, rumor, propaganda, satire, and Frankfurt's "bullshit"; graft it onto second-order cybernetics, Bateson, Chadwick, and Luhmann; and derive their multi-level model. They then walk through three illustrative real-world cases: the Veerender Jubbal photoshop after the 2015 Paris attacks, Chris Lamb's satirical HuffPo piece on Trump and the Statue of Liberty, and the 2016 misidentified KKK "march" photo in Mebane, NC.

## Findings

- Existing definitions privilege the *generative act* of creating false content and neglect downstream propagation.
- Micro-level truthfulness judgments rest on source cues (authority, proximity), content cues (confirmation bias, congruence, relevance), and context cues (overload, limited attention, "tweet first, verify later").
- In social media, authority and proximity increasingly collapse into each other, eroding expertise-based evaluation.
- Original intent does not determine the fate of a piece of false content: satirical, mistaken, and deceptive items are equally susceptible to being propagated as true by mainstream actors.
- Global cascades emerge as autonomous phenomena that cannot be reduced to any single propagation type.
- Societal consensus on truthfulness is unlikely; coexisting opposing beliefs are the *normal* condition of the hybrid news system.
