# The politics of ‘platforms’

> Gillespie, T. (2010). The politics of ‘platforms’. *New Media & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444809342738
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1461444809342738)

## Summary

Gillespie interrogates the word "platform" as a strategically ambiguous term deployed by online content intermediaries—YouTube in particular—to position themselves across incompatible audiences: users, advertisers, media partners, and regulators. Drawing on four semantic lineages of the term (computational, architectural, figurative, and political), he shows how its connotations of openness, neutrality, egalitarianism, and support let firms elide tensions between user-generated content populism and their actual reliance on commercial partnerships and editorial intervention. The paper warns that this rhetorical framing forecloses the critical vocabulary needed to hold intermediaries accountable for the substantive ways they shape public discourse.

## Key Contributions

- Establishes "platform" as a discursively loaded term whose ambiguity does political work for intermediaries.
- Provides a foundational framework that seeded subsequent platform studies and platform governance scholarship.
- Traces continuities between older structural metaphors in information policy (conduit, common carrier, network) and contemporary "platform" talk.
- Names the gap between intermediaries' neutrality claims and their editorial interventions, calling for sharper critical vocabulary.

## Methods

Critical discourse analysis anchored in an etymological reading of the OED's four senses of "platform." Gillespie closely reads corporate press releases, executive statements (Chad Hurley, Eric Schmidt), industry commentary (O'Reilly, Andreessen), and policy filings, and situates them against legal cases (Viacom v. YouTube), the DMCA safe harbor, and net neutrality debates. The approach sits within STS and media policy traditions that treat structural metaphors as constitutive of regulation.

## Findings

- YouTube strategically repositioned its self-description toward "distribution platform," reflecting a discursive shift.
- The term migrated from strictly computational usage toward figurative senses of participation and opportunity across the tech industry.
- YouTube's economics (roughly $240m revenue vs. $711m costs in 2009; ads paired with only ~9% of content) reveal a dependence on commercial partners that contradicts the egalitarian UGC framing.
- Tools like ContentID, algorithmic demotion, and content policies constitute editorial intervention despite neutrality rhetoric.
- "Platform" functions simultaneously as a marketing appeal to users, a pitch to advertisers, and a legal shield analogous to common carrier status.
