# Reshares on social media amplify political news but do not detectably affect beliefs or opinions

> Guess, A. M., Malhotra, N., Pan, J., Barberá, P., Allcott, H., Brown, T., Crespo-Tenorio, A., Dimmery, D., Freelon, D., Gentzkow, M., González-Bailón, S., Kennedy, E., Kim, Y. ## Summary

This paper reports one of the four large-scale randomized experiments conducted through the 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study (FIES), a collaboration between Meta and independent academic researchers. It isolates a single platform feature — resharing — by randomly assigning consenting US Facebook users to a "No Reshares" condition, whose feeds were stripped of reshared content for roughly three months around the 2020 election, versus an unchanged control. The intervention dramatically reshaped the content environment (cutting political news, untrustworthy-source content, and engagement), yet produced no detectable effects on political polarization or nearly any measured political attitude or offline behavior, with only mixed, uncertain evidence for reduced news knowledge. The authors frame reshares as a "double-edged sword" that amplifies both reliable and low-quality content but with surprisingly limited downstream attitudinal consequences.

## Key Contributions

- Rigorous, large-scale *experimental* (not correlational) evidence on the causal effect of a specific virality-enabling platform feature during a live presidential election.
- Shows that a widely proposed intervention — removing reshares — substantially alters content exposure and on-platform behavior but has limited detectable impact on attitudes and offline behavior.
- Quantifies the composition of reshared content, clarifying its role in amplifying political news and untrustworthy sources, and that most reshared content does not go viral.
- Provides precise null / near-null estimates powered to rule out even moderately sized effects, informing debates over social media's political influence.
- Models transparent industry-academic collaboration via preregistration, PATE/SATE reporting, and archived data and code.

## Methods

A preregistered RCT embedded in FIES, recruited via feed survey invitations seen by ~14.6 million users, with N=193,880 consenting. The analysis compares a control group against a "No Reshares" treatment (feeds excluding reshared content from friends, Groups, and Pages) active 24 September–23 December 2020. Analytic samples were N=23,402 (survey) and N=3,781 (web-tracking). Outcomes were drawn from five surveys, Facebook behavioral data, and web-visit tracking, with content classified for political relevance, news, trustworthiness, incivility, slurs, and ideological alignment. The primary estimand is a population average treatment effect (PATE) weighted on ideology, friend count, pages followed, and activity, with unweighted SATE also reported; estimation uses covariate-adjusted OLS with HC2 errors and sharpened FDR correction.

## Findings

- Reshared content fell from 28% to 5.8% of views; political news dropped from 6.2% to 2.5%, and untrustworthy-source content from 2.6% to 1.8%.
- Moderate/mixed-source content rose ~16%; both like-minded and cross-cutting content decreased modestly; uncivil content rose slightly; slur exposure unchanged.
- Treatment reduced time on Facebook, click rate (8.3%→7.5%), and reaction rate; friend-sourced content fell ~10 points while Groups (+8) and Pages (+2) rose. Users' own posting behavior was unchanged.
- No significant effects on affective or issue polarization (FDR-adjusted p>0.8), nor on media trust, institutional confidence, election legitimacy, factual perceptions, or support for political violence.
- News knowledge fell in the sample (SATE −0.069 SD) but the PATE (−0.053 SD) was not significant after correction (p=0.16); a 62% drop in mainstream news exposure is a candidate mechanism.
- The one robust behavioral effect: reduced clicks on partisan news (PATE −0.109 SD); off-platform news visits were unaffected.

## Connections
