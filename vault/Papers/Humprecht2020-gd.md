# Resilience to Online Disinformation: A Framework for Cross-National Comparative Research

> Humprecht, E., Esser, F., & Aelst, P. V. (2020). Resilience to Online Disinformation: A Framework for Cross-National Comparative Research. *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, 1940161219900126. https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161219900126
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/1940161219900126)

## Summary

This paper develops a theoretical and empirical framework for studying societal **resilience to online disinformation** across countries, arguing that structural conditions—not just individual psychology—determine why some societies resist disinformation better than others. Humprecht, Esser, and Van Aelst define resilience as a collective, structural characteristic that captures both lower exposure to disinformation and citizens being less inclined to believe or spread it. They identify seven macro-level dimensions spanning the political, media, and economic environment, operationalize them using existing comparative datasets, and apply them to eighteen Western democracies. A cluster analysis produces three distinct groups: a high-resilience cluster (Northern/Western Europe and Canada), a low-resilience polarized cluster (Southern Europe), and the United States as a uniquely vulnerable outlier. A central methodological caution follows: findings drawn largely from the U.S. and U.K. should not be assumed to generalize across Western democracies.

## Key Contributions

- A systematic macro-level theoretical framework and conceptual map for cross-national comparative study of online disinformation.
- Translation of abstract structural dimensions into concrete, measurable indicators from existing comparative datasets, enabling country ranking and typology building.
- Introduction and empirical application of country-level **resilience**, reframing disinformation as a matter of structural defense rather than only threat.
- An empirical three-cluster classification of eighteen Western democracies to guide case selection in future comparative research.
- Explicit warning about the exceptional, non-generalizable nature of the U.S. case.

## Methods

The authors build a literature-based framework of seven macro-level dimensions (plus an eighth "outcome" dimension, exposure to disinformation) and operationalize them via secondary data from the Digital News Report, V-Dem, the Timbro Authoritarian Populism Index, the Global Populism Database, a public service broadcasting strength index, and World Bank data. The eighteen-country sample follows Hallin and Mancini's media systems typology. Measures were merged into seven z-standardized indices (Cronbach's alpha ≈ .71–.96, with some inverted so higher values mean more resilience). Analysis combined principal components factor analysis with varimax rotation, a two-stage cluster analysis (Ward's hierarchical clustering, three-cluster solution), and OLS regression predicting reported exposure to disinformation.

## Findings

- Seven structural conditions weaken resilience: societal polarization, populist communication, low news trust, weak public service media, fragmented/less overlapping audiences, large advertising markets, and high social media use.
- Substantial cross-national variation: Finland, Denmark, and the Netherlands score high across resilience indices, while Spain, Italy, Greece, and the U.S. score low.
- Factor analysis extracted two factors explaining 69% of variance (factor 1 = 49.7%; factor 2 = 19.1%), with public service broadcasting loading on both.
- Three clusters emerged: a media-supportive/consensual high-resilience group (Western Europe plus Canada), a polarized low-resilience group (Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain), and the United States alone.
- The OLS model explained 83% of variance in reported exposure, with media trust, social media use, and market size significant; using a V-Dem expert-coded outcome measure, the model explained ~40% of variance with populism and social media use significant and polarization/shared media in the expected directions.

## Connections

This framework builds directly on Benkler et al.'s structural account of media resilience [[Benkler2018-lw]] and shares the concern that U.S. and U.K. dynamics dominate—and distort—the broader misinformation literature, a meta-scientific worry echoed by work reviewing the state of the field such as [[Budak2024-ef]]. Its emphasis on structural rather than purely individual-level explanations complements psychological and inoculation-oriented approaches like [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]] and [[Pennycook2021-jq]], and its treatment of exposure connects to empirical estimates of who actually encounters disinformation, e.g. [[Grinberg2019-ua]] and [[Guess2019-ym]].
