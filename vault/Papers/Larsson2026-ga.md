# From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter

> Larsson, A. O., & Skogerbø, E. (2026). From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter. *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*, 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282)

## Summary

This study investigates how the content features of political campaign posts drive low-threshold user engagement (likes) across Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter during Norway's 2021 parliamentary election. Through a manual content analysis of 2,197 posts from nine parties and their leaders, Larsson and Skogerbø examine three content types — calls to action, policy topics, and populist styles — and model their effects on likes platform by platform. The central argument is that engagement is strongly platform-specific: identical content provokes different responses depending on the platform's affordances and opportunity structures. The paper offers a non-US, multiparty European case to counterbalance the US-dominated literature, and its 2021 Twitter data now carries historical value given the platform's subsequent transformation into X.

## Key Contributions

- Rare **cross-platform** comparative analysis of how multiple content types *jointly* shape engagement, moving beyond single-content-type studies.
- Treats **likes as a functionally equivalent engagement metric** to enable systematic comparison across three platforms.
- Supplies empirical evidence from a **non-US, multiparty European (Norwegian)** context.
- Documents 2021 Twitter engagement patterns with lasting historical value given the platform's decline in Norwegian campaigning.
- Draws practical implications: vary populist style and calls to action by platform, and recognize that policy content rarely boosts engagement.

## Methods

Standardized manual content analysis of 2,197 posts (Facebook: 1,106; Instagram: 576; Twitter: 515) from nine Norwegian parliamentary parties and their leaders. Data were gathered via the CrowdTangle API (Facebook, Instagram) and the Twitter academic research API, with deleted posts and retweets removed. Posts were coded for three concepts: calls to action (informing, mobilizing, interacting), 37 topics reduced to 22 issue categories, and populist styles (people-centrism, anti-elitism, exclusion of out-groups) following Reinemann et al.'s framework. Indices for calls-to-action and populist dimensions were built by summing coded categories (range 0–1). Three separate negative binomial regressions — one per platform — modeled number of likes with controls for party vs. leader, populist (Progress Party) status, and 2021 vote share. Intercoder reliability, assessed via Brennan and Prediger's kappa on 140 posts, ranged from .781 to 1.

## Findings

- **Facebook:** the informing index negatively predicted likes, while exclusion of out-groups was positive and significant; the populist Progress Party, party leaders, and larger parties all gained more likes.
- **Instagram:** the interacting index negatively affected likes; the Progress Party had no advantage; more topics had negative than positive effects (rural, education, environmental, defense, immigration negative; economic inequality and foreign policy positive).
- **Twitter:** both informing and mobilizing indices positively predicted likes; no populist style was significant (the Progress Party was absent from Twitter), and party size was not significant.
- Only **exclusion of out-groups on Facebook (H2c)** among the populism hypotheses was supported; informing and mobilizing on Twitter (H1a, H1b) were supported.
- Across all platforms, very few policy topics engaged users, and significant topic effects were more often negative than positive.
- Wedge issues (immigration/integration, environmental policy) showed significant negative effects only on Instagram.
- Party leaders consistently drew more likes than the parties themselves; observed platform differences were real but less stark than prior literature implies.
