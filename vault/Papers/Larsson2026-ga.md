# From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter

> Larsson, A. O., & Skogerbø, E. (2026). From content to likes – how political parties’ campaign posts trigger user engagement across Facebook, Instagram and Twitter. *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*, 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2767282)

## Summary

This study investigates how the content of political campaign posts drives low-threshold user engagement (measured as likes) across Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter during Norway's 2021 parliamentary election. Through manual content analysis of 2,197 posts from nine parties and their leaders, Larsson and Skogerbø test how three content features — calls to action, policy topics, and populist styles — predict likes on each platform. Their central argument is that engagement is platform-specific: identical content types trigger systematically different responses depending on platform affordances. Calls to action performed best on Twitter, the populist exclusion of out-groups performed best on Facebook, and policy topics generally failed to engage users anywhere. By using a non-US, multiparty European case and comparing platforms systematically, the paper answers longstanding calls for cross-platform comparative research while cautioning that observed platform differences, though real, are less pronounced than prior literature implies.

## Key Contributions

- Rare cross-platform comparative analysis examining how multiple content types *jointly* affect engagement, rather than isolating a single feature.
- Uses likes as a functionally equivalent engagement metric to permit systematic comparison across Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter.
- Provides empirical evidence from the under-studied Norwegian multiparty context, countering the US-centric bias in the literature.
- Documents 2021 Twitter engagement patterns of historical value given the platform's subsequent transformation into X and decline in Norwegian campaigning.
- Offers practical guidance for campaigners (tailor populist style and calls to action by platform; policy content rarely helps) and research directions on issue ownership and topic clustering.

## Methods

Standardized manual content analysis of 2,197 posts (Facebook 1,106; Instagram 576; Twitter 515) from nine parliamentary parties and their leaders. Data were collected via the CrowdTangle API (Facebook, Instagram) and the Twitter academic research API, with deleted posts and retweets removed. Posts were coded for three concepts: calls to action (informing, mobilizing, interacting), 37 topics reduced to 22 issue categories, and populist styles (people-centrism, anti-elitism, exclusion of out-groups) following Reinemann et al.'s framework. Indices (0–1) were built for calls-to-action and populist dimensions. Three separate negative binomial regressions — one per platform — modeled likes with controls for party versus leader, Progress Party (populist) status, and 2021 vote share. Intercoder reliability (Brennan and Prediger's kappa) on 140 posts ranged from .781 to 1.

## Findings

- **Facebook:** the informing index negatively predicted likes, while exclusion of out-groups was significantly positive; the populist Progress Party, party leaders, and larger parties all gained more likes.
- **Instagram:** the interacting index negatively affected likes; the Progress Party had no advantage; more topics had negative than positive effects (rural, education, environmental, defense, and immigration policy negative; economic inequality and foreign policy positive).
- **Twitter:** both informing and mobilizing indices positively predicted likes; no populist style was significant (the Progress Party was absent from Twitter) and party size did not matter.
- Hypotheses on informing/mobilizing being positive on Twitter were supported; interacting was not. Among populism hypotheses, only exclusion of out-groups on Facebook was supported.
- Policy topics rarely engaged users across platforms, and significant topic effects were more often negative than positive.
- Wedge issues (immigration/integration, environmental policy) showed significant negative effects only on Instagram.
- Across all three platforms, party leaders' posts consistently received more likes than party accounts' posts.

## Connections

This paper sits within a line of cross-platform and engagement-metrics research on political communication; it is closely related to other work by the same author exploring platform-specific campaign dynamics and engagement, including [[Larsson2026-ro]], [[Larsson2026-nl]], and [[Kalsnes2025-zb]] in the Norwegian political communication context. Its reliance on CrowdTangle-sourced data and engagement metrics ties it to broader methodological discussions of platform affordances and metric comparability, such as [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and [[Bucher2017-qh]]. The focus on populist exclusion of out-groups as an engagement driver connects it to literature on partisan and affective dynamics in online political content, including [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Iyengar2019-jj]].
