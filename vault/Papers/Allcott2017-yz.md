# Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election

> Allcott, H., & Gentzkow, M. (2017). Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, *31*, 211–236. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.31.2.211
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.31.2.211)

## Summary

This foundational paper by Allcott and Gentzkow provides theoretical and empirical grounding for the debate over whether "fake news"—defined as intentionally and verifiably false news articles—circulating on social media influenced the 2016 US presidential election. The authors advance an economic framing in which fake news emerges as a market equilibrium: false signals are cheaper to produce than accurate ones, consumers cannot costlessly verify accuracy, and consumers derive psychological utility from partisan-confirming content. Drawing on web browsing data, a 1,200-person post-election survey, and a database of 156 fact-checked false stories, they document that fake news was widely shared and heavily tilted toward Trump—yet estimate that the average American adult saw and remembered only about one fake news story. The disciplined conclusion: for fake news to have been pivotal, it would have needed implausibly high per-article persuasiveness.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces a working definition of fake news and an economic framework distinguishing it from adjacent phenomena (rumors, conspiracy theories, satire, slant, unintentional error).
- Provides the first systematic empirical estimates of the scale of fake news circulation and voter exposure around the 2016 election.
- Develops a placebo-headline survey methodology to correct for false recall when measuring media exposure.
- Documents correlates of who believes fake news and quantifies ideologically aligned inference and its moderators, including social-network segregation.
- Offers a non-partisan, back-of-the-envelope framework for assessing—without claiming to settle—whether fake news was decisive.

## Methods

The paper combines a theoretical media-market model (adapted from Gentzkow, Shapiro, and Stone 2016), in which firms sell signals about a true state to consumers with heterogeneous priors and fake news producers make no investment in accuracy, with several empirical strands. These include: a database of 156 fact-checked false election stories (Snopes, PolitiFact, BuzzFeed) with Facebook share counts via BuzzSumo; Alexa web-traffic analysis comparing referral sources for 690 top news sites versus 65 fake news sites; and an online post-election survey of 1,208 US adults shown 15 randomly assigned headlines across five categories, including invented placebo headlines. Three benchmarking approaches—shares-to-visits ratios, browsing impressions, and placebo-corrected survey recall—triangulate estimates of fake news exposure.

## Findings

- The database held 115 pro-Trump fake stories shared ~30 million times on Facebook versus 41 pro-Clinton stories shared ~7.6 million times—roughly a 3-to-1 pro-Trump tilt.
- Social media drove only ~10% of traffic to top news sites but ~41.8% of traffic to fake news sites; only 14% of adults named social media their most important election news source.
- Upper-bound estimates: ~760 million reads (~3 per adult); 159 million impressions on 65 fake sites in the election month (0.64 per adult); placebo-corrected recall implied the average adult saw and remembered about 1.14 fake articles.
- Placebo (never-circulated) headlines were recalled and believed at nearly the same rate as real fake headlines, indicating substantial false recall.
- Republicans were more credulous of both true and false articles; education, age, and media consumption predicted more accurate discernment.
- Democrats and Republicans were 17.2 and 14.7 percentage points more likely to believe ideologically aligned headlines, an effect amplified by heavier media consumption and more segregated social networks.
- Benchmarking one fake article against the ~0.02-point effect of one TV ad implies vote-share shifts of only hundredths of a percentage point—far below Trump's margins in pivotal states.

## Connections

This paper is a canonical reference point for the empirical measurement of misinformation exposure and belief, and connects directly to the wave of exposure-estimation studies that followed—[[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], and [[Allen2020-nj]] all refine or contest its "modest exposure" conclusion, while [[Lazer2018-mm]] offers a companion science-of-fake-news agenda. Its partisan-motivated-reasoning findings dialogue with [[Kunda1990-cg]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]], and [[Osmundsen2021-et]], and its diffusion and echo-chamber framing links to [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]]; the authors' own follow-on trend analysis appears in [[Allcott2019-gn]]. Broader ecosystem accounts such as [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Tsfati2020-uo]] (on mainstream media amplification) situate its market model within the larger information-disorder debate.
