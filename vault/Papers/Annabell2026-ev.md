# Regulating a floating signifier: Interpretations of “data” in the digital services act researcher access provision

> Annabell, T. (2026). Regulating a floating signifier: Interpretations of “data” in the digital services act researcher access provision. *Media, Technology and Governance*. https://doi.org/10.1177/29777984261486601
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/29777984261486601)

## Summary

This article interrogates a term so common it is rarely examined: "data." Focusing on Article 40 of the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) and its subsequent Delegated Act, Taylor Annabell argues that neither instrument ever defines "data," leaving the concept open to contestation. Drawing on Laclau's notion of the "floating signifier," the paper shows how the meaning of data drifts across regulatory and commercial discourses. Very Large Online Platforms and Search Engines (VLOPSEs) exploit this openness, interpreting data narrowly as aggregated, anonymized, quantified, and predefined datasets — a datafication-centric, data-science framing that sits in tension with the multiplicity of data understandings across academic disciplines, especially qualitative traditions. The result is that platforms exert quiet but consequential power over how researchers can even imagine data for scholarly work, a governance dimension overlooked in current debates that treat data as a self-evident technical object.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes *the concept of data itself* — not data access, quality, or observability — as a neglected site of governance contestation within the DSA.
- Applies Laclau's "floating signifier" as an analytical lens for how "data" is discursively constructed and contested, contrasting it with the interpretative flexibility of boundary objects.
- Provides an empirical mapping of how 25 designated VLOPSEs operationalize data in their first published data catalogs and how they contested definitional boundaries in public feedback.
- Extends existing DSA scholarship by foregrounding the taken-for-granted meaning of data and its implications for which disciplines count as legitimate research.
- Calls for reflexivity about terminology in research communities and in dialogue with the European Commission.

## Methods

Discourse analysis grounded in Fairclough's critical discourse analysis and Laclau's discourse theory. The author analyzes three corpora: (1) regulatory texts — relevant articles and recitals on "data" and "information" in the DSA (Article 40, Recitals 96–98) and the draft and adopted Delegated Act (Recital 11, Article 6); (2) ten public feedback submissions (covering 15 of 25 designated VLOPSEs) on the draft Delegated Act during the October–December 2024 period, with word counts and issues mapped by article and recital; and (3) publicly available DSA data catalogs published by VLOPSEs from November 2025, examining how data types, categories, and structures are presented.

## Findings

- Recitals tied to Article 40 illustrate data chiefly through aggregated, quantified interactional metrics (views, reactions, shares), whereas the Delegated Act's Recital 11 offers a wider, non-exhaustive list including textual forms, documentation, A/B test results, and content moderation data.
- Booking.com and Amazon argued most explicitly for data as quantitative raw datasets, claiming the Delegated Act deviated from the DSA's intent; Amazon distinguished "data" from "documents" and "information."
- Meta pushed for data as *existing* structured datasets, arguing providers should not have to create new data — exposing a tension between data as ready-made and data as requiring assemblage — and raised concerns about accuracy and researcher competence.
- Platforms positioned themselves as authorities over what data exists, what does not, and what counts as quality, advocating limitations on granularity, availability, and anonymization.
- Data catalogs reveal varied operationalizations: Meta, Google, and Wikipedia rely on hyperlinks to existing datasets; AliExpress, Amazon, TikTok, and Temu offer tables organized by metric or systemic risk; Zalando offered the widest range (including A/B testing and policy documentation), while Shein was notably vague.
- Online marketplaces were more aligned in classifying data than social media platforms; computer and data science conventions dominate catalog articulations over qualitative social science and humanities framings.

## Connections

This paper is a critical-data-studies counterpoint to the growing body of work on DSA Article 40 and researcher data access, which it argues has treated data as self-evident; it connects to analyses of the platform-governance and data-access regime such as [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[Rieder2025-ju]], and [[Bruns2026-yv]]. Its argument about platforms asserting definitional authority over what counts as accessible data resonates with broader critiques of platform power and infrastructural control in [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[Gillespie2022-jx]], and with the platform-society framing of [[van-Dijck2018-up]].
