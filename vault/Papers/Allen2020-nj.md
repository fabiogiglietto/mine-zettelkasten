# Evaluating the fake news problem at the scale of the information ecosystem

> Allen, J., Howland, B., Mobius, M. M., Rothschild, D. M., & Watts, D. (2020). Evaluating the fake news problem at the scale of the information ecosystem. *Sci. Adv.*, *6*, eaay3539. https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3502581
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3502581)

## Summary

This paper measures the prevalence of "fake news" against the full backdrop of American media consumption, rather than within the narrow confines of social media. Drawing on a nationally representative multimode dataset spanning television, desktop, and mobile from 2016 to 2018, the authors place fake news in the *denominator* of total media diet. Their central finding is deflationary: news of any kind is a small share of Americans' media diet, television dominates online news by roughly five to one, and overt fake news accounts for only about 0.15% of the daily media diet — roughly one-tenth of one percent. They argue that the roots of public misinformedness and polarization are more likely to lie in ordinary news bias and widespread news avoidance than in engineered online falsehoods, and that academic attention has been badly miscalibrated relative to the empirical scale of the phenomenon.

## Key Contributions

- One of the first estimates of fake news prevalence at the scale of the *entire* information ecosystem, integrating TV, desktop, and mobile consumption.
- Combines three otherwise siloed data sources (Nielsen TV, Comscore online, Nielsen web with referral URLs) to address representativeness and comprehensiveness gaps in prior work.
- Reframes the misinformation debate by expressing fake news as a fraction of total media consumption, exposing its negligible relative share.
- Documents a mismatch between the volume of academic attention and the actual scale of the phenomenon, urging a pivot toward ordinary news bias and news avoidance.

## Methods

The authors assembled a multimode dataset categorizing content by *mode of consumption* rather than production. TV news was measured via Nielsen's nationally representative panel (N~100,000, plus a ~50,000 local-market subset) across ~400 classified "news" programs. Online media used Comscore's desktop and mobile panels, with online news defined as 800+ hard-news sites and fake news as 98 researcher/fact-checker-identified sites. Passive exposure via social media (Facebook, YouTube, Twitter, Reddit) and search engines was imputed using Nielsen's web panel referral URLs, including a sample of 360,000 YouTube "news and politics" videos. A ~15,000-person overlap subset linked desktop and TV consumption. News and fake news were defined broadly (including hyperpartisan and fraudulent categories) with robustness checks under stricter definitions.

## Findings

- Americans consume ~460 minutes of media per day, ~86% of which is not news-related.
- Online news, even including passive social/search exposure, is only 4.2% of total online consumption; TV news is ~23% of TV consumption.
- The TV-to-online news ratio exceeds 5:1 (54 vs 9.7 minutes), ranging from ~2:1 for 18–24 year olds to >7:1 for those 55+.
- 44% of the overlap panel consumed no online news at all; nearly three-quarters spend under 30 seconds/day reading online news.
- No age group spent more than ~1 minute/day on fake news or more than 1% of news consumption on it; older, conservative-leaning users consumed more.
- Only ~1.97% of desktop panelists consumed more fake than mainstream news, dropping to 0.7% (and lower under stricter definitions).
- Local news dominates TV news for most age groups; late-night comedy is under 5% overall.
- Google Scholar shows 2,210 "fake news"-titled publications since 2017 versus 73 before — vastly disproportionate to fake news's actual consumption share.

## Connections

This paper is a foundational corrective within the meta-science of misinformation research, and its "prevalence in the denominator" reframing dovetails with related efforts to measure the true footprint of problematic content across platforms [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and to reassess whether the field's assumptions match empirical reality [[Budak2024-ef]], [[Munger2025-cz]]. Its skeptical, scale-conscious stance connects to critiques of exaggerated online-influence claims [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]] and to the broader post-2016 fake-news literature it responds to, including work on exposure concentration among older conservatives and low overall reach [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], [[Guess2020-rr]], [[Allcott2017-yz]]; the same authors' engagement-based reframing of information consumption appears in [[Allen2021-ai]].
