# All talk, no rules: Content moderation practices and discursive strategies in platform governance of climate misinformation

> Kuznetsova, V. (2026). All talk, no rules: Content moderation practices and discursive strategies in platform governance of climate misinformation. *Platforms & Society*, *3*. https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624261499168
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624261499168)

## Summary

This paper investigates how four major platforms—Meta, YouTube, TikTok, and X/Twitter—formally govern climate misinformation and how they discursively justify their (limited) interventions. Through qualitative content analysis of 95 platform documents, Kuznetsova finds a persistent gap between what platforms say publicly and what they codify as enforceable rules: climate misinformation is rarely treated as a distinct, high-priority category, and platforms overwhelmingly favour "soft" interventions (labeling, demotion, demonetization) over removal. The central argument is that platforms substitute performative evidence of care for enforceable duties, cultivating a narrative of benevolence and neutrality through three recurring discursive strategies that redistribute governance responsibility onto experts, users, and content creators. Given these structural limits of self-governance, the paper calls for external oversight such as the EU Digital Services Act's systemic risk framework.

## Key Contributions

- Systematic cross-platform comparison of how climate misinformation is *formally regulated* versus *publicly communicated* across four major platforms.
- Extends platform governance scholarship beyond policy monitoring into the discursive-normative dimension, naming a repertoire of three discursive strategies.
- Documents and theorizes the misalignment between stated commitments and codified rules, contributing to the "governance-washing" literature.
- Frames climate misinformation as a distinctive governance puzzle—epistemically settled but politically contested—and contrasts it with COVID-19 and election misinformation.
- Makes a normative case for external regulatory oversight given the structural limits of platform self-governance.

## Methods

Qualitative content analysis of 95 public-facing documents (YouTube 25, Meta 26, TikTok 26, X/Twitter 18), collected in early 2025 using Goanta's (2023) platform documentation framework via Google Advanced Search plus direct navigation of community guidelines, ad policies, and enforcement docs. RQ1 used thematic coding classifying documentation as "explicit," "implicit," or "not mentioned," applying Schaffner et al.'s elements of comprehensive content moderation. RQ2 used inductive coding (Braun and Clarke; Scharp and Sanders) with constant comparison to aggregate recurring rhetorical patterns into higher-order discursive strategies.

## Findings

- All four platforms address climate misinformation in public communications, but formal policy integration is uneven or absent.
- **TikTok** is the only platform with clear rules in both content and advertising guidelines, treating climate misinformation as a distinct category and using removal as a sanction.
- **Meta** offers the most extensive public communication but has no formal rules in Facebook/Instagram community guidelines or ad policies.
- **YouTube** addresses it only in advertiser-facing demonetization docs; **X/Twitter** only in the Curation Style Guide for a single feature.
- Only TikTok removes user-posted climate misinformation; others rely on demotion, labels, informational hubs, and monetization restrictions. All claim to block climate-misleading ads.
- Moderation criteria anchor definitions in "scientific consensus" but often remain abstract; scientific authority is selectively operationalized—invoked to *define* misinformation but withheld as a basis for *removal*.
- The three strategies: **"increasing the good, decreasing the bad"** (framing counter-speech and engagement metrics as remedies and "quantified legitimacy"); **"empowering others"** (externalizing epistemic authority to experts, NGOs, users); and **"authorizing content creators"** (making creators moral agents while platforms claim moral credit).
- Neither scientific consensus nor political contestation adequately explains the absence of climate-specific rules, since stricter regimes were built for comparably contested COVID-19 and election misinformation.

## Connections

This paper sits at the intersection of platform governance infrastructure and information-disorder research; its focus on discursive legitimation and the gap between stated and enforced moderation connects to theoretical treatments of platform curation and gatekeeping such as [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]. Its "governance-washing" argument and attention to how platforms frame their own interventions resonate with critical platform governance work like [[Farkas2026-lr]] and [[Crosset2026-mq]]. Its comparative framing of a politically contested but scientifically settled misinformation domain complements broader misinformation scholarship including [[Lewandowsky2026-ob]] and [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]].
