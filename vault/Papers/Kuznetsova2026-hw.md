# All talk, no rules: Content moderation practices and discursive strategies in platform governance of climate misinformation

> Kuznetsova, V. (2026). All talk, no rules: Content moderation practices and discursive strategies in platform governance of climate misinformation. *Platforms & Society*, *3*. https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624261499168
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/29768624261499168)

## Summary

This paper examines how four major platforms—Meta, YouTube, TikTok, and X/Twitter—govern climate misinformation and, crucially, how they rhetorically justify their (limited) interventions. Through qualitative content analysis of 95 platform documents, Kuznetsova finds that climate misinformation is rarely treated as a distinct, high-priority category in enforceable rules, even as platforms make conspicuous public commitments about it. Platforms favor "soft" interventions—labeling, demotion, demonetization—over removal, and deploy three recurring discursive strategies that construct a narrative of benevolence and neutrality. The central argument is that platforms substitute performative evidence of care for enforceable duties, redistributing governance responsibility to experts, users, and content creators. Because neither scientific consensus nor political contestation adequately explains the absence of climate-specific moderation (platforms built stricter rules for COVID-19 and elections), the paper concludes that self-governance is structurally limited and calls for external oversight.

## Key Contributions

- Systematic cross-platform comparison of how climate misinformation is *formally regulated* versus *publicly communicated*.
- Extends platform governance scholarship from policy monitoring to the discursive-normative dimension, identifying a repertoire of three legitimation strategies.
- Documents and theorizes the misalignment between stated commitments and codified rules, adding evidence to the "governance-washing" literature.
- Frames climate misinformation as a distinctive governance puzzle—settled epistemic ground yet politically contested—contrasted against COVID-19 and election domains.
- Argues for external regulatory oversight (e.g., the EU Digital Services Act systemic risk framework).

## Methods

Qualitative content analysis of 95 public-facing documents (YouTube 25, Meta 26, TikTok 26, X/Twitter 18), gathered in early 2025 using Goanta's platform documentation framework via Google Advanced Search plus direct navigation of community guidelines, advertising policies, and enforcement documentation. For RQ1, thematic coding classified documentation as "explicit," "implicit," or "not mentioned," applying Schaffner et al.'s elements of comprehensive content moderation. For RQ2, inductive thematic coding (Braun and Clarke; Scharp and Sanders) identified recurring rhetorical patterns, grouped via constant comparison into higher-order discursive strategies.

## Findings

- All four platforms address climate misinformation in public communications, but formal policy integration is uneven or absent.
- **TikTok** is the only platform with clear rules in both content and advertising guidelines and the only one that *removes* user-posted climate misinformation.
- **Meta** offers the most extensive public communication but has no formal rules in community guidelines or ad policies.
- **YouTube** addresses it only in advertiser-facing demonetization documentation; **X/Twitter** only in the Curation Style Guide for X Moments.
- Moderation definitions anchor in "scientific consensus" but remain abstract and require external knowledge; only TikTok and Meta provide concrete examples.
- Three discursive strategies: (1) *increasing the good, decreasing the bad*—justifying soft moderation via counter-speech, free expression, and engagement metrics as "quantified legitimacy"; (2) *empowering others*—externalizing epistemic authority to experts, NGOs, and users; (3) *authorizing content creators*—framing creators as moral agents while platforms claim the moral credit.
- A cross-cutting feature: scientific authority is selectively operationalized—invoked to define misinformation but withheld as a basis for removal.
