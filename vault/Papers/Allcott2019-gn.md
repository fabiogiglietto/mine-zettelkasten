# Trends in the diffusion of misinformation on social media

> Allcott, H., Gentzkow, M., & Yu, C. (2019). Trends in the diffusion of misinformation on social media. *Research \& Politics*, *6*, 2053168019848554. https://doi.org/10.3386/w25500
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.3386/w25500)

## Summary

This paper offers one of the earliest longitudinal, cross-platform measurements of the scale of social media misinformation before and after the 2016 US election. Using BuzzSumo data on 569 fake news websites and 9,540 individually verified false-story URLs, Allcott, Gentzkow, and Yu track Facebook engagements and Twitter shares from January 2015 through July 2018. Their central finding is a divergence: interactions with false content rose on both platforms through late 2016, but afterward fell sharply on Facebook while continuing to climb on Twitter — with the Facebook-to-Twitter engagement ratio for fake news declining by roughly 60%. Because comparison categories of legitimate content show no such divergence, the authors read the pattern as suggestive (though not causal) evidence that Facebook-specific interventions dampened the relative diffusion of misinformation.

## Key Contributions

- One of the first longitudinal, cross-platform empirical measures of how the scale of misinformation evolved around the 2016 election.
- A comparative Facebook-vs-Twitter ratio design that nets out sample-selection and demand-side confounds affecting both platforms similarly.
- A large combined, documented dataset of fake news sites and verified false-story URLs, with published replication files.
- Suggestive evidence bearing on the evaluation of platform content-moderation interventions.

## Methods

The authors assembled a list of 672 fake news sites (569 with usable data) by merging five prior lists and studies, and constructed three comparison groups from Alexa rankings (major news, small news, and business/culture sites). Monthly Facebook engagements (shares + comments + reactions) and Twitter shares were gathered via the BuzzSumo commercial content database and averaged by quarter. A complementary URL-level sample was built by scraping Snopes "false"/"mostly false" claims, extracting keywords, matching in BuzzSumo, and manually screening down to 9,540 verified false-story URLs. Robustness was tested extensively — restricting to sites flagged by multiple lists, excluding single-list sites, filtering by active-operation dates, dropping outliers, using likelihood tiers, an alternative political comparison group, and Facebook shares in place of total engagements.

## Findings

- Fake news interactions rose on both platforms from early 2015 through just after the 2016 election.
- After the election, Facebook engagements with fake news fell by more than 50%, while Twitter shares kept rising.
- The Facebook-to-Twitter ratio for fake news held near 45:1 through late 2016, then fell to about 15:1 by mid-2018 (~60% decline).
- Comparison categories stayed relatively stable and showed no comparable divergence across platforms.
- Facebook engagements with fake news peaked at ~160 million/month in late 2016 and fell to ~60 million/month; major news sites typically garner 200–250 million.
- On Twitter, fake news shares stayed at 3–5 million/month after 2016 versus ~20 million for major news.
- The URL-level analysis of 9,540 false stories reproduced the post-2016 halving of the Facebook-to-Twitter ratio.

## Connections

This paper is a foundational quantitative reference point for the information-disorder literature, building directly on [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Lazer2018-mm]], and its diffusion focus complements the classic contagion analysis of [[Vosoughi2018-at]] and [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]]. Its concern with platform-level interventions and moderation efficacy connects to platform-governance work such as [[Gillespie2022-jx]], and its exposure-measurement approach speaks to studies of misinformation reach like [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Allen2020-nj]]. More recent replications and extensions of platform-scale misinformation trends — including [[Pierri2025-hm]] and [[Murtfeldt2025-wu]] — sit downstream of the empirical template established here.
