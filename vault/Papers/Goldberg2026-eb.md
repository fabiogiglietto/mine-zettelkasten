# AI and the future of digital public squares

## Summary

This position paper, emerging from a 2024 convening of over 70 civil society experts and technologists, argues that large language models represent a second major technological inflection point—after the internet itself—for how public deliberation happens at scale. It frames today's digital public squares as falling short of democratic ideals along three dimensions: access and inclusion, privacy and surveillance, and distorted information ecosystems driven by engagement optimization, echo chambers, and manipulative propaganda. Drawing on democratic theory (Habermas's public sphere, Rawls's public reason, Landemore's Open Democracy) alongside computational social science and platform governance, the authors chart both the opportunities LLMs afford for more decentralized, participatory, and deliberative online spaces and the distinct risks of deepening societal schisms. The paper's central organizing move is to lay out four families of AI-enabled applications, each with an agenda of opportunities, risks, and research directions.

## Key Contributions

- A consolidated, multidisciplinary research and investment agenda for using LLMs to strengthen digital public squares while guarding against misuse.
- A framework of four concrete AI application families: **collective dialogue systems**, **bridging systems**, **community-driven moderation**, and **proof-of-humanity systems**.
- An explicit bridge between democratic theory (Habermas, Rawls, Landemore, DuBois) and applied technical and platform-governance work.
- Design principles for a "meta-platform" enabling interoperability and composability among deliberative technologies.

## Methods

- Synthesis of insights from an April 2024 convening of the authors plus roughly 50 additional technologists and thinkers in New York City.
- Literature review integrating democratic theory, internet studies, computational social science, and platform governance.
- Structured analysis of each of the four application areas through opportunities, risks/mitigations, and future research.
- Documentation of existing systems and case studies including Polis, Remesh, X's Community Notes, Meta's diverse engagement ranking, Jigsaw's Perspective API, Policy Synth, and New Jersey's AI Task Force deliberations.

## Findings

- Collective dialogue systems (bridging surveys and focus groups) face adoption barriers—labor-intensive sensemaking, onboarding, hosting costs, institutional will, moderation burden—many of which LLMs could reduce via translation, prompt generation, vote prediction, and summarization.
- Meta's "diverse engagement" comment ranking reduced polarizing and bullying content while increasing comment views by 0.69%; X's Community Notes uses matrix factorization to surface notes rated helpful across the primary axis of division.
- Bridging can be signaled through user diversity (embeddings, behavior, networks) or content features (curiosity, compassion, personal stories, reasoned argument), with a combination likely optimal.
- Bridging carries risks: elevating generic content, gaming of ranking incentives, and the fact that greater content heterogeneity does not always reduce polarization—uncivil opposing content can worsen attitude polarization.
- ChatGPT 3.5 performed worse than a coin toss at moderating context-heavy subreddits, cautioning against wholesale AI replacement of human moderators; AI should augment rather than substitute human deliberation.
- Synthetic participation, while promising, is democratically problematic and demands transparency.
