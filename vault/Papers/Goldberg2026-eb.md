# AI and the future of digital public squares

> Goldberg, B., Acosta-Navas, D., Bakker, M., Beacock, I., Botvinick, M., Buch, P., DiResta, R., Donthi, N., Fast, N., Iyer, R., Jalan, Z., Konya, A., Danciu, G. >

## Summary

This position paper, emerging from a 2024 New York convening of over 70 civil society experts and technologists, examines how large language models could reshape digital public squares. It argues that LLMs represent a second major inflection point (after the internet itself) for deliberation at scale, offering opportunities to move online spaces toward more decentralized, participatory, and deliberative forms — while also risking deepening societal division and undermining democratic legitimacy. Grounded in democratic theory of the public sphere (Habermas, Rawls, Landemore's Open Democracy), the paper structures its analysis around four families of AI-enabled applications and lays out research and investment agendas for each.

## Key Contributions

- Consolidates a multidisciplinary agenda for using LLMs to strengthen digital public squares while guarding against misuse.
- Frames four concrete AI application families — collective dialogue systems, bridging systems, community-driven moderation, and proof-of-humanity systems — each analyzed through opportunities, risks/mitigations, and open research questions.
- Bridges democratic theory (Habermas's public sphere, Rawls's public reason, Landemore's Open Democracy, DuBois) with applied technical and platform-governance work.
- Proposes design principles for a "meta-platform" enabling interoperability and composability among deliberative technologies.

## Methods

The paper synthesizes insights from an April 2024 convening of authors and ~50 additional thinkers and technologists, integrated with a literature review across democratic theory, internet studies, computational social science, and platform governance. Each of the four application areas is examined structurally, drawing on documented systems and case studies including Polis, Remesh, X's Community Notes, Meta's diverse engagement ranking, Jigsaw's Perspective API, Policy Synth, and the New Jersey AI Task Force deliberations.

## Findings

- Collective dialogue systems (bridging surveys and focus groups) face adoption obstacles — labor-intensive sensemaking, onboarding and hosting costs, institutional will, moderation burden — many of which LLMs could reduce via translation, prompt generation, vote prediction, and summarization.
- Meta's "diverse engagement" comment ranking reduced polarizing and bullying content while increasing comment views by 0.69%; X's Community Notes uses matrix factorization to surface notes rated helpful across the primary axis of division.
- Bridging can be signaled through user diversity (embeddings, behavior, networks) or content analysis (curiosity, compassion, personal stories, reasoned argument); a combination is likely optimal.
- Bridging risks include elevating generic insubstantial content and gaming of ranking incentives; heterogeneity does not always reduce polarization, since uncivil opposing content can increase attitude polarization.
- Community moderators face declining platform support, burnout, and inadequate tooling; ChatGPT 3.5 performed worse than a coin toss at moderating context-heavy subreddits, cautioning against wholesale AI replacement.
- AI can support moderation proactively (listening, LLM simulations, guideline co-creation, author nudges) and reactively (customizable filters, triaging, appeals, governance procedures).
- AI should augment rather than substitute human deliberation; synthetic participation is promising but democratically problematic and requires transparency.
