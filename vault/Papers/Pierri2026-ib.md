# Research opportunities and challenges of the EU’s Digital Services Act

> Pierri, F., Araujo, T., Kruikemeier, S., Lorenz-Spreen, P., Abeele, M. M. V., Vandenbosch, L., Gonçalves-Sa, J., & Grabowicz, P. (2026). Research opportunities and challenges of the EU’s Digital Services Act. *Communications of the ACM*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3779412
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3779412)

## Summary

This perspective piece examines the research opportunities and practical obstacles created by Article 40 of the European Union's Digital Services Act (DSA), which grants vetted researchers access to public and non-public data from very large online platforms (VLOPs) and search engines (VLOSEs). Drawing on the authors' direct involvement in EU policy discussions—including an ERC-supported pilot program and a roundtable with platforms and the European Commission—the paper argues that while Article 40 is a landmark framework for studying systemic online risks, significant legal, technical, and organizational barriers still hinder effective independent research even after the 2025 Delegated Act. It advances a normative case for independent, publicly funded research as essential infrastructure for digital accountability, contrasting Europe's transparency-oriented approach with a US trend toward limiting independent scrutiny.

## Key Contributions

- An insider, practitioner-informed assessment of the DSA Article 40 data-access process from researchers engaged in its policy formation.
- A taxonomy of challenges: misaligned incentives, power/resource asymmetries, and implementation bottlenecks.
- Three concrete recommendations: (1) streamline access procedures via dataset categorization, data-sharing standards, and coordinated DSC/DPO training; (2) support and foster independent research by addressing the circular resource problem and coordinating researcher efforts; and (3) close regulatory blind spots by extending the DSA to large language models.
- Frames successful DSA implementation as a potential global model for transparent digital governance.

## Methods

A collective expert perspective/position piece rather than an empirical study. It is grounded in direct participation in EU policy discussions on the Delegated Act, including an 18-month ERC-supported pilot program bringing together researchers, DG-CONNECT, and national Digital Services Coordinators (DSCs). It is further informed by a roundtable with major platforms and the European Commission, a public workshop held in October 2025, and a synthesis of prior literature on industry influence in research, algorithmic bias, privacy, and digital media effects.

## Findings

- Platforms have expressed reluctance to share data directly, favoring "independence by permission" models that tend to produce outcomes aligned with industry interests.
- A cited example: an independent analysis of a Meta–researcher collaboration found the research portrayed Meta's platforms as more effective at curbing misinformation than normal, because temporary 2020-election algorithmic changes were not disclosed.
- Four asymmetries disadvantage academic researchers: financial/infrastructural (AI industry dominance), information access, legal resources, and public reach for narratives.
- Implementation faces a "circular problem of resources": data access is needed to secure grants, but funding is needed to endure the complex data-access application process.
- Mismatches in language and expectations between researchers, DSCs, DPOs, and platforms—driven by empirical versus risk-averse orientations—are likely to slow the process and impose unnecessary restrictions.
