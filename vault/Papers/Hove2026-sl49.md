# Seeing Is Believing? Most People Struggle to Detect AI-Generated Images

> Hove, M. F., Hjorth, F., Golovchenko, Y., Adler-Nissen, R., Bechmann, A., van Dalen, A., Hobolt, S. B., & de Vreese, C. (2026). Seeing Is Believing? Most People Struggle to Detect AI-Generated Images. *Social Media + Society*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261483376
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261483376)

## Summary

This paper asks whether ordinary people can tell photorealistic AI-generated images apart from authentic photographs in a social-media-like viewing context. Through a preregistered survey experiment with 2,091 representative Danish respondents, the authors demonstrate that human discernment is strikingly poor: although people perform marginally above random guessing, they would have scored *better* by simply assigning every image a flat 50% probability of being AI-generated. Because Denmark has high digital literacy, the authors treat it as a "most likely" case—making the weak performance especially alarming. They conclude that individual-level detection is an inadequate safeguard against AI-generated misinformation and that structural interventions are needed instead.

## Key Contributions

- Expands discernment research beyond faces and trivial everyday images to four high-stakes contexts: regular, people, crisis, and political scenes.
- Introduces a design using only *true* real-world events with paired authentic and AI versions, disentangling discernment ability from memory effects that confounded prior studies.
- Enhances ecological validity by using freely available AI tools (OpenArt.ai) and stimulus sampling across 52 images, approximating content from low-resource actors rather than state-level disinformation operations.
- Provides evidence that individual-level interventions (e.g., teaching visual-cue detection) may be ineffective or counterproductive, favoring structural safeguards like watermarking and transparency.
- Documents overconfidence and the decoupling of confidence from accuracy in AI image discernment.

## Methods

A preregistered online survey experiment (fielded by Epinion, June 2024) with 2,091 adult Danes representative on age, gender, and region. Each respondent viewed 7 of 52 images drawn from a pool of 26 real-world events, each with one authentic (Wikimedia Commons) and one AI-generated (OpenArt.ai) version across four categories. No source, labels, or engagement metrics were shown, and respondents gave fast judgments (median 7 seconds). Participants rated the likelihood each image was AI-generated (0–100). Discernment was operationalized via Brier scores—benchmarked against simulated random guessing and a constant 50% probability—while confidence was measured as deviation from the midpoint. Analytical thinking was captured with the three-item Cognitive Reflection Test, and hypotheses were tested with t-tests and cross-classified multilevel models with random intercepts for participants and images.

## Findings

- Mean Brier score was 0.05 lower than random guessing (significant) but 0.034 *higher* than a constant 50% benchmark—people would have done better by always guessing 50%.
- No image category (regular, people, crisis, political) differed significantly in discernment; even regular images were not significantly better than random.
- Analytical thinking predicted better discernment (≈0.04 lower Brier), but high analytical thinkers still averaged 0.26, failing to beat the 0.25 benchmark.
- Women and generative-AI-experienced users discerned slightly better; older individuals discerned worse; only 18–24-year-olds scored significantly below the 0.25 benchmark.
- Gender effects vanished when using raw probability estimates, suggesting differences reflect calibration/confidence rather than true discrimination.
- Confidence did not vary significantly across crisis/political versus regular images and was largely decoupled from actual accuracy, indicating overconfidence.
