# Exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency foreign influence campaign on Twitter in the 2016 US election and its relationship to attitudes and voting behavior

> Eady, G., Paskhalis, T., Zilinsky, J., Bonneau, R., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J. A. (2023). Exposure to the Russian Internet Research Agency foreign influence campaign on Twitter in the 2016 US election and its relationship to attitudes and voting behavior. *Nature Communications*, *14*, 62. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-022-35576-9)

## Summary

This paper asks whether ordinary Americans exposed to the Russian Internet Research Agency (IRA) foreign influence campaign on Twitter changed their political attitudes, polarization, or votes during the 2016 US presidential election. By linking a three-wave longitudinal YouGov survey of 1,496 US Twitter users to their actual Twitter timelines, the authors measure real exposure to Russian disinformation as it occurred, rather than inferring it from account-level content. They find that exposure was extremely concentrated among a small group of strongly Republican users, was dwarfed by domestic news and candidate content, and showed no meaningful relationship to any measured attitudinal or behavioral outcome. The result situates foreign influence campaigns within the political-science tradition of minimal persuasion effects and offers a pointed corrective to alarmist narratives about Russian manipulation of voters.

## Key Contributions

- First study to link a longitudinal campaign-period survey to respondents' actual Twitter timelines to measure exposure to foreign influence content as it happened.
- Quantifies both the scale and the concentration of IRA exposure, benchmarking it directly against domestic news media and politician content.
- Directly estimates the relationship between exposure and within-person changes in issue positions, polarization, and vote choice.
- Applies equivalence testing (TOST) to bound and substantiate null findings, going beyond mere non-significance.
- Provides a back-of-the-envelope aggregate estimate (~32 million Americans potentially exposed) and a corrective to popular assumptions about foreign influence efficacy.

## Methods

The authors ran a three-wave panel survey (April–October 2016 plus post-election vote validation) approximately representative of the US voting-age public, linked respondents to their Twitter accounts, and collected ~1.2 billion tweets from accounts they followed in the eight months before election day to reconstruct potential timeline exposures. Using Twitter's released data on state-sponsored foreign influence accounts, they identified IRA and other foreign posts in these timelines. Analysis proceeded through empirical cumulative distributions to measure concentration, OLS models (including a squared party-ID term) predicting log exposure, and within-subject fixed-effects models relating changes in issue positions, perceived candidate polarization, and vote choice to exposure. Equivalence testing bounded the null effects, and simulations estimated counterfactual vote change under observed versus zero exposure.

## Findings

- 70% of respondents saw at least one foreign influence post; Russian accounts made up 86% of foreign-influence exposures.
- Exposure was highly concentrated: 1% of users accounted for 70% of Russian exposures, and 1% of Russian accounts produced 89% of content seen — far more concentrated than domestic news (1% = 24%) or politicians (1% = 37%).
- Exposure was largely incidental, flowing through retweets from ordinary followed accounts (75–80% of pathways by election day) rather than direct following.
- In the final month, respondents saw ~4 Russian posts/day versus ~106 from national news media (25×) and ~35 from politicians (9×); median weekly Russian exposure was zero.
- Strong Republicans saw roughly nine times as many Russian posts as Democrats or Independents; the relationship was monotonic, not U-shaped.
- No meaningful relationship between exposure and changes in eight issue positions, ideology, or perceived polarization; magnitudes near zero (~0.05–0.06 SD), bounded below 0.2 SD.
- Voting-outcome relationships were near zero, non-significant, and signed against benefiting Trump; simulated median change in Trump vote was about −0.18 to −0.4 points.
- Aggregate estimate suggests ~32 million Americans potentially exposed to IRA content over eight months.

## Connections

This paper contributes empirical evidence to the disinformation-effects debate that runs through much of this corpus, complementing work that similarly finds concentrated exposure and limited persuasive impact of low-quality or manipulative content such as [[Guess2023-ai]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Allen2020-nj]], and [[Allcott2017-yz]]. Its methodological identification of coordinated state-sponsored accounts and their reach connects to [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Nizzoli2020-cf]], while its framing around minimal persuasion effects and affective polarization links to [[Iyengar2019-jj]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]].
