# Mainstreaming and transnationalization of anti-gender ideas through social media: the case of CitizenGO

> Righetti, N., Kulichkina, A., Paroni, B. A., Cseri, Z. F., Aguirre, S. I., & Maikovska, K. (2025). Mainstreaming and transnationalization of anti-gender ideas through social media: the case of CitizenGO. *Information, Communication & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2025.2470229
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2025.2470229)

## Summary

This paper offers the first large-scale, decade-long (2013–2022) empirical analysis of CitizenGO, a transnational anti-gender advocacy organization headquartered in Spain, and its use of Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, and Telegram. Combining open-source intelligence, structural topic modeling, and coordinated link-sharing analysis, the authors distinguish a centrally controlled *core network* of multilingual CitizenGO accounts from a broader *amplification network* of right-wing, religious, and pro-life accounts that redistribute its content. They argue that anti-gender narratives operate as transnational "symbolic glue" and that CitizenGO's mainstreaming strategy mimics progressive digital advocacy organizations (notably Change.org) while exhibiting signs of astroturfing rather than purely organic connective action.

## Key Contributions

- First systematic empirical mapping of CitizenGO's cross-platform, multilingual communication over a decade.
- Introduces the *core network* / *amplification network* distinction as an analytical framework for studying mainstreaming by digital advocacy organizations.
- Demonstrates empirically that anti-gender content functions as transnational symbolic glue, more evenly diffused across national contexts than pro-life content.
- Bridges anti-gender movement studies, digital advocacy scholarship, and coordinated inauthentic behavior research.
- Methodological contribution: combines structural topic modeling, co-URL network analysis (CooRTweet), and narrow-window coordination detection on multilingual data.

## Methods

- OSINT (LinkedIn, job postings, Facebook transparency data, Wayback Machine) to reconstruct CitizenGO's organizational structure.
- Collection of accounts and posts across Twitter, CrowdTangle-indexed Facebook and Instagram, and Telegram for CitizenGO, plus Avaaz and Change.org as comparison cases.
- Scraping and analysis of 3,413 petition URLs to identify authorship and signature volume.
- Structural topic modeling (100 topics) on 24,401 Facebook posts translated via Google Translate API.
- Gini coefficients to measure cross-national distribution of topics.
- Co-URL sharing network analysis with Louvain community detection and native-speaker qualitative coding; coordination detection using a strict 2.5-hour co-sharing window.

## Findings

- Core network of 33 active accounts spanning four platforms; Facebook dominates in followers and engagement.
- Every national Facebook page has an administrator based at CitizenGO's Spanish headquarters, indicating centralized control rather than grassroots federation.
- CitizenGO itself authored 54.4% of the petitions it circulated, and self-authored petitions gathered significantly more signatures.
- Pro-life topics dominate content (≈40% of topics) but anti-gender topics (≈19%) are more evenly distributed transnationally (Gini 0.77 vs. 0.88).
- Amplification network of 19,176 accounts, with a core of 212 highly active accounts (25M+ cumulative followers) split among right-wing (36.2%), less-partisan political (15.3%), pro-life/anti-gender (28.6%), and religious (18.4%) actors.
- Language-based clusters differ in structure: a star-shaped, tightly centralized Spanish-English cluster around HazteOír/CitizenGO; a decentralized Portuguese Bolsonarist cluster; a Hungarian Fidesz-aligned anti-EU/anti-LGBTQ+ cluster.
- A tightly synchronized subnetwork of 148 accounts across 11 languages shares CitizenGO URLs within 2.5 hours — consistent with coordinated amplification.
- By June 2022, CitizenGO's website was visually nearly indistinguishable from Change.org's, reflecting deliberate mimicry of progressive digital advocacy aesthetics.
