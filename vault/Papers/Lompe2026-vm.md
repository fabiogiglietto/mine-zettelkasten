# The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs

> Lompe, M. (2026). The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, *35*, 20. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1)

## Summary

This paper argues for the renewed methodological value of the humble URL link — a web research object largely abandoned after the rise of platformization — as a tool for cross-platform controversy mapping. Through a nearly decade-long Polish controversy over the construction of a shipping canal across the Vistula Spit, the author analyzes 21,756 Facebook posts and 133,814 tweets to show that links remain reliable indicators of how participants engage with a contested issue, but in platform-specific ways. Grounded in controversy mapping as an empirical version of Actor-Network Theory, the study treats links not as neutral carriers but as socio-technical mediators whose meaning is structured by platform affordances, thereby bridging early digital methods with contemporary platformization and CSCW research.

## Key Contributions

- Demonstrates empirically that URLs can serve as cross-platform controversy indicators revealing platform-specific modes of engagement.
- Shows that platform affordances shape not just the circulation but the *meaning* and *function* of links within controversies.
- Argues for and revives link analysis as a method for cross-platform controversy mapping, bridging early digital methods, platformization studies, and CSCW.
- Reframes links as active socio-technical mediators (non-human actors in ANT terms) rather than residual or neutral artifacts, offering link-based trace analysis as a complement to discursive/semantic approaches.

## Methods

A mixed-methods approach centered on a single digital object — URLs in posts about the Vistula Spit Canal (1 Jan 2016 – 3 Feb 2023). Twitter data was gathered via the academic API through 4CAT; Facebook data via CrowdTangle, using identical keywords. A nested query design distinguished a "core collection" (bare, non-evaluative object-phrases) from an "extended collection" adding partisan terms from both supporters' and opponents' discourse. Beyond frequency analysis of platform-native metrics (likes, shares, mentions, retweets, links), the author built co-URL networks in Gephi (Force-Atlas 2 layout, Louvain modularity clustering) and manually coded link clusters into seven categories, labeling political leanings using Media Bias/Fact Check and Wikipedia.

## Findings

- On Twitter, external linking was among the most frequent activities (48.7% core, 61.8% extended); the extended, retweet-dominated layer was markedly self-referential (~52% links pointing back to Twitter vs. 31% in the core).
- Twitter link networks in the extended collection were dense and polarized, clustering around conservative, far-right, and suspicious (including Russian-language) sites against a news cluster — links signaled political alignment rather than evidence.
- On Facebook, over 90% of posts contained links regardless of collection, a near-universal default despite the platform's 2018 deprioritization of external-publisher content; link posts drew more comments than link-free posts.
- Facebook link clusters were more dispersed and outward-facing, connecting to local news, pro-environmental groups, and grassroots protest accounts — functioning evidentially and as mobilizing resources.
- The same resource type (mainstream news portals) was mobilized by different political camps across platforms (e.g., state broadcaster TVP circulated in Twitter's conservative/nationalist segment, while Facebook news portals were shared by center/left accounts).
- Post volume on both platforms tracked key controversy milestones (2019 tender, 2020 cost and environmental decisions, September 2022 opening).

## Connections

This work is a direct descendant of the URL-sharing and co-link analysis tradition, resonating closely with coordinated link-sharing and URL-based inauthentic behavior detection in [[Giglietto2022-b30e8b4e]], [[Giglietto2019-e9be81c1]], and [[Giglietto2017-4375de2f]]. Its treatment of platform affordances as structuring the meaning of circulated content engages platformization scholarship such as [[Helmond2026-ll]], while its link-based mapping of political alignment and polarization speaks to hyperlink and cross-platform network studies including Bastos2025-ya and Bastos2025-ol.
