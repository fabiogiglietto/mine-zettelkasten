# The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs

> Lompe, M. (2026). The link has died; Long live the link! Cross-platform controversy mapping with the use of URLs. *Computer Supported Cooperative Work (CSCW)*, *35*, 20. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10606-026-09557-1)

## Summary

This paper argues that URL links, largely written off as a research object after the "platformization" turn in web research, remain valuable instruments for cross-platform controversy mapping. Through a nearly decade-long case study of the contested construction of a shipping canal through Poland's Vistula Spit, Lompe analyzes 21,756 Facebook posts and 133,814 tweets to show that links reliably trace how participants engage with a controversy — but in platform-specific ways shaped by platform affordances. Grounded in controversy mapping as an empirical form of Actor-Network Theory, the paper reframes links not as neutral carriers of information but as active socio-technical mediators whose meaning is co-constituted by platform design, and calls for a methodological return to link analysis as a complement to discursive approaches.

## Key Contributions

- Demonstrates empirically that URLs can function as cross-platform controversy indicators revealing platform-specific modes of engagement.
- Shows that platform affordances shape not just the *circulation* of links but their *meaning and function* within a controversy.
- Revives link analysis as a method, bridging early digital methods, platformization studies, and CSCW research on socio-technical systems.
- Reframes links as non-human actors/mediators in ANT terms rather than residual artifacts, offering link-based trace analysis as a complement to semantic/discursive analysis.

## Methods

A mixed-methods controversy-mapping design focused on a single digital object — URLs — collected across two platforms with identical keywords (Twitter via 4CAT; Facebook via CrowdTangle), covering January 2016 to February 2023. A nested query design distinguished a "core collection" of bare, non-evaluative object-phrases from an "extended collection" adding terms drawn from supporters' and opponents' discourse. Quantitative analysis tracked frequency and distribution of digital objects (likes, shares, retweets, links). Network analysis in Gephi connected accounts sharing identical URLs (Force-Atlas 2 layout, Louvain community detection), with link clusters manually coded into seven categories and political-leaning labels drawn from Media Bias/Fact Check and Wikipedia.

## Findings

- On Twitter, external linking was among the most frequent activities (48.7% core, 61.8% extended), but extended, retweet-dominated layers were heavily self-referential (~52% pointed back to Twitter vs. 31% in the core).
- Twitter link networks were dense and polarized, clustering around conservative, far-right, and suspicious (including Russian-language) sites opposed to a news cluster — bridged mainly by platform-internal links, signaling political alignment rather than evidence.
- On Facebook, over 90% of posts contained links regardless of collection, a near-universal default despite the platform's 2018 deprioritization of external-publisher content; link posts drew more comments than link-free posts.
- Facebook link clusters were more dispersed and outward-oriented, connecting to local news, environmental groups, and grassroots protest accounts — functioning evidentially and as mobilizing resources.
- The same resource type was mobilized by different camps across platforms (e.g., state broadcaster TVP circulated within Twitter's conservative segment while news portals on Facebook were shared by center/left accounts).
- Post volume on both platforms tracked key controversy milestones (2019 tender, 2020 cost/environmental decision, September 2022 canal opening).

## Connections
