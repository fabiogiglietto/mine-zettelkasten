# The effects of unsubstantiated claims of voter fraud on confidence in elections

> Berlinski, N., Doyle, M., Guess, A. M., Levy, G., Lyons, B., Montgomery, J. M., Nyhan, B., & Reifler, J. (2023). The effects of unsubstantiated claims of voter fraud on confidence in elections. *Journal of Experimental Political Science*, *10*, 34–49. https://doi.org/10.1017/xps.2021.18
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1017/xps.2021.18)

## Summary

This paper offers direct experimental evidence that unsubstantiated elite claims of voter fraud erode public confidence in the integrity of elections. Fielding a survey experiment among a nationally representative U.S. sample shortly after the 2018 midterms, the authors exposed respondents to real tweets from prominent Republicans (including Trump, Rubio, and Graham) alleging fraud. Such exposure significantly reduced confidence in electoral integrity, with the damage concentrated among those for whom the claims were politically congenial — Republicans and Trump approvers. Notably, fact-check tweets from mainstream outlets failed to measurably offset the harm, though the fraud claims did not diminish support for democracy as a regime type. The study frames unfounded fraud allegations as a distinct threat to electoral legitimacy, presciently anticipating the dynamics of Trump's 2020 claims and January 6.

## Key Contributions

- Provides causal experimental evidence — rather than mixed panel data — that unfounded elite fraud claims reduce confidence in elections.
- Advances external validity by testing claims from actual political leaders in their original tweet format.
- Demonstrates that mainstream fact-checking may be insufficient to counter congenial misinformation.
- Empirically distinguishes erosion of *election confidence* from erosion of *support for democracy as a regime*.
- Connects to literatures on belief echoes, elite cues, motivated reasoning, and electoral legitimacy.

## Methods

An online survey experiment fielded by YouGov among 4,283 U.S. respondents in December 2018/January 2019. Respondents were randomly assigned to four conditions: placebo (non-political tweets), low dose (four fraud tweets), high dose (eight fraud tweets), and low dose plus fact-check (four fraud tweets plus four corrections from the AP, PBS NewsHour, and NYT Politics). Election confidence was measured with a seven-item composite validated via exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, alongside a separate five-item democracy-support battery. Analysis used OLS regression with robust standard errors, including models interacting treatment with partisanship and Trump approval as congeniality moderators. The design was preregistered, with documented deviations.

## Findings

- Low-dose exposure reduced composite election confidence by 0.147 SD and high-dose by 0.168 SD relative to placebo (both p < 0.005).
- No reliable difference between low and high doses — higher dosage did not produce a stronger effect.
- The fact-check condition still significantly reduced confidence (−0.092 SD, p < 0.05); corrections did not offset the harm.
- High-dose effects varied by congeniality, reducing confidence significantly only among Republicans (~0.27 SD) and Trump approvers (~0.34 SD).
- Fact-check effects did not vary by partisanship; negative effects persisted even with corrections present.
- Treatments had overwhelmingly null effects on broader support for democracy.
- Beliefs that ballots are secure from tampering were unaffected, consistent with that specific claim not being challenged in the stimuli.
