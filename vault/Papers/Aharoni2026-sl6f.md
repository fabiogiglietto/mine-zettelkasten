# “Having Fun and Learning Something”: How Users in Four Countries Define What News Is – and Is Not

> Aharoni, T., Arguedas, A. R., Banerjee, S., Mont’Alverne, C., Toff, B., Nielsen, R. K., & Fletcher, R. (2026). “Having Fun and Learning Something”: How Users in Four Countries Define What News Is – and Is Not. *Digital Journalism*. https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2026.2729391
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2026.2729391)

## Summary

This paper flips the usual question of "what is news?" from journalists and scholars back onto ordinary media users. Drawing on 232 participants across Brazil, India, the UK, and the US, the authors show that audiences hold two coexisting definitions of news: a "core" definition tightly aligned with traditional journalism (consequential/political content, formal journalistic tone, current national and global relevance), and an "expanded" definition that emerges in digital, platformized contexts (positive, entertaining, commercial, older, local, and personally relevant content from non-journalistic sources). Both are organized around three criteria of "news-ness": content, format, and relevance. The central argument is that users perform their own everyday boundary work—deciding what counts as news and what does not—in ways that increasingly diverge from professional journalistic definitions online.

## Key Contributions

- Offers what the authors describe as the most expansive and globally comparative empirical investigation to date of what news means to the public, deliberately including Global South contexts.
- Develops a bottom-up, audience-centered typology distinguishing "core" versus "expanded" definitions, each organized along content, format, and relevance.
- Extends "news-ness" and applies boundary work theory to audiences rather than journalists, documenting how users decide what is *not* news.
- Shows that different qualitative methods elicit different definitions—abstract normative associations in focus groups versus contextual judgments in think-aloud tasks—and that platforms actively shape lay understandings of news.
- Proposes two candidate mechanisms (contextual vs. personal) for how the two definitions operate, pointing toward future quantitative work.

## Methods

Qualitative secondary analysis of a dataset originally gathered for the Reuters Institute's Trust in News Project (2021–2022). The corpus combines eight chat-based focus groups (70 participants, split by higher/lower trust), 62 in-depth video interviews, and 100 think-aloud interviews with lower-trust users interacting in real time with Google, Facebook, and (in India and Brazil) WhatsApp. The design is cross-national, spanning Global North (UK, US) and Global South (Brazil, India) markets with variation in age, gender, and news trust. Coding was inductive and iterative in NVivo, using a shared framework, at least two coders per transcript, and translation of Portuguese and Hindi data into English.

## Findings

- **Core content**: news equated with politics and negativity; entertainment strongly excluded—even legacy outlets (BBC, Fox, Indian TV) were dismissed as "not news" when seen as too entertaining.
- **Core format**: expectation of a formal, professional journalistic tone with separation of fact and opinion, though some preferred transparent opinion over feigned neutrality.
- **Core relevance**: news expected to be current and to cover national/global affairs; "old" or "recycled" material often lost its news status.
- **Expanded content**: on platforms, users admitted positive and soft news (celebrities, animals, human drama) and, especially in India, commercial content mimicking journalism.
- **Expanded format**: acceptance of news from non-journalistic actors (politicians, influencers, ordinary users, creators) and tolerance of satire/humor, while accuracy remained central.
- **Expanded relevance**: broadened to intentionally revisited "slow news," local news via Facebook/WhatsApp, and personally useful updates from friends, family, and work.
- The expanded definition surfaced mainly in individual (especially think-aloud) interviews, while normative core definitions dominated group discussions.
- Older, more news-interested users and those familiar with Anglo-American journalism leaned on core definitions; younger, less news-oriented users and Indian and Brazilian participants emphasized personal relevance and practical/local information.

## Connections

This paper's demonstration that platforms reshape lay definitions of news connects to broader work on platform governance and the constitution of public communication, such as [[Gillespie2022-jx]] and [[van-Dijck2018-up]]. Its audience-centered account of how ordinary users judge and distrust news content complements research on media trust and misinformation susceptibility like [[Tsfati2020-uo]], while the finding that non-journalistic and personally relevant content is increasingly folded into "news" bears on the hybrid media system logic articulated in [[Chadwick2013-ns]]. It also speaks to [[Fletcher2026-lv]] as adjacent work on news audiences.
