# “Having Fun and Learning Something”: How Users in Four Countries Define What News Is – and Is Not

> Aharoni, T., Arguedas, A. R., Banerjee, S., Mont’Alverne, C., Toff, B., Nielsen, R. K., & Fletcher, R. (2026). “Having Fun and Learning Something”: How Users in Four Countries Define What News Is – and Is Not. *Digital Journalism*. https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2026.2729391
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/21670811.2026.2729391)

## Summary

This paper asks a deceptively simple question: when ordinary people say "news," what do they actually mean? Drawing on a large multi-method qualitative dataset spanning Brazil, India, the UK, and the US, the authors show that media users maintain two coexisting definitions of news. A "core" definition hews closely to professional journalism — consequential, often negative, political content delivered in an authoritative journalistic format about current national and global affairs. An "expanded" definition, surfacing especially in digital and platform contexts, admits positive, entertaining, commercial, older, local, and personally relevant material from non-journalistic sources. Both are organized around three criteria of "news-ness": content, format, and relevance. The central argument is that audiences perform their own everyday boundary work, deciding what is and is not news in ways that increasingly diverge from how journalists and scholars define the category.

## Key Contributions

- Offers what the authors frame as the most expansive and globally comparative empirical study to date of lay definitions of news, deliberately including often-overlooked Global South contexts.
- Develops a bottom-up, audience-centered typology distinguishing a **core** and an **expanded** definition of news, each structured by content, format, and relevance.
- Extends the concept of "news-ness" and applies **boundary work** theory to audiences rather than journalists, documenting how users actively decide what is *not* news.
- Shows methodologically that different qualitative approaches elicit different definitions — abstract normative associations in focus groups versus situated, context-dependent judgments in think-aloud platform tasks.
- Proposes two candidate mechanisms (contextual vs. personal) for how the two definitions operate, setting up future quantitative work.

## Methods

Qualitative secondary analysis of a dataset originally gathered for the Reuters Institute's Trust in News Project (2021–2022), covering 232 participants across four countries. The corpus combines eight chat-based focus groups (70 participants, split by higher- and lower-trust), 62 in-depth video interviews, and 100 think-aloud interviews in which lower-trust users interacted in real time with Google, Facebook, and (in India and Brazil) WhatsApp. The cross-national comparative design spans Global North and Global South markets with variation in age, gender, and news trust. Coding was inductive and iterative in NVivo, using a shared framework, at least two coders per transcript, and translation of Portuguese and Hindi data.

## Findings

- **Core content:** News was associated with politics and negativity; entertainment was strongly excluded, and even legacy outlets (BBC, Fox, Indian TV) were dismissed as "not news" when deemed too entertaining.
- **Core format:** Users expected a formal, professional journalistic tone with separation of fact and opinion — though some preferred transparent opinion to feigned neutrality.
- **Core relevance:** News was expected to be current and to cover national/global affairs; "old" or recycled information often lost its news status.
- **Expanded content:** On platforms, users admitted positive and soft news (celebrities, animals, human drama) and, especially in India, commercial content mimicking journalism.
- **Expanded format:** Users accepted news from non-journalistic actors (politicians, influencers, ordinary users, creators) and tolerated satire and humor, while still prizing accuracy.
- **Expanded relevance:** Digital news broadened to include intentionally revisited "slow news," local updates via Facebook/WhatsApp, and personally useful information from friends, family, and work.
- The expanded definition emerged mainly in individual think-aloud interviews, while normative core definitions dominated group discussions.
- Older, more news-interested users and those familiar with Anglo-American journalism leaned on core definitions; younger, less news-oriented users and participants in India and Brazil emphasized personal relevance and practical/local information.

## Connections

This paper's central concern with how platforms reshape users' lived understandings of news connects it to work on how platform audiences and online communities interpret and circulate information (e.g., [[Kristensen2025-ni]]). Its focus on trust in news and divergence between professional and public conceptions of journalism in a hybrid media environment bears on debates about misinformation and media credibility; its finding that users treat accuracy as central even in an expanded definition speaks to research on how publics distinguish reliable from unreliable information (e.g., [[Humprecht2020-gd]]). Beyond these, the connections to the information-disorder literature under this topic are thematic rather than tightly methodological, since the paper's contribution is audience-centered genre definition rather than disinformation detection per se.
