# The social and cultural life of artificial intelligence (AI) slop: A research framework

> So, R. J., & Long, H. (2026). The social and cultural life of artificial intelligence (AI) slop: A research framework. *Big Data & Society*, *13*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517261506941
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20539517261506941)

## Summary

This conceptual paper reframes the problem of AI-generated content by rejecting the dominant institutional view that treats such content as "slop"—a form of digital pollution to be detected and filtered. So and Long argue that AI content should be understood **relationally** rather than as an object category with intrinsic properties: its value, meaning, and harm depend on who produces it, who engages with it, and why. They introduce SLOP (capitalized, as an analytic category) to name a *social condition* in which the scale and surface competence of AI content have broken the link between how content is made and what its form signals about quality. On this view, the real scarcity generative AI produces is not content but reliable evaluative signals—the marks by which human judgment once distinguished genuine competence from its imitation. The governance problem, they contend, has been misdiagnosed: institutions seek better rules when what they have actually lost is the evaluative capacity those rules stood in for.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **SLOP** as an analytic category distinct from the vernacular dismissal "slop," recasting AI content as a social condition rather than a class of objects.
- Maps systematically what the detection, spam, and misinformation/disinformation frameworks each capture and each miss.
- Articulates a three-dimensional structure—**production, reception, effects/governance**—grounded in platform studies and cultural sociology.
- Offers a concrete, **falsifiable research agenda** with methods designed to work without platform cooperation.
- Reframes governance debates around lost evaluative capacity, calling for investment in evaluation itself rather than new rules.

## Methods

A work of theory-building rather than empirical study. The authors critically synthesize and compare three literatures (AI-content detection, spam scholarship, misinformation studies), then draw on platform studies (Gillespie, Srnicek, Seaver) and the sociology of culture and valuation (Bourdieu's field theory, Lamont on evaluation and "heterarchy," Bok on deception vs. concealment, Johns on print fixity). They specify a three-strand, falsifiable agenda: ethnographic fieldwork with content producers in Kenya, India, Vietnam, and the Philippines; computational analysis of "judgment in the wild" (e.g., LinkedIn's AI-slop flagging button); and interviews plus archival analysis of peer-review gatekeepers (desk-rejection rates, time-to-decision, policy changes).

## Findings

- Prevalence estimates: ~20% of YouTube videos, >80% of LinkedIn long-form posts, 9% of new US newspaper online articles, and 6.5–16.9% of reviews at major AI conferences show LLM-generated text.
- Detection tools perform poorly: one study found a >60% false-positive rate for non-native English writers; another found mean accuracy under 40%, dropping to 22% when AI text was lightly edited.
- Detection is structurally positioned to lose an arms race, since distributional signatures fade as models improve.
- Spam's economics were bounded by human production costs and defined by *unwantedness*; SLOP breaks both conditions—content is cheap to make and is wanted by some while rejected by others.
- Generative AI moves spam's "cost collapse" upstream from distribution to creation, decoupling surface signals of expertise from the labor they once indexed.
- Most AI-assisted content involves **concealment rather than deception** (per Bok), is neither necessarily false nor intended to harm, and therefore falls outside misinformation categories.
- Predicts producers' self-accounts will track platform position rather than tools, and that overloaded gatekeepers will default to hard-to-fake proxies like institutional prestige and personal acquaintance—risking a crisis of *access*.
