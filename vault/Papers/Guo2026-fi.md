# The politics of the “non-political”: A conceptual review

> Guo, Y., & Fan, Y. (2026). The politics of the “non-political”: A conceptual review. *Political Communication*, 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2745844
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2745844)

## Summary

This conceptual review interrogates how political science and communication scholars use the label "non-political" and how they draw the boundary between the political and the nonpolitical. Adopting a *relational* rather than *substantialist* ontology of politics—in which the political is constituted through its differentiating relations with nonpolitical domains rather than through intrinsic features—the authors conduct a boundary analysis of 140 empirical studies. They identify two dominant conceptual treatments of the nonpolitical (categorical and contextual) and four relational modes linking the two domains. Their central argument is that attending to the nonpolitical and to (de)politicization offers a heuristic, indirect route to reflecting on the nature of politics itself, and that boundary-drawing is itself a power-laden, inherently political act. Rather than proposing the nonpolitical as a standalone subfield, they call for a more active reconciliation of the two treatments—"the politics of the non-political."

## Key Contributions

- Organizes a fragmented literature by distinguishing **two conceptual treatments** (categorical and contextual) and **four relational modes** (spillover/invasion, comparison, (de)politicization, embeddedness).
- Offers a relational, boundary-analysis framework for understanding *how* and *why* scholars demarcate the nonpolitical.
- Provides practical operational guidance: using "non-political" labels more carefully, clarifying criteria and level of analysis, and moving from binary toward continuous/gradational measures of political relevance—especially for computational research.
- Reclaims (de)politicization as a meta-theoretical, power-sensitive relational lens, urging processual explanations of how and for whom political meanings emerge.
- Advances epistemological reflection on the nature of politics by "decentering the political."

## Methods

The study combines a genealogical review of the nature of politics in political theory (from Plato and Aristotle through Schmitt, Wolin, feminist theory, Foucault, and relational theorists) with a systematic literature review and inductive coding. Treating "non-political" as a boundary object rather than a mature analytical concept, the authors searched Web of Science using three keyword sets (non-political/nonpolitical, apolitical, and non-news). Scope was limited to English-language SSCI articles in political science and communication, 2004–2024, yielding 448 articles; screening for empirical evidence and retaining journals with more than five sample articles produced a final sample of 140. Inductive coding characterized boundary delineation, recurring relational mechanisms, and authors' stated motivations and implications.

## Findings

- Roughly **two-thirds** of articles adopt a **categorical treatment**, operationalizing the nonpolitical as a binary category—either residually (what remains after political elements) or substantively (the private, mundane/entertainment, neutral, or nonpartisan).
- Categorical work is linked to the relational modes of **spillover/invasion** (including "backward spillover" from nonpolitical to political) and **comparison** (stable domains placed side by side).
- The **contextual treatment** views the nonpolitical as a temporary, hybrid, transformative state, tied to **(de)politicization** (processual change driven by media, elites/institutions, and grassroots actors) and **embeddedness** (political meaning within ostensibly nonpolitical settings, e.g., "third spaces," intertextuality).
- Categorical distinctions dominate quantitative/post-positivist work; contextual fluidity is emphasized in interpretive and critical work. Some studies reconcile them pragmatically via hedging ("ostensibly," "seemingly") and gradational measures of "politicalness."
- *Political Behavior* (15) and *Political Communication* (11) published the most sampled studies.
- Boundaries persist because they serve theoretical coherence, methodological classification, and rhetorical signaling of novelty—but they can essentialize the nonpolitical and conceal embedded political meaning and power relations.
