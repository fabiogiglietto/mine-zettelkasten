# The politics of the “non-political”: A conceptual review

> Guo, Y., & Fan, Y. (2026). The politics of the “non-political”: A conceptual review. *Political Communication*, 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2745844
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/10584609.2026.2745844)

## Summary

This conceptual review interrogates how political science and communication scholars use the label "non-political" and how they draw the boundary between the political and the nonpolitical. Adopting a relational (rather than substantialist) ontology — in which the political is constituted through its differentiating relations with nonpolitical domains rather than through intrinsic features — the authors perform a boundary analysis of 140 empirical studies. They identify two dominant conceptual "treatments" of the nonpolitical (categorical and contextual) and four relational modes that link the two domains. Their central argument is that attending to the nonpolitical and to processes of (de)politicization offers a heuristic, indirect route to reflecting on the nature of politics itself — "the politics of the non-political."

## Key Contributions

- Organizes a fragmented literature by distinguishing two conceptual treatments of the nonpolitical — **categorical** (inherent, stable, exclusive) and **contextual** (temporary, transformative, context-dependent).
- Identifies four **relational modes** linking the political and nonpolitical: spillover/invasion, comparison, (de)politicization, and embeddedness.
- Provides a relational, boundary-analysis framework for understanding how and why scholars demarcate the nonpolitical.
- Offers operational guidance: clarifying criteria and level of analysis, and shifting from binary toward continuous/gradational measures of "politicalness," especially for computational research.
- Reclaims (de)politicization as a meta-theoretical, power-sensitive relational lens, urging processual explanations of how and for whom political meanings emerge.
- Advances epistemological reflection on the nature of politics by "decentering the political."

## Methods

The paper combines a **genealogical review** of the nature of politics in political theory (from Plato and Aristotle through Schmitt, Wolin, feminist theory, Foucault, and relational theorists such as Emirbayer and Selg & Ventsel) with a **systematic literature review** grounded in boundary analysis. Treating "non-political" as a boundary object rather than a mature concept, the authors searched Web of Science using three keyword sets (non-political/nonpolitical, apolitical, non-news). Scope was limited to English-language SSCI articles in political science and communication, 2004–2024, yielding 448 articles; screening for empirical evidence and journal representation produced a final sample of 140. These were **inductively coded** for how the boundary is drawn, recurring relational mechanisms, and stated motivations and implications.

## Findings

- Roughly two-thirds of articles adopt a **categorical** treatment, defining the nonpolitical as a binary — either residually (what remains after political elements) or substantively (the private, mundane/entertainment, neutral, or nonpartisan).
- Categorical work is associated with **spillover/invasion** (including "backward spillover" from nonpolitical to political) and **comparison** (stable domains placed side by side).
- The **contextual** treatment views the nonpolitical as a temporary, hybrid, transformative state, tied to **(de)politicization** (driven by media, elites/institutions, grassroots actors) and **embeddedness** (political meaning in "third spaces" and intertextuality).
- Categorical distinctions dominate quantitative/post-positivist work; contextual fluidity characterizes interpretive and critical work. Some studies reconcile the two via hedging ("ostensibly," "seemingly") and gradational measures.
- *Political Behavior* (15 articles) and *Political Communication* (11 articles) contributed the most studies.
- Boundaries serve theoretical coherence, methodological classification, and rhetorical signaling of novelty — but can essentialize the nonpolitical and conceal embedded power relations, making boundary-drawing itself a political act.
