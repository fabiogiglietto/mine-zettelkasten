# (In)Visible politics: Influencers as brokers of the state on TikTok and Douyin

> Divon, T., & Lu, Y. (2026). (In)Visible politics: Influencers as brokers of the state on TikTok and Douyin. *Convergence: The International Journal of Research into New Media Technologies*. https://doi.org/10.1177/13548565261449632
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/13548565261449632)

## Summary

This paper investigates how social media influencers—many drawn from ostensibly apolitical domains like fitness, food, and lifestyle—strategically engage with the state during political crises to sustain their online visibility. Through a comparative content analysis of 589 videos from TikTok in Israel (2023 judicial reform) and Douyin in China (2020 COVID-19 crisis), the authors theorize "state brokerage" as a visibility practice emerging from transactional relations among influencers, platforms, and the state. Their central argument is that during moments of crisis the state itself becomes a strategic visibility *resource* that influencers draw upon, mobilize, and translate into platform-native vernaculars—a dynamic that operates across both democratic and authoritarian regimes, though conditioned differently by each.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and theorizes **state brokerage** as a visibility practice rooted in transactional relations among influencers, platforms, and the state.
- Extends political brokerage theory beyond networked intermediaries to foreground the state as an *object and resource* of brokerage.
- Offers a cross-regime comparative framework (democratic Israel vs. authoritarian China) on shared corporate infrastructure (ByteDance's TikTok and Douyin).
- Provides an operationalizable three-part analytic lens: **state glamorization, patriotic expression, out-group derogation**.
- Reconceptualizes "political influencing" as a contextual, emergent practice encompassing non-political creators who become vocal during crises.
- Contributes to scholarship on digital nationalism and "platform nations" where algorithmic visibility, participation, and state power intersect.

## Methods

Comparative, multimodal content analysis of 589 videos: 244 TikTok videos from 97 Israeli influencers and 345 Douyin videos from 242 Chinese influencers. Israeli data was purposively sampled from a corpus of 6,533 videos (across 998 trending hashtags, Jan–Jul 2023), selecting Hebrew-language videos on the judicial reform from non-political accounts with over 10,000 followers. Chinese data was drawn from the Douyin Trending Video dataset (50,813 videos, 9,946 hashtags, Mar–Jun 2020), sampling verified or large (100,000+ follower) accounts addressing COVID-19. The authors used open coding to surface emergent patterns, followed by multimodal analysis of narration, in-video text, imagery, sound, templates, gestures, and body language, developing a coding framework around three visibility strategies with primary/secondary classifications for ambiguous cases.

## Findings

- Three visibility strategies emerged: (1) state glamorization, (2) patriotic expression, (3) out-group derogation.
- **Israel** (244 videos): 61 state glamorization, 53 patriotic expression, 42 out-group derogation—the latter mostly targeting internal left-wing adversaries framed as threats to national unity.
- **China** (345 videos): 86 patriotic expression (50 celebrating medical workers as heroes), 55 state glamorization, only 9 out-group derogation—mainly targeting the United States or domestic rule-violators.
- Israeli creators embedded pro-reform messaging into viral templates ("Freedom Squats," "come to work with me"), enabling self-directed positioning under limited regional platform oversight.
- Chinese creators largely reproduced standardized state scripts (party slogans, hashtags like #BecomingAngelsToGuardYou), reflecting an instrumental mode where compliance increases reach.
- Brokerage was both **proactive** (framing crisis as a visibility opportunity) and **reactive** (aligning with dominant discourse to avoid invisibility).

## Connections

This paper's focus on platform governance as a structuring force that shapes what circulates connects to work on platform infrastructures and curation such as [[Helmond2026-ll]] and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], while its reworking of influencer self-presentation and visibility labour builds on [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. Its treatment of out-group derogation and the construction of internal adversaries during crises resonates with scholarship on affective polarization and partisan animosity, including [[Iyengar2019-jj]], and its account of non-political creators becoming politically vocal speaks to debates over how digital nationalism and partisan content spread across platforms (e.g., [[Cabbuag2024-me]]).
