# (In)Visible politics: Influencers as brokers of the state on TikTok and Douyin

> Divon, T., & Lu, Y. (2026). (In)Visible politics: Influencers as brokers of the state on TikTok and Douyin. *Convergence: The International Journal of Research into New Media Technologies*. https://doi.org/10.1177/13548565261449632
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/13548565261449632)

## Summary

This paper investigates how social media influencers—especially those from ostensibly non-political domains like fitness, food, and lifestyle—strategically engage with the state during political crises to sustain their online visibility. Drawing on a comparative content analysis of TikTok videos from Israel's 2023 judicial reform crisis and Douyin videos from China's 2020 COVID-19 crisis, Divon and Lu theorize **state brokerage** as a visibility practice emerging from transactional relations among influencers, platforms, and the state. Their central argument is that during crises the state itself becomes a *strategic visibility resource*—a thing influencers draw upon and mobilize—rather than merely a political institution. By comparing a democratic and an authoritarian regime operating on the same corporate infrastructure (ByteDance), the authors show that state power and platform governance jointly condition how political alignment, affect, and aesthetics circulate and are rewarded.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and theorizes **"state brokerage"** as a visibility practice born of transactional relations among influencers, platforms, and the state.
- Extends political brokerage theory beyond networked intermediaries to foreground the **state as both object and resource** of brokerage.
- Offers a rare **cross-regime comparative framework** (democratic Israel vs. authoritarian China) on a shared platform infrastructure.
- Provides an operationalizable **three-part analytic lens**: state glamorization, patriotic expression, and out-group derogation.
- Reconceptualizes "political influencing" as a **contextual, emergent practice** that includes non-political creators who become politically vocal during crises.
- Treats nationalism and patriotism as **situational repertoires of practice** rather than fixed ideologies.

## Methods

A comparative content analysis of 589 videos: 244 TikTok videos from 97 Israeli influencers and 345 Douyin videos from 242 Chinese influencers. The Israeli corpus was purposively sampled from 998 trending hashtags (Jan–Jul 2023) yielding 6,533 videos, filtered for Hebrew-language judicial-reform content from non-political accounts over 10,000 followers. The Chinese corpus drew from an existing Douyin Trending Video dataset (9,946 hashtags, 50,813 videos, Mar–Jun 2020), sampling verified or 100,000+ follower accounts addressing COVID-19. The authors used open coding to surface emergent patterns, then multimodal content analysis (narration, in-video text, descriptions, imagery, sound, templates, gestures, body language), assigning primary and secondary classifications for ambiguous cases, with faces and profiles blurred per AoIR ethics guidelines.

## Findings

- Three visibility strategies emerged: **state glamorization, patriotic expression, and out-group derogation**.
- In Israel (244 videos): 61 state glamorization, 53 patriotic expression, 42 out-group derogation—the latter largely targeting internal left-wing adversaries framed as threats to national unity.
- In China (345 videos): 86 patriotic expression (50 celebrating medical workers as heroes), 55 state glamorization, and only 9 out-group derogation—mostly aimed at the United States or domestic rule-violators.
- Israeli creators embedded pro-reform messaging into viral templates like "Freedom Squats" and "come to work with me," exercising **self-directed political positioning** under limited regional platform oversight.
- Chinese creators largely reproduced standardized **state-aligned scripts** (party slogans, hashtags like #BecomingAngelsToGuardYou), reflecting an instrumental mode where compliance boosts reach.
- Brokerage operated through a dual dynamic: **proactive** (framing upheaval as an attention opportunity) and **reactive** (aligning with dominant discourse to avoid invisibility).

## Connections

This paper bridges platform-governance and visibility scholarship with studies of political communication under algorithmic conditions, resonating with work on how platform infrastructures shape and mediate public discourse such as [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], and with the influencer-centered study of self-presentation and visibility practices in [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. Its treatment of partisan out-group derogation and nationalism as situational repertoires speaks to the affective-polarization literature represented by [[Iyengar2019-jj]]. Within the provided set, these are the genuine intellectual ties; much of the surrounding partisanship and echo-chamber work addresses adjacent but distinct concerns.
