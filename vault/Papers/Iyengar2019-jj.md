# The origins and consequences of affective polarization in the United States

> Iyengar, S., Lelkes, Y., Levendusky, M., Malhotra, N., & Westwood, S. J. (2019). The origins and consequences of affective polarization in the United States. *Annual Review of Political Science*, *22*, 129–146. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-polisci-051117-073034)

## Summary

This review article synthesizes an interdisciplinary literature on **affective polarization** in the United States — the growing tendency of Democrats and Republicans to dislike, distrust, and avoid one another. The authors' central argument is that affective polarization stems from **partisanship as a social identity** that sorts the political world into a liked in-group and a disliked out-group, and that this affective divide is theoretically and empirically distinct from ideological (issue-based) polarization. Drawing on social psychology, they reframe the long-running debate over whether Americans are polarized (Fiorina versus Abramowitz) by shifting attention from policy disagreement to partisan identity and affect. The paper traces measurement approaches, causes, and consequences that spill well beyond politics into economic and social life, and evaluates strategies to mitigate partisan animus.

## Key Contributions

- Offers an integrative framework grounding affective polarization in social identity theory and cleanly distinguishing it from ideological polarization.
- Catalogs three families of measurement techniques — survey self-reports, implicit measures, and behavioral measures — with their strengths and limitations.
- Assembles evidence on the **nonpolitical spillover** of partisan animus into social, economic, and professional decisions as a distinctive consequence.
- Reviews and evaluates candidate causes and mitigation strategies, flagging which claims (echo chambers, residential sorting) remain empirically contested.
- Sets out a future research agenda covering mechanisms, in-group-love versus out-group-hate, the causal role of sorting, elite/mass linkages, and comparative extensions.

## Methods

A narrative review and synthesis spanning political science and social psychology. The authors discuss three measurement approaches: **survey self-reports** (ANES feeling thermometers, trait ratings, social distance/marriage items), **implicit measures** (partisan IAT), and **behavioral measures** (trust and dictator economic games, scholarship-selection and hiring experiments). Evidence is drawn from longitudinal survey and time-series data (ANES ~1978–2016, Pew), experimental and audit studies, natural and field experiments, online dating data, voter files, and consumer/tax-receipt behavioral data.

## Findings

- ANES feeling-thermometer affective polarization rose from **22.64° (1978) to 40.87° (2016)**, driven mainly by declining out-party warmth rather than rising in-party affection.
- Opposition to a child marrying across party lines climbed from ~4–5% (1960) to roughly one-third of Democrats and one-half of Republicans by 2010.
- Implicit partisan bias is widespread (~70% favor their in-party) and **larger than implicit racial bias** (D-score 0.50 for party vs. 0.18 for race).
- In a scholarship experiment, ~80% of partisans chose the co-partisan even when the out-partisan was more qualified; racial bias was minimal.
- Co-partisans receive financial and social advantages; partisan matching raised dating message exchange by 9.5%, and ~80.5% of married couples share party ID.
- Sorting explains only ~5% of the variance in affective polarization, and animus rose comparably among ideologically consistent and inconsistent partisans — indicating affect is largely distinct from ideology.
- Correcting misperceptions about party composition (people overestimate stereotypical group prevalence by ~342%) and priming common American identity both reduce out-party animus, though effects may be fragile.
