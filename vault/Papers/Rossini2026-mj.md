# Disrupting democracy: how informational pathways and misinformation shape trust in election results

> Rossini, P., Mont'Alverne, C., & Kalogeropoulos, A. (2026). Disrupting democracy: how informational pathways and misinformation shape trust in election results. *Journal of Elections, Public Opinion and Parties*, 1–23. https://doi.org/10.1080/17457289.2026.2701896
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/17457289.2026.2701896)

## Summary

This paper investigates how citizens' informational diets — mainstream news, social media, and messaging apps — together with belief in electoral misinformation shaped changes in trust in the 2022 Brazilian presidential election results. Using an original three-wave panel survey of Brazilian internet users fielded around each voting round, the authors show that professional journalism bolsters electoral trust over time while belief in electoral misinformation and having voted for the illiberal incumbent Bolsonaro depress it. The central argument is theoretical as much as empirical: misinformation should be understood not as isolated false content but as a strategic elite practice embedded in evolving digital media systems, weaponized by illiberal incumbents (with WhatsApp functioning as a "propaganda machine") to erode electoral legitimacy and drive democratic backsliding.

## Key Contributions

- First multi-wave, over-time measurement of how informational pathways and electoral misinformation shape trust in election results in a Global South context, extending largely US-based scholarship.
- Demonstrates the democratic-resilience role of professional/mainstream journalism, holding even among supporters of the illiberal incumbent.
- Disaggregates messaging-app effects by use type (news consumption vs. discussion) and by network composition (strong/weak/no ties), rather than treating platforms monolithically.
- Reframes misinformation as strategic elite practice tied to democratic backsliding.
- Tests motivated reasoning and the winner–loser gap in a multi-party system with weak partisanship and compulsory voting, where vote choice reflects rejection more than partisan strength.

## Methods

An original three-wave opt-in panel survey of Brazilian internet users (W1 n=1,600; W2 n=1,328; W3 n=1,034, October–December 2022), with quotas for age, gender, social class, and region. The dependent variable was a single-item, 4-point measure of trust in the presidential election results adapted from the World Values Survey. Independent variables included frequency of mainstream news, social media news, and messaging news use; WhatsApp group talk with strong/weak/no ties; and a "misinformed score" (0–8) built from 12 statements measured in Wave 2. Random effects within–between models captured both between-person and within-person change, with Bonferroni correction across five focal hypotheses and replication on the subset of Bolsonaro voters.

## Findings

- Belief in electoral misinformation was negatively associated with trust in elections (β = −.157, p<.001).
- Voting for Bolsonaro strongly predicted lower trust (β = −.946), as did right-wing ideology (β = −.315).
- Mainstream news use positively predicted trust both between and within participants over time (β = .069, p<.01), holding even among Bolsonaro voters.
- Social media news use had null effects; messaging news showed a negative between-person effect (β = −.129) but no within-person effect in the full sample.
- Among Bolsonaro voters, messaging news use eroded trust over time (β = −.069), strong-tie WhatsApp discussion raised trust (β = .165), and no-tie (stranger) discussion lowered it (β = −.161).
- WhatsApp group effects varied by network composition: no-tie discussion lowered trust while weak-tie discussion weakly raised it (not robust to correction).
