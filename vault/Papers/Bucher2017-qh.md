# The algorithmic imaginary: exploring the ordinary affects of Facebook algorithms

> Bucher, T. (2017). The algorithmic imaginary: exploring the ordinary affects of Facebook algorithms. *Information, Communication & Society*, *20*, 30–44. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2016.1154086
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118x.2016.1154086)

## Summary

Bucher investigates how ordinary Facebook users come to perceive and make sense of algorithms that are, by design, hidden and opaque. Through 25 personal "algorithm stories" gathered from tweets and email interviews, she develops the concept of the **algorithmic imaginary** — the ways people imagine, perceive, and experience algorithms. Her central move is to treat these imaginings not as mere misperceptions but as affectively productive forces that play a generative role in shaping the Facebook algorithm itself. The paper argues that users need no access to code to encounter algorithms; they do so through moods, affects, and sensations, making attention to how algorithms *make people feel* essential for understanding their social power.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces the **algorithmic imaginary** as an analytical tool for studying how people perceive and relate to algorithms.
- Shifts focus from "algorithm awareness" (whether users know algorithms exist) to the situated, affective encounters through which people experience them.
- Applies affect theory and phenomenological/ethnographic approaches to algorithm studies, foregrounding emotion and sensation over code.
- Advances a recursive "force-relations" account in which user perceptions and practices help shape algorithmic systems rather than being merely their effects.
- Demonstrates a methodology for accessing personal algorithm experiences by "getting behind the tweets" rather than peeking inside the black box.

## Methods

A qualitative study combining analysis of public tweets with email interviews, conducted over nine months (October 2014–June 2015). Bucher searched Twitter for keywords like "Facebook algorithm," "algorithm AND weird," and "algorithm AND creepy," screenshotting personal rather than marketing tweets. She contacted 47 users; 25 participated via short email interviews about the tweet's context, their mental model of the algorithm, and whether awareness changed their use. Responses were coded for triggering situations, beliefs, effects on use, tactics, and concerns. The theoretical framing draws on Berlant's notion of the "situation" and Stewart's "ordinary affects," focusing exclusively on Facebook for comparability.

## Findings

- Awareness typically surfaces in moments of breakdown, surprise, or violated expectations.
- Recurring affective scenes include *profiling identity* (feeling misclassified), *whoa moments* (uncanny targeted ads), *faulty prediction* (annoyance at misrepresentation), the *popularity game* (strategic posting for visibility), *cruel connections* (painful algorithmic memories like Year in Review), and *ruined friendships* (friends vanishing or reappearing).
- Participants held normative expectations about how algorithms *should* behave, calling the system "broken" when violated.
- Users built tacit theories of the algorithm despite admitting ignorance of its workings, optimising behaviour (timing, word choice, positive phrasing) to become "algorithmically recognizable."
- Strategic adjustments ranged from liking posts to boost visibility, to hiding posts to "train" the feed, to data obfuscation designed to mislead the algorithm.
- Algorithmic classification often clashed with self-perception, producing discomfort, anger, or counter-behaviour.

## Connections

This paper is foundational for research on folk theories and algorithmic sense-making, closely linked to [[Cotter2019-js]] on visibility games and playing the algorithm, and to [[Petre2019-zh]] on how producers manage algorithmic uncertainty. Its argument that users actively shape and resist platform systems resonates with platform-power accounts such as [[van-Dijck2018-up]] and with studies of self-presentation under algorithmic conditions like [[Marwick2011-sl02]]. More broadly, the emphasis on felt, everyday experience of opaque systems complements work on algorithmic awareness and engagement such as [[Gagrcin2024-dl]].
