# Misinformation and its correction: Continued influence and successful debiasing: Continued influence and successful debiasing

> Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U. K. H., Seifert, C. M., Schwarz, N., & Cook, J. (2012). Misinformation and its correction: Continued influence and successful debiasing: Continued influence and successful debiasing. *Psychol. Sci. Public Interest*, *13*, 106–131.
>
> [View paper](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=Misinformation%20and%20its%20correction%3A%20Continued%20influence%20and%20successful%20debiasing%3A%20Continued%20influence%20and%20successful%20debiasing)

## Summary

This influential review synthesizes cognitive and social psychology to explain why misinformation persists even after clear, credible corrections — the "continued influence effect." The authors argue against the naive assumption that debunking alone suffices to erase false beliefs, showing that retractions typically only halve reliance on misinformation and can sometimes backfire, entrenching the very beliefs they aim to dispel. The paper positions individual-level cognition (truth assessment, fluency, memory updating) within broader societal and media dynamics, and distills the evidence into concrete, theory-grounded recommendations for practitioners tasked with correcting false information.

## Key Contributions

- Comprehensive cross-disciplinary synthesis of why misinformation persists at both individual and societal levels.
- Consolidation of competing theoretical accounts of the continued influence effect: mental models, retrieval failure, fluency/familiarity, and reactance.
- Actionable, cognitively grounded recommendations for journalists, health professionals, educators, and science communicators.
- Identification and explanation of counterproductive "backfire" effects (familiarity, overkill, worldview) that corrections must avoid.
- A bridge between laboratory cognitive findings and real-world public-information and policy contexts.

## Methods

Narrative literature review and synthesis spanning cognitive psychology, social psychology, political science, and communication research. The authors draw on the experimental continued influence paradigm (notably the fictitious warehouse-fire narrative with target misinformation and later retraction) and integrate real-world case studies including the vaccine-autism myth, Iraq WMD claims, and the Obama "birther"/"death panel" myths. Experimental findings on truth assessment, fluency, source credibility, and perceived social consensus are woven throughout.

## Findings

- Retractions at most halve references to misinformation and sometimes have no effect, even when people remember and believe the retraction.
- Strengthened negations can backfire, increasing reliance on misinformation.
- "Myth vs. fact" campaigns can reverse after a short delay, with readers later misremembering myths as facts — especially older adults.
- Only three techniques reliably improve corrections: pre-exposure warnings, repeated retractions, and providing a plausible alternative causal explanation.
- Providing an alternative narrative that fills the coherence gap is the most effective technique.
- Worldview-dissonant corrections can produce backfire effects, though findings on this are mixed.
- Media outlet choice predicts misinformation levels, and "false balance" distorts public understanding of issues like climate consensus.
- Fluency, repetition, and perceived social consensus increase acceptance of statements as true — even from a single repeated source.
