# Narrative monitoring and argument-checking: Enhancing effectiveness in countering disinformation beyond fact-checking

> Ramos, C., Jiménez-Cruz, C., & Hernández-Escayola, P. (2026). Narrative monitoring and argument-checking: Enhancing effectiveness in countering disinformation beyond fact-checking. *Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review*. https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-198
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-198)

## Summary

This commentary, authored by practitioners at the Spanish fact-checking organization Maldita.es, argues that conventional fact-checking—verifying discrete claims—is necessary but insufficient to disrupt the persistence and adaptability of disinformation during crises. Using the October 2024 Valencia (DANA) floods as a case study, the authors propose supplementing debunking with two complementary strategies: **narrative monitoring** (real-time tracking of clustered, evolving claims) and **argument-checking** (interrogating the logical and rhetorical structures that make claims persuasive). Their central example is the conspiracy theory alleging authorities were hiding hundreds of flood victims in an inaccessible underground parking structure at the Bonaire shopping center in Aldaia. The paper reframes fact-checking as one layer in a broader "narrative and argument integrity" strategy aimed at neutralizing disinformation narratives as whole units rather than as isolated hoaxes.

## Key Contributions

- Proposes a **three-step hybrid workflow**: rapid claim-level verification, concurrent narrative surveillance to detect storyline recombinations, and argument-checking templates exposing recurring fallacies.
- Reframes fact-checking as one layer within a broader **narrative and argument integrity** strategy rather than a standalone remedy.
- Offers a concrete practitioner account of deploying narrative monitoring and argument-checking during a live crisis, bridging argumentation theory and fact-checking practice.
- Articulates how argument-checking has both a debunking function and an **anticipatory (prebunking/inoculation) dimension** transferable across claim variants.

## Methods

A single-case study of the Bonaire "hidden victims" conspiracy during the DANA floods. Narrative monitoring was conducted through Maldita.es's AI-assisted platform (DMS), which tracks user queries, social posts, and forwarded messages in real time, clustering thematically related claims and mapping their evolution. Argument-checking drew on argumentation theory, applying critical questions to assess causal links, logical coherence, and rhetorical mechanisms rather than veracity alone. The authors also applied David Grimes's probabilistic model of conspiracy viability to test the logistical feasibility of concealing hundreds of bodies, and documented message volumes and narrative variants via a project (AC/DC) with the University of Navarra.

## Findings

- An information vacuum around the inaccessible flooded structure fostered rumors of trapped bodies; claimed victim counts escalated from 44 to 86, 258, over 800, and even 1,000.
- Official confirmation (Nov 5, 2024) that no bodies were found did not dissolve the narrative but triggered a **second, conspiratorial phase** alleging cover-ups, hidden child victims, and refrigerated-truck body removals.
- The conspiracy relied on identifiable persuasive tactics: appeals to false authority, fabricated documents, personal-experience storytelling, and emotional appeals (anger, fear).
- Recurring fallacies included post hoc ergo propter hoc, non sequitur, argument from ignorance, false authority, and slippery slope.
- Message volume reached ~13,000 in seven days—four times the average—overwhelming claim-by-claim debunking.
- Argument-checking counterarguments were reformulated as accessible questions to give the public reusable reasoning tools.
