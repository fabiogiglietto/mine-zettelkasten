# Narrative monitoring and argument-checking: Enhancing effectiveness in countering disinformation beyond fact-checking

> Ramos, C., Jiménez-Cruz, C., & Hernández-Escayola, P. (2026). Narrative monitoring and argument-checking: Enhancing effectiveness in countering disinformation beyond fact-checking. *Harvard Kennedy School Misinformation Review*. https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-198
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.37016/mr-2020-198)

## Summary

This commentary, authored by practitioners at the Spanish fact-checking organization Maldita.es, argues that traditional claim-level fact-checking is necessary but insufficient to disrupt disinformation during crises. Corrected beliefs regress over time, and neutralizing individual hoaxes does little to stop the momentum of a broader narrative that groups many claims under a shared, emotionally resonant explanatory frame. The authors propose two complementary strategies: **narrative monitoring** (real-time tracking and clustering of related claims) and **argument-checking** (analyzing the logical and rhetorical structures that make claims persuasive). They ground the argument in a single-case study of the conspiracy theory that authorities had hidden hundreds of bodies in a flooded underground parking structure at the Bonaire shopping center during the October 2024 Valencia (DANA) floods.

## Key Contributions

- Proposes a three-step hybrid workflow: rapid claim-level verification, concurrent narrative surveillance to detect storyline recombinations, and argument-checking templates that expose recurring fallacies.
- Reframes fact-checking as one layer within a broader "narrative and argument integrity" strategy rather than a standalone solution.
- Provides a concrete practitioner account of applying these tools during a real crisis, bridging argumentation theory and fact-checking practice.
- Articulates how argument-checking has both a debunking and an anticipatory (prebunking / inoculation) dimension, producing responses that transfer across claim variants.

## Methods

A single-case study of the Bonaire "hidden victims" conspiracy following the DANA floods. Narrative monitoring was conducted via Maldita.es's AI-assisted platform (DMS), which tracks user queries, social media posts, and forwarded messages in real time, clustering thematically related claims and mapping their evolution. Argument-checking drew on argumentation theory, applying critical questions to assess causal premises, warrants, and rhetorical mechanisms rather than only veracity. The authors additionally used David Grimes's probabilistic model of conspiracy viability to evaluate whether concealing hundreds of victims was logistically feasible, and documented message volumes and narrative variants (linked to the AC/DC project with the University of Navarra).

## Findings

- An information vacuum around the inaccessible flooded structure fostered rumors of trapped bodies, with claimed victim counts escalating from 44 to 86, 258, over 800, and even 1,000.
- Official confirmation on November 5, 2024 that no bodies were found did not dissolve the narrative but triggered a second, conspiratorial phase alleging cover-ups, hidden child victims, and refrigerated-truck body removals.
- The conspiracy relied on identifiable persuasive tactics: appeals to false authority via anonymous testimony, fabricated documents, personal-experience storytelling, and emotional appeals (anger, fear).
- Recurring fallacies included post hoc ergo propter hoc, non sequitur, argument from ignorance, false authority, and slippery slope.
- Message volume reached roughly 13,000 in seven days—about four times the average—overwhelming claim-by-claim debunking.
- Counterarguments were reformulated as accessible questions (e.g., about missing-family reports or the feasibility of coordinated silence) to give the public reusable reasoning tools.
