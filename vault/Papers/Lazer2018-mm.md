# The science of fake news

> Lazer, D. M. J., Baum, M. A., Benkler, Y., Berinsky, A. J., Greenhill, K. M., Menczer, F., Metzger, M. J., Nyhan, B., Pennycook, G., Rothschild, D., Schudson, M., Sloman, S. A., Sunstein, C. R., Thorson, E. A., Watts, D. J., & Zittrain, J. L. (2018). The science of fake news. *Science*, *359*, 1094–1096. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao2998
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aao2998)

## Summary

This influential Policy Forum article by Lazer and colleagues argues that the surge of politically oriented fake news reflects the erosion of institutional safeguards—chiefly journalistic gatekeeping—that once constrained the spread of falsehoods. The authors define fake news not by the accuracy of individual stories but by the intent and organizational processes of its publishers: fabricated content that mimics the *form* of news media while lacking their editorial norms. Synthesizing social and computer science research, they document how the internet lowered barriers to entry, degraded journalism's business model, and combined with deepening political polarization to make audiences receptive to ideologically congenial lies. Crucially, they emphasize how little rigorous evidence exists about fake news's prevalence and effects, and they issue an agenda-setting call for interdisciplinary research and platform–academic collaboration to rebuild a trustworthy information ecosystem.

## Key Contributions

- Offers a process-and-intent-based definition of fake news, distinguishing it from the broader categories of misinformation and disinformation.
- Situates the problem historically within the rise and decline of journalistic norms of objectivity, and within trends of geographic, attitudinal, and affective polarization.
- Organizes interventions into two families—empowering individuals (fact checking, media literacy) and structural/platform changes (algorithms, regulation)—and critically weighs the evidence and risks of each.
- Articulates a normative agenda for a "science of fake news," including a call for platforms to share unique data with independent researchers.

## Methods

An interdisciplinary synthesis spanning communication, political science, psychology, and computer science. The authors review existing empirical literature (exposure estimates from the 2016 U.S. election, diffusion studies on Twitter, bot-prevalence estimates), advance a conceptual definition and a taxonomy of interventions, and assess platform practices and legal/regulatory frameworks such as the 1996 Communications Decency Act and the European "right to be forgotten."

## Findings

- The average American encountered roughly one to three known fake-news stories in the month before the 2016 election—likely a conservative estimate.
- False information on Twitter spreads faster and reaches more people than true information, especially on political topics.
- Estimates put bots at 9–15% of active Twitter accounts, with Facebook estimating up to 60 million; bots amplified political content in the 2016 U.S. and 2017 French campaigns.
- General trust in mass media hit historic lows in 2016, with a stark partisan gap (51% of Democrats vs. 14% of Republicans).
- Support for fact checking is mixed at best; cognitive tendencies (selective exposure, confirmation and desirability bias, fluency/familiarity effects) can render corrections ineffective or even counterproductive.
- No comprehensive data-collection system exists to track the evolving fake-news ecosystem.

## Connections

This paper is a foundational agenda-setting statement whose empirical building blocks are cited throughout the field: the Twitter diffusion result draws on [[Vosoughi2018-at]], exposure and consumption estimates connect to [[Allcott2017-yz]], [[Grinberg2019-ua]], and [[Guess2019-ym]], and its account of ideological homophily and echo chambers echoes [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]]. Its skepticism about correction and its cognitive framing anticipate later intervention research such as [[Pennycook2021-jq]] and prior work on continued-influence effects like [[Lewandowsky2012-vn]], while its institutional and hybrid-media framing resonates with [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Chadwick2013-ns]]. The paper's core call for a "science of fake news" and platform–academic collaboration is directly taken up by later meta-scientific efforts including [[Budak2024-ef]] and [[Bak-Coleman2025-pm]].
