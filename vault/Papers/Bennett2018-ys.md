# The disinformation order: Disruptive communication and the decline of democratic institutions

> Bennett, W. L., & Livingston, S. (2018). The disinformation order: Disruptive communication and the decline of democratic institutions. *European Journal of Communication*, *33*, 122–139. https://doi.org/10.1177/0267323118760317
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/0267323118760317)

## Summary

Bennett and Livingston reframe the contemporary problem of strategic disinformation as a systemic condition rather than a scatter of isolated "fake news" incidents. They coin the term **disinformation order** to describe an emerging pattern in which intentional falsehoods circulate through social media and journalism-mimicking websites, corroding the credibility of centre parties, elections and the mainstream press. Their central argument is that this order is rooted less in new media technology than in a deeper legitimacy crisis — declining citizen trust in democratic institutions — which creates fertile ground for radical-right movements and foreign (especially Russian) influence operations. The essay is a call to political communication scholarship to abandon the limiting framework of "populism" and to revise core concepts whose assumptions about coherent, functional democratic public spheres no longer hold.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **"disinformation order"** as a conceptual frame shifting attention from isolated falsehoods to systemic institutional and communicative breakdown.
- Proposes the **disinformation-amplification-reverberation (DAR) cycle** as an analytic tool for tracing how disinformation moves between alt-right and mainstream media.
- Clarifies terminology by distinguishing disinformation from misinformation and "fake news," and separating alt-right disinformation outlets (e.g. Breitbart) from partisan conservative journalism.
- Critiques the dominant "populism" literature for reducing the problem to anti-elite anger while missing agendas of racism, nationalism and anti-globalism.
- Sets out a research agenda linking disinformation to institutional trust decline, foreign information warfare, and normative democratic assessment.

## Methods

A conceptual and theoretical essay rather than an empirical study. The authors develop their framework through case-study illustration and narrative analysis (the Ami Horowitz/Fox News Sweden episode, the "birther" movement, Germany's "Lisa" story), synthesise secondary evidence (Eurobarometer/OECD trust data, European Social Survey, Facebook's internal Information Operations report), and adapt Facebook's operational distinction between intentional disinformation and inadvertent misinformation. They draw comparatively across multiple national contexts (US, UK, Germany, Sweden, Netherlands, Hungary, Poland and others).

## Findings

- Average EU trust in national parliaments and governments has fallen to roughly 30%, down about a dozen points since the 2008 financial crisis.
- A legacy-media "amplifier effect" spreads absurd stories that earlier gatekeeping would have suppressed — an effect sometimes strengthened even by fact-checking.
- Radical-right party vote share has grown disproportionately relative to the left and to the actual distribution of radical identifiers.
- Documented foreign operations include Russia's Internet Research Agency and Fancy Bear hacking, and China's paid commenters producing an estimated 448 million posts per year.
- During the 2016 US election, the top 20 fake news stories on Facebook drew more engagement (8.7M) than the top real news stories (7.3M).
- Disinformation is embedded in networks of funders, think tanks and parties (Mercer-backed Breitbart, Koch-backed Daily Caller), making it far from marginal.
- Institutional responses are emerging, such as Germany's Netzwerkdurchsetzungsgesetz and Finland's NATO-affiliated hybrid-threats centre.

## Connections

This is a foundational agenda-setting piece for the information-disorder literature and connects directly to the same authors' later work in [[Bennett2025-xs]]. Its emphasis on radical-right media ecosystems and the systemic, networked production of disinformation resonates with [[Benkler2018-lw]] on asymmetric media polarisation and with [[Starbird2019-qv]] on participatory disinformation, while its Facebook-engagement evidence and critique of the "fake news" frame anticipate empirical audits such as [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]] and [[Lazer2018-mm]]. The reframing of disinformation as rooted in institutional legitimacy crisis and affective partisanship also connects to [[Iyengar2019-jj]] and [[Chadwick2013-ns]]'s hybrid media system, on which the authors explicitly draw.
