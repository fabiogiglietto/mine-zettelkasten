# Computational research in the post-API age

> Freelon, D. (2018). Computational research in the post-API age. *Polit. Commun.*, *35*, 665–668. https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/56f4q
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.31235/osf.io/56f4q)

## Summary

Freelon's brief essay names and diagnoses a defining condition of computational communication research: the "post-API age." Prompted by Facebook's April 2018 closure of its Pages API — which eliminated all terms-of-service-compliant methods for extracting Facebook content — the piece argues that heavy investment in platform-specific API methods is dangerously fragile, since companies can restrict or eliminate access overnight without recourse. Drawing on the author's own experience of watching a Facebook data-collection workshop and Python module become instantly obsolete, the essay offers guidance for graduate computational methods education: teach web scraping as a more durable skill, and cultivate serious understanding of the legal and ethical stakes of violating platform terms of service.

## Key Contributions

- Names and diagnoses the "post-API age" as a structural challenge for computational communication research.
- Provides practical curricular guidance, advocating web-scraping instruction alongside API methods in graduate training.
- Articulates ethical/legal recommendations: use authorized methods where possible, distinguish TOS compliance from research ethics, and understand the risks of TOS violation.
- Calls on scholars to foreground data-access fragility in future publications and conference submissions.

## Methods

A reflective, argumentative essay rather than an empirical study. Freelon draws on his own experience with now-obsolete Facebook tooling and reviews the landscape of web-scraping approaches — browser extensions (Web Scraper, Grepsr), standalone software (Teleport Pro), cloud solutions (Import.io, Webhose.io), and code-based frameworks (BeautifulSoup, Selenium). Illustrative cases (e.g., collecting Congressional campaign messages via the Twitter API versus scraping candidate websites) ground the argument.

## Findings

- Code-based scraping tools (BeautifulSoup, Selenium) offer maximum flexibility at minimum cost but require understanding each page's unique DOM structure, making them harder to learn and inherently site-specific.
- Major platforms (Facebook, Google) prohibit automated scraping in their TOS and attempt to detect and block it, though carefully crafted software can sometimes circumvent restrictions.
- Consequences of TOS violation range from blacklisted API credentials and IP bans to, in extreme cases, criminal prosecution under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (as in the Aaron Swartz case).
- The then-pending *Sandvig v. Sessions* lawsuit challenges criminal prosecution of TOS violations, but its unresolved status leaves researchers legally uncertain.
- TOS compliance is distinct from human-subjects compliance and privacy protection — respecting a company's terms does not equate to protecting users.
