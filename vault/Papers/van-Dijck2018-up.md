# The platform society

> van Dijck, J., Poell, T., & de Waal, M. (2018). The platform society. https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190889760.001.0001
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780190889760.001.0001)

## Summary

*The Platform Society* argues that online platforms have not merely disrupted Western societies but have become embedded in their very core — reshaping economic transactions, social practices, and public institutions. Van Dijck, Poell, and de Waal reject celebratory "platform revolution" and "sharing economy" framings, instead conceptualizing a **platform society** in which public values are perpetually contested between private corporate interests and the collective good. Drawing on media studies, political economy, and science and technology studies, they contend that platforms are neither neutral nor value-free: they carry specific (largely US-based) norms inscribed in their architectures that frequently clash with the public values embedded in European and North American societal structures. The book issues an overdue call for governments and civil society to anchor public values — fairness, solidarity, accountability, transparency, democratic control — in platform governance.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces and theorizes the concept of the **platform society** to capture the inextricable entanglement of platforms and societal structures.
- Offers a functional taxonomy of platform roles — **infrastructural vs. sectoral**, **connectors vs. complementors** — usable for updating antitrust and regulatory frameworks.
- Provides an analytical "instrumentarium" built on three mechanisms: **datafication, commodification, and selection**.
- Reframes platforms as **performative constructors of value regimes** rather than neutral enablers, bridging economic, technological, and public-value perspectives.
- Advances a normative governance agenda centered on public values, with particular attention to the European context.

## Methods

The book is a multidisciplinary theoretical synthesis drawing on STS, political economy, business/management studies, media studies, and legal studies. It deploys a three-level analytical framework — micro (single-platform anatomy), meso (the platform ecosystem), and macro (geopolitics of platform societies) — and deconstructs platform "anatomy" into technical, economic, and sociolegal elements (data, algorithms, interfaces, ownership, business models, terms of service). These tools are applied through sector-based comparative case studies across four domains: news, urban transport, healthcare/health research, and education (e.g., Airbnb, Uber, Coursera, AltSchool, 23andMe, PatientsLikeMe).

## Findings

- Platforms deliberately blur the distinctions between infrastructure and sectors, market and state, consumer and citizen, and public and private — adopting a hybrid status to bypass regulation and professional norms (Airbnb refusing "hotel" status; Uber rejecting the "taxi business"; Facebook long refusing to be a "media organization").
- The Amsterdam Airbnb case shows how platform adoption triggers clashes over fairness, level playing fields, safety enforcement, taxation, affordable housing, and inequality.
- The "Big Five" (Alphabet, Apple, Facebook, Amazon, Microsoft) form a privatized, opaque, centralized infrastructural core, counterbalanced globally only by a state-linked Chinese ecosystem.
- Infrastructural and sectoral platforms are increasingly integrated (Google's stake in Uber; Amazon buying Whole Foods), letting the Big Five accumulate cross-sectoral power via sector-agnostic data.
- The ecosystem is riddled with paradoxes: seemingly egalitarian but hierarchical; seemingly public-serving but corporate; seemingly neutral but ideologically loaded; seemingly local but globally consequential.
- Because Western online infrastructure is now fully privatized, governments, NGOs, and public institutions depend on Google or Facebook for login, search visibility, and reach.

## Connections

This book provides foundational conceptual grounding for platform-data-governance research, complementing infrastructural and gatekeeping analyses such as [[Gillespie2010-sla2]], [[Gillespie2022-jx]], and [[Katzenbach2026-sl2e]]. Its emphasis on the political economy and structural power of dominant platforms resonates with critical accounts like [[Benkler2018-lw]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]]. The performativity and public-values framing is broadly relevant to work on platform governance and regulation but is not directly engaged by the more empirical information-disorder studies in this corpus.
