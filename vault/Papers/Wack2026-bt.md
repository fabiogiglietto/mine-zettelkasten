# Making sense of AI-generated disinformation: How audience interpretations influence the impact of deepfakes in Kenya

> Wack, M., & Prochaska, S. (2026). Making sense of AI-generated disinformation: How audience interpretations influence the impact of deepfakes in Kenya. *Social Media + Society*, *12*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261462092
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261462092)

## Summary

This paper challenges the assumption that a deepfake's persuasive power is fixed at production, arguing instead that its impact is socially negotiated through audience interpretation. Using a large preregistered survey experiment (N=7,008) in Kenya, the authors show that political deepfakes of presidential candidates confessing to fabricated corruption schemes had different effects depending on the social commentary accompanying them. Credulous comments deepened the deepfake's damage to candidate support, while skeptical comments partially restored it, with partisanship strongly moderating both. The authors frame these dynamics through "collective sensemaking" and participatory disinformation theory, contending that deepfakes function as flexibly deceptive cues embedded in social contexts rather than as isolated individual credibility problems — a framing especially consequential in low-resource, weakly-moderated media ecosystems that describe most global social media users.

## Key Contributions

- Provides experimental evidence that audience commentary and social context—not just individual cognition—shape the persuasive impact of deepfakes.
- Introduces a novel in-survey comment instrument designed to simulate platform-native social commentary and improve ecological validity of deepfake experiments.
- Centers an understudied, low-resource, non-Western (Kenyan) electoral context, addressing gaps in cross-cultural deepfake and misinformation research.
- Bridges individual-focused deepfake psychology with meso-level collective sensemaking and participatory disinformation frameworks.
- Offers practical implications for countermeasures and moderation where AI labeling is inconsistent.

## Methods

- Preregistered (OSF #182038) between-subjects online survey experiment with 7,008 validated Kenyan participants recruited via Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook, stratified by region, age, and gender.
- Conditions: video (N=2,820), audio (N=2,733), or control (N=1,455), showing a fabricated corruption "confession" by either William Ruto or Raila Odinga to test aligned vs. misaligned partisanship.
- Deepfakes built with accessible open-source tools—DeepFaceLab and Wav2Lip for video, ElevenLabs for voice—refined with local feedback; no watermarks or AI labels.
- An embedded comment instrument delivered three consensus conditions: credulous ("not surprising"), incredulous ("clearly fake"), and mixed.
- Outcomes included a 0–100 warmth/support thermometer, perceived media legitimacy, institutional trust, and corruption perceptions; robust regression with respondent-clustered standard errors and standardized coefficients.
- Ethical safeguards: IRB exemption review, extensive debriefing, comprehension checks (95% correct identification of inauthenticity), and local consultation.

## Findings

- Support for the depicted candidate dropped most sharply under the credulous "not surprising" comment (β=−4.90, p<.001).
- Simply viewing the video without comments modestly reduced support (β=−2.25, p=.04); the incredulous "clearly fake" comment had no reliable effect (β=−0.64, p=.56).
- The mixed-comment condition produced an intermediate drop (β=−2.85, p<.05) indistinguishable from the no-comment control, implying uncertainty itself reduced support.
- Among co-partisans, viewing the video reduced support regardless of comment type, with little additional movement from comment cues.
- Among cross-partisans, the incredulous comment *increased* support (β=4.77, p=.006 relative to no-comment), suggesting counter-narratives can blunt or reverse deepfake harm.
- Perceived accuracy moderated effects: seeing the video as real lowered support, while seeing it as fake raised it above baseline.
