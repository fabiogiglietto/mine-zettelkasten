# Making sense of AI-generated disinformation: How audience interpretations influence the impact of deepfakes in Kenya

> Wack, M., & Prochaska, S. (2026). Making sense of AI-generated disinformation: How audience interpretations influence the impact of deepfakes in Kenya. *Social Media + Society*, *12*. https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261462092
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1177/20563051261462092)

## Summary

This paper challenges the assumption that a political deepfake's persuasive power is fixed at the moment of production, arguing instead that it is socially negotiated through audience interpretation. Using a large preregistered survey experiment in Kenya (N=7,008), Wack and Prochaska show that the social commentary surrounding a deepfake meaningfully shapes its impact on candidate support. Participants viewed AI-generated audio or video of William Ruto or Raila Odinga confessing to a fabricated corruption scheme, accompanied by comments that were credulous, incredulous, or mixed. The authors frame their results through "collective sensemaking" and participatory disinformation theory, contending that deepfakes function as flexibly deceptive cues within social ecosystems rather than as isolated individual credibility problems — a dynamic especially consequential in low-resource, non-Western contexts with limited moderation and inconsistent AI labeling.

## Key Contributions

- Experimental evidence that audience commentary and social context, not just individual cognition, shape a deepfake's persuasive impact.
- A novel in-survey comment instrument that simulates platform-native social commentary to improve ecological validity of deepfake experiments.
- Centers an understudied, low-resource, non-Western electoral context (Kenya), addressing gaps in cross-cultural misinformation research.
- Bridges individual-level psychology of deepfake interpretation with meso-level collective sensemaking and participatory disinformation frameworks.
- Practical implications for countermeasures and moderation in resource-constrained environments.

## Methods

Preregistered (OSF #182038) between-subjects online survey experiment with 7,008 validated Kenyan participants recruited via Instagram, WhatsApp, and Facebook, stratified by region, age, and gender. Participants were assigned to video (N=2,820), audio (N=2,733), or control (N=1,455) conditions, and randomly shown a fabricated corruption "confession" by either Ruto or Odinga to test aligned versus misaligned partisanship. Stimuli were created with accessible open-source tools (DeepFaceLab and Wav2Lip for video; ElevenLabs for voice) and refined with local feedback for realism, with no AI labels or watermarks. A comment instrument embedded three consensus conditions — credulous, incredulous, and mixed — into the survey flow. Outcomes included a 0–100 support thermometer, perceived media legitimacy/accuracy, institutional trust, and corruption perceptions, analyzed via robust regression with respondent-clustered standard errors.

## Findings

- Support for the depicted candidate dropped most under the credulous "not surprising" comment (≈5 points below baseline; β=−4.90, p<.001).
- Simply viewing the video without comments modestly reduced support (β=−2.25, p=.04), while the incredulous "clearly fake" comment had no reliable effect relative to baseline (β=−0.64, p=.56).
- The mixed-comment condition produced an intermediate drop (β=−2.85, p<.05) indistinguishable from the no-comment control, implying uncertainty itself drove reduced support.
- Among co-partisans, viewing the video reduced support regardless of comment type, with little additional movement from comment cues.
- Among cross-partisans, the incredulous comment *increased* support (β=4.77, p=.006), suggesting counter-narratives can blunt or reverse deepfake harm.
- Perceiving the video as real lowered support; perceiving it as fake raised support above baseline (though not at p<.05).

## Connections

This work extends deepfake persuasion research toward the social and interpretive dimensions of synthetic media, complementing experimental studies of AI-generated persuasion such as [[Hackenburg2025-dj]] and [[Hackenburg2026-ud]], and audience-perception work on synthetic content like [[Hameleers2026-mc]]. Its focus on collective sensemaking and participatory disinformation in a non-Western, low-resource electoral context resonates with the broader meta-scientific concern about generalizability and representativeness in misinformation research raised by [[Budak2024-ef]].
