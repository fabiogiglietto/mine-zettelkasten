# The performance of borderline content on Facebook

> Rogers, R., & Koronska, K. (2026). The performance of borderline content on Facebook. *Content Moderation across Social Media Platforms*, 97–123. https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003693192-4
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.4324/9781003693192-4)

## Summary

This chapter traces how "borderline" content — material that nears but does not cross Facebook's community standards — has performed on the platform across the 2016, 2020, and 2024 U.S. presidential elections. Building on Craig Silverman's original 2016 "fake news" data journalism and the authors' own 2020 replication, it interleaves two parallel genealogies: the evolution of Meta's content-moderation terminology (from "fake news" to "false news" to "misinformation" to "borderline") and the successive restrictions on data access that constrain independent research. The central argument is that these redefinitions progressively shrank and depoliticized the problem, allowing most problematic content to remain online by default. Empirically, the authors find that while problematic content first worsened and then declined relative to the mainstream by 2024, the borderline content that still performs well is almost exclusively right-wing — directly challenging Meta's "content-agnostic" moderation stance.

## Key Contributions

- A longitudinal, methodologically consistent comparison of problematic-content performance across three U.S. election cycles (2016, 2020, 2024) using an off-platform data source (BuzzSumo).
- A critical genealogy of Meta's shifting moderation vocabulary, showing how redefinition from "fake news" to "borderline" functions to depoliticize and retain content.
- Documentation and critique of the progressive winnowing of Facebook research data access and the rise of vetted academic–industry partnerships ("independence by permission").
- A post-API, "off-platform" methodology for studying moderation effects once official pipelines (e.g., CrowdTangle) are closed.
- An analysis of the "metric politics" of *reach* versus *engagement*, showing how metric choice determines whether right-wing content appears as top-performing.

## Methods

The study combines comparative historical and documentary analysis with empirical replication. The authors trace Facebook/Meta's data-access regimes (Open Graph API v1 and v2, Graph Search, Social Science One, CrowdTangle, the Meta Content Library) and perform documentary analysis of policy texts, white papers, and transparency reports to reconstruct evolving definitions of problematic content. Empirically, they replicate Silverman's 2016 method via the BuzzSumo content research tool, querying election-related keywords (drawn from party platforms and *New York Times* headlines on Biden, Harris, and Trump) and ranking URLs by total Facebook engagement for the 2024 campaign. Domains were labeled using Media Bias/Fact Check ratings and grouped into custom categories ("borderline/non-borderline," "biased/least-biased"), with supplementary analysis of top YouTube-originated content and checks for "pink slime" imposter sites.

## Findings

- Across 2016–2024, the problematic-content problem first worsened and then declined, with mainstream domains consistently outperforming borderline domains for all three 2024 candidates.
- In 2024, borderline content made up only a small fraction of engagement (~330,723 of 47.2 million total engagements with election-related URLs).
- Borderline engagement came in short bursts driven almost entirely by extreme-right sources (e.g., infowars.com, zerohedge.com); mainstream engagement was steadier and more politically balanced.
- Biased content received significantly less engagement than less-biased content, with the gap widening in the final six months — suggesting intensified moderation effects.
- YouTube-originated content grew in significance; ~20 of the top 100 engaged videos came from "borderline" channels, all pro-Trump, drawing ~200,000 engagements versus ~900,000 for mainstream outlets.
- A review of 12,000+ unlabeled URLs found no imposter or "pink slime" sources, consistent with the 2020 finding that such sites had lost traction.
- Confirming prior work, misinformation shows an empirical rightward lean — which the authors argue motivates Meta's depoliticizing, content-agnostic stance.

## Connections

This chapter directly extends the authors' own prior definitional-classification work [[Rogers2025-sl8f]] and the companion election study [[Rogers2026-cy]]. Its account of the "APIcalypse" and the politics of vetted platform data access speaks closely to work on the Meta Content Library and post-API research strategies [[Rieder2025-ju]], [[Rieder2026-pp]], [[Bruns2026-yv]], while the broader claim that misinformation has an empirical rightward lean connects to the asymmetry literature represented by [[Freelon2020-yp]] and [[Benkler2018-lw]]. Its scrutiny of engagement metrics and viral reach also resonates with critical analyses of what circulates widely on Facebook [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]] and with foundational treatments of content moderation [[Gillespie2022-jx]], [[Gillespie2010-sla2]].
