# Exposure to untrustworthy websites in the 2016 US election

> Guess, A. M., Nyhan, B., & Reifler, J. (2020). Exposure to untrustworthy websites in the 2016 US election. *Nature Human Behaviour*, *4*, 472–480. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0833-x
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0833-x)

## Summary

This paper offers one of the first rigorous, behavioral individual-level accounts of "fake news" exposure during the 2016 U.S. presidential campaign, combining survey responses with passively logged web-traffic data from a national YouGov Pulse panel. Its central argument is deflationary: while consumption of untrustworthy websites clearly followed a selective-exposure pattern—Trump supporters and those with strongly conservative information diets consumed far more pro-attitudinal false content—such sites made up only about 6% of the average American's news diet, and exposure was concentrated in a small, deeply committed subgroup. The authors argue that popular alarm about the prevalence and electoral impact of fake news has been substantially overstated, though they are careful to note that turnout and vote-choice effects cannot be precisely estimated.

## Key Contributions

- First comprehensive individual-level behavioral analysis linking real-world web traffic, candidate preference, and voter-file-validated turnout for untrustworthy-website consumption in 2016.
- Quantifies the actual prevalence and heavy concentration of fake news exposure, correcting overstated claims based on self-reports or aggregate data.
- Provides independent behavioral evidence of Facebook's role as a distribution vector for untrustworthy content.
- Documents a "fact-checking mismatch": corrective content almost never reaches those exposed to the falsehoods it targets.
- Tests and rejects (within precision limits) the claim that fake news displaced hard news or altered the election outcome.

## Methods

The authors merged an online survey (n=3,251; core analysis on 2,525 with desktop/laptop traffic and 2,170 candidate supporters) with time-stamped, URL-level web-traffic data from YouGov's Pulse panel over October 7–November 14, 2016. Untrustworthy sites were identified using the Grinberg et al. domain classification (black/orange/red), with robustness checks using Allcott & Gentzkow domains and fact-checked-false articles. They measured both binary exposure and share of news diet (denominator = hard-news plus untrustworthy sites), constructed an information-diet slant measure via Facebook-sharing-based scores divided into deciles, and estimated Facebook's referral role using usage terciles and the last three URLs visited within 30 seconds before an untrustworthy visit. Analysis used weighted OLS (with probit checks), a difference-in-differences crowd-out comparison to 2015 traffic, TargetSmart turnout validation, Cognitive Reflection Test scores, and equivalence tests for null effects.

## Findings

- 44.3% of Americans visited an untrustworthy website; such sites were 5.9% of the hard-news diet (4.6% conservative, 0.6% liberal).
- 56.7% of Trump supporters visited an untrustworthy conservative site (11.0% of their diet) versus 27.7% of Clinton supporters (0.8% of their diet).
- Exposure was "deep but narrow": in the most conservative decile, 84.0% visited such sites, comprising ~20.9% of their diet (~91 articles); 62% of all untrustworthy traffic came from the top two conservative deciles.
- Users spent more time per untrustworthy article (64.2s) than on hard news (42.1s); high-CRT Trump supporters consumed less, suggesting genuine engagement among consumers.
- Facebook appeared among the three prior URLs in 15.1% of untrustworthy visits versus 5.9% for hard news—unlike Google or Twitter—and heavy Facebook users had far higher exposure.
- Only 18.6% of those exposed also saw any fact-check, and only 3 of 111 who read a matchable false article read its corresponding fact-check.
- Hard news and untrustworthy consumption were positively correlated (no crowd-out); associations with Trump support and turnout were too imprecise to confirm, ruling out only large effects (~10 and ~9 points respectively).

## Connections

This paper is a cornerstone of the behavioral fake-news exposure literature and shares data, methods, and authorship lineage with related audience-tracking studies such as [[Grinberg2019-ua]] (whose domain classification it adopts), [[Allcott2017-yz]] (whose domain list it uses for robustness), and [[Guess2019-ym]]. Its findings on Facebook as a distribution vector, selective exposure, and the limits of fact-checking connect it to broader work on misinformation and platforms including [[Lazer2018-mm]], [[Vosoughi2018-at]], [[Pennycook2021-jq]], [[Guess2023-ai]], and [[Eady2023-xg]].
