# Networked intimacy. Intimacy and friendship among Italian Facebook users

> Farci, M., Rossi, L., Artieri, G. B., & Giglietto, F. (2017). Networked intimacy. Intimacy and friendship among Italian Facebook users. *Information, Communication & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1203970
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2016.1203970)

## Summary

This paper develops the concept of **networked intimacy** to describe how Italian Facebook users cultivate intimate relationships not through classical private self-disclosure, but through public, collaborative practices that depend on the active cooperation of one's friendship network. Drawing on 120 semi-structured interviews, the authors argue that intimacy on Facebook is negotiated in semi-public symbolic spaces where meanings are encoded for select audiences while remaining visible to a wider network. Against narcissistic readings of social media (Turkle, Lasch, Bauman), they reframe such practices as community-oriented resources for maintaining friendship, reconfiguring rather than collapsing the public/private boundary.

## Key Contributions

- Introduces **networked intimacy** as a theoretical framework synthesizing prior scholarship on Facebook self-disclosure.
- Identifies and operationalizes **five collaborative disclosure practices**, extending earlier typologies by adding *Tagging* and *Liking*.
- Provides empirical grounding in the **Italian context**, broadening SNS intimacy research beyond Anglo-American samples.
- Recasts public intimacy as **relational and community-oriented** rather than narcissistic.
- Bridges classical sociology of intimacy and friendship (Giddens, Jamieson, Pahl) with platform and affordance studies.

## Methods

Qualitative study using 118 semi-structured interviews with Italian Facebook users aged 13–54, part of a national PRIN-funded project (2013–2015). Participants were recruited via guided snowball sampling stratified by age and gender to match Italian Facebook demographics. Interviews averaged 45 minutes, took place in participants' homes with the interviewee browsing their profile, and were audio-recorded and transcribed. Analysis followed Strauss and Corbin's Grounded Theory with three stages of coding (open, axial, selective) in Dedoose, condensing ~400 initial codes into higher-order categories.

## Findings

- Users' central concern is **managing visibility** and avoiding unintended disclosure while still communicating intimately.
- **Showing rather than telling**: preferring images with layered meaning legible only to intended audiences.
- **Sharing implicit content**: quotes, song lyrics, and cryptic posts encoding messages for specific friends — related to but distinct from social steganography.
- **Tagging**: publicly marking exclusive relationships and directing content to individuals while keeping the post visible to the network.
- **Expectation of mutual understanding**: close friends are assumed to decode targeted content without explicit cues — a tacit relational competence.
- **Liking**: a low-cost gesture signalling recognition, proximity, and "thinking of you."
- These practices produce **ephemeral, semi-public symbolic spaces** in which intimate audiences are continually reconstituted.
- Participants did not spontaneously connect "intimacy" with Facebook, but described intimate practices readily once prompted.

## Connections

This work speaks directly to research on context, audience, and self-presentation in networked publics — most notably [[Marwick2011-sl02]] on imagined audiences and context collapse, which underpins the authors' account of encoding messages for selective decoding within a broader visible network. The framing of relational practice and community formation on platforms also resonates with [[Marwick2026-ss]] and [[Wedel2026-ix]] on online sociality, while offering a counterpoint focused on friendship maintenance rather than platform-specific community dynamics.
