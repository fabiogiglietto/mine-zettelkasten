# Diverging patterns of interaction around news on social media: insularity and partisanship during the 2018 Italian election campaign

> Giglietto, F., Valeriani, A., Righetti, N., & Marino, G. (2019). Diverging patterns of interaction around news on social media: insularity and partisanship during the 2018 Italian election campaign. *Information, Communication & Society*. https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2019.1629692
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/1369118X.2019.1629692)

## Summary

This paper investigates how partisan communities engaged with political news on social media during the 2018 Italian general election, a landmark contest that brought populist parties (Five Star Movement / M5S and the League) to power. Combining Twitter-based measures of news source partisanship with Facebook engagement data on 84,815 stories, the authors argue that sources favored by populist supporters are more *insular* (concentrated in a single partisan audience), that insularity correlates with a shift from commenting toward sharing, and that M5S uniquely leverages a strategic amplification/contestation pattern — sharing positive coverage of itself while flooding negative coverage with comments. The findings complicate simple echo-chamber narratives and highlight how populism intertwines with digital infrastructure in a "polarized pluralist" media system.

## Key Contributions

- Adapts the Media Partisanship Attention Score (MPAS) from two-party US contexts into a **Multi-Party MPAS (MP-MPAS)** applicable to multiparty systems.
- Introduces a novel **insularity index** for news sources, combining maximum partisan attention with the Gini coefficient of attention distribution.
- Demonstrates a **cross-platform triangulation** design that uses Twitter for partisanship estimation and Facebook for engagement analysis.
- Provides empirical evidence from a **non-US polarized-pluralist context**, addressing the US-centrism of echo-chamber research.
- Reframes the echo-chamber thesis: insular sources drive amplification via sharing, while cross-partisan sources become arenas of comment-based contestation.
- Identifies a distinctive **"cyber party" attention-economy strategy** by M5S linking populism to digital mobilization capacity.

## Methods

The authors collected ~216,765 retweets of Italian party and leader accounts (January 2018) to estimate user partisanship, then tracked the top 5,000 partisan contributors via DMI-TCAT, harvesting ~4.4M tweets and ~130M resolved URLs (Feb–Mar 2018). From these they classified 634 news sources on the MP-MPAS and constructed an insularity index with four tiers. A parallel Facebook dataset of 84,815 political stories from 4,113 domains was built with Huginn (Sep 2017–Mar 2018), pulling engagement metrics every two hours for a week per story. The top decile (3,731 stories) received manual sentiment coding toward the four main parties (three coders, Krippendorff's α = 0.72–0.84). Analyses used Kruskal-Wallis, Dunn's post-hoc with Bonferroni correction, and Spearman correlations.

## Findings

- Median insularity: **M5S = 0.73**, League = 0.67, LeU = 0.63, PD = 0.63 (system mean 0.65); M5S sources significantly more insular than all others (p < .001).
- Of 634 adjudicated sources: 33.9% League, 27.9% M5S, 19.1% cross-partisan, 9.5% PD, 7.7% LeU; Forza Italia and FdI attracted almost no adjudicated sources.
- Insularity is **negatively correlated with the comments/shares ratio** (Spearman ρ = -.31, p < .001): insular sources are shared; cross-partisan sources are commented on.
- For **M5S**: positive stories → more shares per comment; negative stories → more comments per share (p < .001) — amplification of favorable, contestation of hostile coverage.
- For **PD, Forza Italia, and the League**: the inverse pattern — negative stories drove sharing, positive stories drove commenting.
- Populist supporters do not fully self-segregate: they actively intervene in adversarial spaces rather than only inhabiting echo chambers.

## Connections

This paper is part of a substantial line of work by the authors and their collaborators on Italian political communication, hybrid media ecologies, and cross-platform news engagement — most directly extending [[Iannelli2015-e0818c3e]] and [[Giglietto2020-9d8acdd7]] / [[Giglietto2020-6278a4aa]] on Italian elections and problematic information sharing, and prefiguring their later work on coordinated link sharing and partisan news flows ([[Giglietto2023-fa71a001]], [[Giglietto2024-cbeb3f70]], [[Marino2024-2fbc690f]], [[Ghezzi2023-8bebc91f]]). Its challenge to the echo-chamber thesis via cross-platform engagement data speaks to canonical work such as [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and to more recent audience-level exposure studies like [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]]. The focus on populist "cyber party" dynamics also connects to broader accounts of partisan media asymmetry in Italian and European contexts ([[Mosca2026-yh]], [[Rossi2023-847d5a9f]]).
