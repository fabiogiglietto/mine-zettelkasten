# "Sticking their heads out above the parapets": Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research

> Park, S., & Thomas, D. (2026). "Sticking their heads out above the parapets": Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research. *Strathprints: The University of Strathclyde institutional repository (University of Strathclyde)*.
>
> [View paper](https://strathprints.strath.ac.uk/view/author/1255500.html>;)

## Summary

This paper presents the first qualitative, empirically grounded study of how computer science researchers experience and navigate legal risks arising from overbroad computer-crime and intellectual-property laws such as the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the UK Computer Misuse Act (CMA), and the DMCA. Drawing on 44 interviews (36 researchers discussing 130 projects/incidents, plus 8 supporting professionals such as lawyers), the authors argue that these laws fail to distinguish malicious hacking from good-faith public-interest research, producing serious and often unavoidable legal risk. The central claim is that such risk imposes documentable costs — chilling effects on research, emotional and health tolls, and a security-consequential practice the authors name "stockpiling" — and that systematic evidence of these harms should ground law and policy reform. The framing positions researchers as public-service actors who "stick their heads above the parapets" by publishing.

## Key Contributions

- The first qualitative study of researchers' *lived experiences* of legal risk, spanning 130 projects and two participant groups (researchers and supporting professionals).
- Naming and documenting "stockpiling" — researchers withholding discovered vulnerabilities due to legal risk — as a widespread, security-consequential chilling effect that also creates coercion/bribery exposure.
- Documentation of a recent (post-2020) escalation of politically motivated legal threats against social computing research, exposing fragility where norms substitute for law.
- A synthesized set of actionable strategies and three unilaterally-enactable recommendations for researchers.
- Comparative US–UK jurisdictional analysis, notably the role of the CFAA's civil cause of action (absent from the UK CMA) and cross-jurisdictional exposure of UK researchers to US law.
- Empirical grounding for reform of computer-crime and IP statutes.

## Methods

Reflexive thematic analysis following Mason and Saldaña, coded in NVivo, structured around six high-level research questions (condensed from 10 top-level and 87 sub-questions). Semi-structured interviews (avg. 75 min, 54 hours total) were conducted November 2024–February 2026 with two groups: 36 researchers (academic, independent/industry, some with government experience) and 8 supporting professionals who had each helped at least 20 researchers. Recruitment used public documents, professional networks, snowball sampling, and community outreach. The authors documented participant/project attributes systematically and verified corroborating documents (threat emails, wording). The study is explicitly **non-quantitative** — no saturation was reached, and reported figures reflect frequency within the sample rather than representative statistics. Extensive confidentiality safeguards were used (IRB/UK approval, mostly deleted verbal consent, optional recording, end-to-end encrypted communication, de-linked and participant-approved quotes).

## Findings

- 23 participants described legal risk as ubiquitous, unavoidable, or very common in their fields.
- Projects clustered in the last decade: vulnerability research since ~2000, social computing surging since 2020, AI testing emerging since 2022; most pre-2000 work concerned cryptography export controls.
- Fear, worry, frustration, and stress dominated emotional impacts (90 expressions across interviews); five researchers described the experience as all-consuming. Health/personal impacts included weight loss, sleeplessness, job loss, blacklisting, and an overturned criminal conviction with incarceration.
- 30% of projects involved actual legal threats, but most did not escalate to prosecution or litigation, and many rested on legally tenuous grounds.
- Contract law (especially Terms of Service violations) was the most-referenced legal area, followed by the CFAA, DMCA, defamation, UK CMA, fraud, and data protection.
- US projects faced far more legal threats (40%) than UK projects (15%), largely due to the CFAA's civil cause of action; researchers retained their own lawyer in 30 of 130 projects (mostly pro bono).
- Researchers' *own* lawyers yielded overwhelmingly positive experiences, whereas institutional lawyers served the institution's interests; institutional support varied widely.
- Industry researchers generally felt well protected — except two who exposed flaws in their own employers' products and faced some of the worst consequences.
- Vulnerability research showed mature disclosure norms (though bug-bounty/VDP terms and NDAs pose problems), while social computing norms were immature and reliant on TOS violations for essential research.
- IRB review was generally seen as reasonable; conference ethics review was often viewed as immature. Vendor maturity and the "Streisand effect" moderated hostile reactions.
