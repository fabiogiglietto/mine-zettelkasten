# "Sticking their heads out above the parapets": Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research

> Park, S., & Thomas, D. R. (2026). "Sticking their heads out above the parapets": Lived Experiences of Legal Risks in Research.
>
> [View paper](https://scholar.google.com/scholar?q=%22Sticking%20their%20heads%20out%20above%20the%20parapets%22%3A%20Lived%20Experiences%20of%20Legal%20Risks%20in%20Research)

## Summary

This paper presents the first qualitative, empirically grounded study of how computer science researchers experience legal risk arising from overbroad statutes — chiefly the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), the UK Computer Misuse Act (CMA), and the DMCA. Drawing on interviews with 36 researchers (discussing 130 projects/incidents) and 8 supporting professionals such as lawyers, the authors document the practical, emotional, and health impacts of legal risk, identify the widespread and security-consequential practice they call "stockpiling," and provide empirical grounding for policy reform. The central argument is that anti-hacking and IP laws, by failing to distinguish malicious hacking from good-faith research, paradoxically harm the public interest through chilling effects on researchers who "stick their heads above the parapets" to publish public-interest work.

## Key Contributions

- The first qualitative study of researchers' *lived experiences* of legal risk, spanning 130 projects and two participant groups (researchers and supporting professionals).
- Naming and documenting "stockpiling" — researchers withholding discovered vulnerabilities due to legal risk — as a widespread, security-consequential chilling effect that creates coercion/bribery exposure.
- Documentation of a marked post-2020 escalation of politically motivated legal threats against social computing research, exposing the fragility of norms versus law.
- A synthesized set of actionable strategies and three broad recommendations researchers can enact unilaterally.
- Comparative analysis of US vs. UK jurisdictional variation, including the significance of the CFAA's civil cause of action (absent in the UK CMA) and cross-jurisdictional exposure of UK researchers to US law.

## Methods

A qualitative study using reflexive thematic analysis (coded in NVivo) following Mason's and Saldaña's methodologies. Semi-structured interviews (30–120 minutes, ~75 min average, 54 hours total) were conducted November 2024–February 2026 with 44 participants: 36 researchers (academic, independent/industry) discussing 130 projects, and 8 supporting professionals who had each helped 20+ researchers. Recruitment used public documents, professional networks, and snowball sampling. Extensive ethics and confidentiality mitigations were applied (verbal/deleted consent, opt-out recording, end-to-end encrypted communication, participant-approved de-linked quotes). The analysis is explicitly non-quantitative — no saturation was claimed and reported frequencies describe the sample rather than a representative population.

## Findings

- 23 participants described legal risk as ubiquitous, unavoidable, or very common in their field.
- Projects clustered in the last decade: vulnerability research since ~2000, social computing surging since 2020, AI testing since 2022, with pre-2000 work mostly on cryptography export controls.
- Fear, worry, frustration, and stress dominated (90 expressions across interviews); five researchers described the experience as all-consuming, with impacts including weight loss, sleeplessness, job loss, blacklisting, and one overturned criminal conviction with incarceration.
- 30% of projects involved actual legal threats, most of which did not escalate; attorneys judged many threats legally tenuous.
- Terms of Service / contract violations were the most-referenced legal area, followed by the CFAA, DMCA, defamation, and the UK CMA.
- US projects saw far more threats (40%) than UK projects (15%), reflecting the CFAA's civil cause of action; researchers retained their own lawyer in 30 of 130 projects, mostly pro bono.
- Vulnerability research showed mature disclosure norms (though bug-bounty/VDP terms and NDAs cause problems), while social computing showed immature norms and heavy reliance on TOS-violating methods.
- IRB review was generally seen as reasonable; conference ethics review was often viewed as immature and sometimes unreasonable. Company maturity correlated with less hostile reactions, and the "Streisand effect" deterred some public threats.

## Connections

This paper sits at the intersection of platform data-access constraints and research ethics, and its account of researchers relying on Terms-of-Service-violating methods for essential social computing research speaks directly to work on infrastructure and legal barriers to platform research such as [[Freelon2024-sc]] and Davies2026-... — of the topic list, it most genuinely connects to studies grappling with the erosion and legal contestation of research access, including [[Tonneau2025-bv]] and [[Rieder2025-ju]]. Its framing of platforms as gatekeepers whose policies shape what research is legally feasible also resonates with the platform-governance lineage of [[Gillespie2010-sla2]] and the norms-and-context discussions in [[Marwick2011-sl02]].
