# Multi-platform research in the light of technology affordances: networks of the far right in Germany

> Kakavand, A. E., Righetti, N., & Waldherr, A. (2026). Multi-platform research in the light of technology affordances: networks of the far right in Germany. *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*, 1–20. https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2697186
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.1080/19331681.2026.2697186)

## Summary

This paper offers a rare large-scale comparative study of how German far-right actors organize themselves across five distinct platforms — Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, YouTube, and Telegram. Rather than treating platforms as interchangeable channels, the authors argue that each platform's affordances (connectivity, replicability, scalability) are *relational sociotechnical outcomes* arising from the interaction between platform design and user practice, not fixed technical properties. Empirically, they show that far-right actors do not reproduce the same network everywhere; instead they adapt their practices to platform-specific affordances, yielding markedly different network structures even across text-based platforms with similar connectors. The central methodological argument follows: single-platform studies capture only a slice of a broader multi-platform ecosystem, so cross-platform generalization is unwarranted.

## Key Contributions

- One of the few simultaneous, large-scale multi-platform comparative analyses of far-right networks across five major platforms.
- Demonstrates how network analysis can operationalize and infer technology affordances, mapping measures (HITS, betweenness, reciprocity, modularity) onto connectivity, replicability, and scalability.
- Advances a theory of affordances as relational sociotechnical outcomes, showing empirically that near-equivalent features produce divergent networks depending on social use.
- Provides a transparent methodological template (seed lists, snowball procedure, platform-specific connectors, shared code/OSF materials) while candidly documenting data-access and comparability limitations.

## Methods

An exploratory, multi-platform case study of the German-speaking far right over six months of 2022. The authors started from a curated seed list of ~28–43 prominent far-right actors per platform (AfD politicians and parties, journalists, alternative media, activists), vetted by expert scholars, then applied snowball sampling across three iterations following platform-specific *visible connectors* as edges — retweets/mentions (Twitter), shared posts (Facebook), forwarded messages (Telegram), caption tags (Instagram), channel subscriptions (YouTube). The final iteration retained only nodes linking back to already-collected actors to close the networks. Data came from platform APIs (Twitter v2, CrowdTangle, Google API, Telegram API with Telethon); analysis used Python/NetworkX with Gephi visualization, applying size-robust measures (connected components, reciprocity, clustering, Louvain modularity) alongside node-level roles via HITS and betweenness centrality.

## Findings

- **Twitter**: by far the largest and loosest network (42k nodes, ~7.7M edges, single component), low clustering and very low reciprocity — an international, outward-oriented broadcast arena where authority scores exceed hub scores, suggesting scalability dominates. This contrasts with prior work suggesting far-right ties stay within national boundaries.
- **Telegram**: second largest (8.4k nodes), more clustered but low reciprocity; inward-oriented, dominated by fringe/conspiracy content with differentiated producers, amplifiers, and brokers.
- **YouTube**: communities organized by shared genres/topics (e.g., cooking linked to "Day X" prepping), forming loose, indirect connectivity acting as a possible gateway to radicalization.
- **Instagram**: highest reciprocity, clustering, and modularity — fragmented echo chambers where mainstream political/media accounts dominate and a tight AfD cluster sits at the periphery.
- **Facebook**: smallest network, strongly AfD-dominated with a hierarchical division of labor — federal accounts as content authorities, local accounts as amplifying hubs — indicating higher replicability and party-coordinated connectivity.
- Ties reflect dynamics of amplification, replication, and visibility rather than mere ideological alignment.
