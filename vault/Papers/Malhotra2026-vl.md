# Political unfriending on Facebook: Evidence from over 180 million users

> Malhotra, N., Settle, J., Wojcieszak, M., Bergeron-Boutin, O., Guo, F., Franco, A., Herdagdelen, A., Mason, W., Allcott, H., Barbera, P., Brown, T., Crespo-Tenorio, A., Freelon, D., Gentzkow, M., Gonzalez-Bailon, S., Guess, A., Iyengar, S., Kim, Y. Political unfriending on Facebook: Evidence from over 180 million users. *The Journal of Politics*. https://doi.org/10.1086/744929

## Summary

This paper provides the first large-scale behavioral account of "political unfriending" on Facebook during the 2020 U.S. election, combining aggregate on-platform data from roughly 184 million adult users with a linked panel survey of about 72,000 consenting users. The central argument is that while severing social ties is rare overall, users are disproportionately likely to cut politically *cross-cutting* friendships relative to ideologically *aligned* ones, and that this behavior is dynamic — spiking around acute events like Election Day, the January 6th attack, and Inauguration Day. By moving beyond static snapshots of network homophily and survey self-reports, the authors reframe political homophily as an ongoing *process* of tie dissolution, while also validating that survey measures of unfriending are grounded in real behavior rather than pure expression.

## Key Contributions

- First large-scale behavioral evidence (180M+ users) on the differential severing of aligned versus cross-cutting online friendships.
- Shifts the study of political homophily from static network composition toward the dynamic *process* of tie dissolution.
- Documents how acute political events shape unfriending via a granular daily time series across the election and its aftermath.
- Methodologically validates survey-based self-reports of unfriending against the same individuals' actual on-platform behavior.
- Characterizes heterogeneity in unfriending by strength of political identity and on-platform civic engagement.

## Methods

The analysis draws on two datasets from the U.S. 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study (FIES): aggregate behavioral data from ~183.9 million monthly active adult users (filtered to those with estimable ideology and at least one liberal and one conservative friend), and a six-wave linked panel survey of 71,930 users (August 2020–March 2021) with post-stratification weights. User ideology was estimated via an internal Facebook classifier (0=left to 1=right), with ideological strength bifurcated into strong (<0.2 or >0.8) and weak. Unfriending was normalized by friend-network size separately for aligned and cross-cutting ties, with risk ratios comparing the two rates. The authors benchmarked rates against New Year's Day network "cleaning," ran daily time-series and heterogeneity analyses (by ideology strength and civic engagement quintiles), fit logistic regressions with demographic controls, and validated survey self-reports against logged 90-day behavior. The design was preregistered, with documented deviations and robustness checks.

## Findings

- Unfriending is rare: users severed ~2%–4% of pre-study friends. Liberals unfriended 2.66% of conservative vs. 2.14% of liberal friends (RR 0.80); conservatives unfriended 2.80% of liberal vs. 2.09% of conservative friends (RR 1.34) — roughly 25%–50% more likely to cut cross-cutting ties.
- Nearly one-third of users engaged in some unfriending during the campaign, close to prior survey estimates.
- Large short-term spikes in cross-cutting unfriending followed Election Day, January 6th, and Inauguration Day; conservatives showed the sharpest increases (quadrupling unfriending of liberals on Election Day) and cut some aligned ties too.
- Moderates were relatively unaffected and balanced; strong ideologues were least likely to unfriend like-minded users and (for conservatives) most likely to cut cross-cutting ones.
- Higher civic engagement predicted more cross-cutting unfriending (liberals: 1.5% lowest vs. 3.2% highest quintile for unfriending conservatives), and engagement was a stronger predictor than ideological strength.
- Self-reports were largely accurate: 62% who reported unfriending had done so; 79% reporting none had not; 84% accuracy for reporting no cross-cutting unfriending, 54% for reporting it.
- Reported motivations included too much political posting (26.8%; 37.5% among cross-cutting unfrienders), political disagreement (28.9%; 44.2%), offensive content, abuse, and the events of January 6th (34.1%; 49.8%).
- The study is explicitly descriptive and cannot establish politics as the causal driver or characterize those who were unfriended.

## Connections

This paper is part of the FIES research program and sits alongside other large-scale Facebook election studies examining feeds, networks, and polarization, such as [[Guess2023-ai]], [[Guess2023-ur]], [[Nyhan2023-gb]], and [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]]. It extends foundational work on online ideological self-selection and cross-cutting exposure — notably [[Bakshy2015-rn]] and [[Barbera2015-je]] — by focusing on *tie dissolution* rather than network composition, and speaks directly to the affective-polarization literature on partisan animus represented by [[Iyengar2019-jj]] and [[Osmundsen2021-et]], as well as echo-chamber debates in [[Del-Vicario2016-uj]] and [[Flaxman2016-lm]].
