# Political unfriending on Facebook: Evidence from over 180 million users

## Summary

This paper provides the first large-scale behavioral account of "political unfriending" on Facebook during the 2020 U.S. election cycle. Combining aggregate on-platform data from roughly 184 million adult users with a linked six-wave panel survey of about 72,000 consenting users, the authors examine how people dissolve politically aligned versus cross-cutting friendships. The central finding is that while severing ties is rare overall, users are substantially more likely to cut cross-cutting ties than aligned ones, and this behavior spikes sharply around moments of acute political conflict. The study reframes the study of political homophily as a *dynamic* process of tie dissolution rather than a static feature of network composition, and it validates traditional survey self-reports against logged behavior.

## Key Contributions

- First large-scale behavioral evidence (180M+ users) on the differential severing of aligned versus cross-cutting online friendships.
- Shifts the study of political homophily from static network composition toward the dynamic process of tie dissolution.
- Documents, on a granular daily time series, how acute political events shape unfriending across the 2020 election and its aftermath.
- Methodologically validates survey self-reports of unfriending against actual on-platform behavior from the same individuals.
- Characterizes heterogeneity in unfriending by political identity strength and on-platform civic engagement.

## Methods

The analysis draws on two datasets from the U.S. 2020 Facebook and Instagram Election Study (FIES): aggregate on-platform behavioral data from ~183.9 million adult monthly active users (filtered to those with estimable ideology and at least one liberal and one conservative friend), and a linked panel survey of 71,930 consenting adults across six waves (August 2020–March 2021) with post-stratification weights. User ideology was estimated via an internal Facebook classifier (0=left to 1=right), with ideological strength split into strong and weak. Tie severing was measured as unfriending, normalized by friend-network size separately for aligned and cross-cutting ties, with risk ratios comparing the two. The authors benchmarked unfriending against New Year's Day network "cleaning," conducted daily time-series analysis, ran heterogeneity analyses by ideology strength and civic engagement quintiles, and validated survey self-reports against logged 90-day behavior. The work was preregistered with documented deviations and robustness checks (classifier vs. self-report; expanding severing to include blocking and unfollowing).

## Findings

- Unfriending is rare overall (~2%–4% of pre-study friends severed), but cross-cutting ties are cut 25%–50% more often than aligned ones. Liberals: risk ratio 0.80; conservatives: 1.34.
- Nearly one-third of users engaged in some unfriending during the campaign, close to prior survey estimates.
- Large short-term spikes in cross-cutting unfriending followed Election Day, January 6th, and Inauguration Day; conservatives showed the sharpest increases (quadrupling unfriending of liberals on Election Day) and also cut some aligned ties.
- Moderates were relatively unaffected and balanced; strong ideologues were least likely to unfriend like-minded users and (for conservatives) most likely to unfriend cross-cutting ones.
- Higher civic engagement predicted more cross-cutting unfriending and was a stronger predictor than ideological strength.
- Self-reports were largely accurate: 62% of self-reported unfrienders had actually unfriended; 79% of those reporting none had not; accuracy for cross-cutting reports ranged 54%–84%.
- Reported motivations included excessive political posting (26.8%; 37.5% among cross-cutting unfrienders), political disagreement (28.9%; 44.2%), offensive content, harassment, and the events of January 6th (34.1%; 49.8%).
- The study is descriptive and cannot establish politics as the causal driver, nor characterize those who were unfriended.
