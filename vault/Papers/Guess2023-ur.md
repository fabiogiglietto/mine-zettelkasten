# How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?

> Guess, A. M., Malhotra, N., Pan, J., Barberá, P., Allcott, H., Brown, T., Crespo-Tenorio, A., Dimmery, D., Freelon, D., Gentzkow, M., González-Bailón, S., Kennedy, E., Kim, Y. ## Summary

This paper reports one of the landmark 2020 Facebook/Instagram Election Studies, a Meta–academic collaboration testing whether machine-learning feed-ranking algorithms causally shape users' political attitudes and behavior. Consenting US users of Facebook (n = 23,391) and Instagram (n = 21,373) were randomly assigned for roughly three months (September–December 2020) to receive a reverse-chronological feed instead of the platforms' default algorithmic ranking. The central finding is a striking disconnect: while switching to chronological ordering dramatically altered the on-platform experience — reducing time spent, engagement, and reshaping the mix of content seen — it produced no detectable changes in downstream political outcomes such as affective or issue polarization, political knowledge, or offline participation. The authors argue this tempers popular "filter bubble" and folk theories that pin individual-level political harms directly on algorithmic ranking.

## Key Contributions

- Provides rare, large-scale experimental evidence isolating the causal role of proprietary feed-ranking algorithms rather than the whole social media "bundle."
- Documents a stark gap between large algorithm-induced changes in user experience and negligible changes in political outcomes, informing algorithmic-regulation debates.
- Establishes reverse-chronological ranking as a well-defined counterfactual matching actual policy proposals, offering a methodological template for evaluating feed systems.
- Contributes deidentified data and code via the Social Media Archive (SOMAR) for replication.

## Methods

Two preregistered randomized controlled experiments embedded within Facebook and Instagram, recruiting consenting US adults via in-feed survey invitations in August 2020. The treatment group received a reverse-chronological feed from 24 September to 23 December 2020 (affecting ~80% of non-ad material); the control group retained the default algorithmic feed. Data combined five survey waves with on-platform behavioral logs and passive off-platform web-tracking (fieldwork with NORC). The primary estimand was the population average treatment effect, weighted by predicted ideology, friend count, political pages followed, and days active, with unweighted SATEs also reported. Content was classified along political, ideological cross-cutting/like-minded, untrustworthy-source, incivility, and slur dimensions, with subgroup and heterogeneity analyses. Meta funded data collection but held no prepublication approval; academic lead authors held final control.

## Findings

- Chronological feeds sharply reduced time spent (Facebook daily time above baseline fell from +73% to +37%; Instagram +107% to +84%), with substitution toward TikTok, YouTube, Reddit, and Instagram.
- On-platform engagement dropped: Facebook likes fell from 6.7% to 3.1% of exposures, with comparable declines on Instagram.
- Content mix shifted: more political content and news, more untrustworthy-source content (Facebook 2.6%→4.4%), and less uncivil and slur content on Facebook.
- On Facebook, both like-minded (53.7%→48.1%) and cross-cutting (20.7%→18.7%) sources fell while moderate/mixed-audience sources rose (22.6%→30.9%); friend-sourced content dropped ~24 points.
- No significant effects on affective or issue polarization, election or news knowledge, self-reported participation, or turnout on either platform.
- On-platform political engagement declined (Facebook −0.117 SD; Instagram −0.090 SD).
- The only significant secondary effect: Facebook chronological-feed users clicked more on partisan political news (0.107 SD).

## Connections

This is a flagship study in the 2020 US 2020 Facebook/Instagram Election Studies collaboration and directly complements the parallel [[Allcott2025-jb]] work on the same research program. Its skeptical findings on algorithmic filter bubbles and cross-cutting exposure engage closely with observational and audit-based work on ideological segregation and platform curation such as [[Gonzalez-Bailon2024-rq]], [[Gonzalez-Bailon2023-uy]], and [[Guess2023-ai]], and it speaks to broader debates on echo chambers and depolarization interventions in [[Bail2018-fk]], [[Barbera2015-je]], and [[Guess2020-rr]].
