# The psychology of fake news

> Pennycook, G., & Rand, D. G. (2021). The psychology of fake news. *Trends Cogn. Sci.*, *25*, 388–402. https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ar96c
>
> [View paper](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/ar96c)

## Summary

This review synthesizes cognitive, social, and political psychology research to explain why people believe and share false news online. Against the popular narrative that partisanship and politically motivated reasoning are the chief culprits, Pennycook and Rand argue that poor truth discernment stems primarily from a failure to engage careful, reflective reasoning, from insufficient or inaccurate prior knowledge, and from reliance on heuristics such as familiarity. They further identify a substantial dissociation between what people believe and what they share — most misinformation sharing reflects inattention rather than deliberate deception or confusion. The paper closes by consolidating evidence for scalable interventions, especially accuracy nudges and crowdsourced veracity ratings.

## Key Contributions

- Reframes the psychology of fake news from a politically-motivated-reasoning account toward an **inattention-and-reasoning** account ("classical reasoning" over "motivated System 2 reasoning").
- Clarifies the conceptual distinction between **truth discernment** (signal-detection sensitivity, d′) and **overall belief** (bias), showing that factors changing overall belief need not affect discernment.
- Identifies the **belief–sharing dissociation** and adjudicates among confusion-, preference-, and inattention-based explanations, favoring inattention.
- Consolidates evidence on anti-misinformation interventions, highlighting scalable accuracy nudges and crowdsourced ratings while noting the limits of automated detection and professional fact-checking.
- Positions online misinformation as an ecologically valid testing ground that unites lab experiments, large-scale social media data, and field experiments.

## Methods

The paper is a narrative synthesis grounded in signal detection theory and dual-process (System 1/System 2) models. The authors pool and reanalyze data from 14 studies in which US participants rated the accuracy of true/false, Democrat- and Republican-consistent headlines, comparing high vs. low Cognitive Reflection Test scorers and politically concordant vs. discordant items. They draw on survey experiments contrasting accuracy-rating with sharing-intention conditions, a large-scale Twitter field experiment sending accuracy prompts to users who shared misinformation, and crowdsourcing studies comparing layperson trust/accuracy ratings against professional fact-checkers.

## Findings

- A headline's actual **veracity** typically has a much larger effect on belief than political concordance — "politics does not trump truth." People are somewhat *better* at discernment for concordant news even while believing it more overall.
- More reflective individuals (higher CRT scores) show better truth discernment regardless of concordance; the reasoning-based difference in discernment was roughly **twice** the partisanship-based difference in the pooled data.
- Experimentally increasing deliberation reduces belief in false (but not true) news without increasing polarization; overconfidence and reliance on emotion increase susceptibility.
- Familiarity (illusory truth), source/elite credibility cues, social feedback, and emotionally evocative content act as heuristics that inflate belief in false claims.
- Sharing intentions are largely insensitive to veracity; sharing of false headlines was 91% higher than accuracy assessments, and an accuracy prompt reduced false-news sharing by 51% — strong evidence for inattention.
- Layperson crowd ratings correlate highly with professional fact-checkers (r ≈ 0.90 for publisher trust; r ≈ 0.74–0.79 across studies), supporting crowdsourced veracity as a scalable complement to fact-checking.

## Connections

This paper is a foundational psychological account within the broader misinformation literature, sharing its interventionist orientation (accuracy nudges, prebunking) with inoculation-focused work such as [[van-der-Linden2026-jt]], and its focus on individual susceptibility with [[Lyons2026-ca]]. Its emphasis on reasoning and platform design over pure partisanship contrasts productively with structural and prevalence-focused critiques like [[Budak2024-ef]] and audience-exposure studies such as [[Grinberg2019-ua]], [[Guess2019-ym]], and [[Allen2020-nj]], while its dual-process framing engages directly with the motivated-reasoning tradition exemplified by [[Osmundsen2021-et]] and [[Kunda1990-cg]]. It also connects to foundational agenda-setting reviews such as [[Lazer2018-mm]] and to diffusion findings in [[Vosoughi2018-at]].
